> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Why can an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with a completely normal-appearing mucosa still uncover celiac disease, eosinophilic esophagitis, or precancerous gastric intestinal metaplasia—only if the endoscopist executes the exact multi-site biopsy map?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergent upper GI hemorrhage / food impaction / caustic ingestion vs outpatient diagnostic EGD

Patients with hematemesis, melena with hemodynamic instability, variceal bleeding, esophageal food bolus impaction with inability to manage secretions, or button-battery ingestion require urgent/emergent airway-protected endoscopy after resuscitation; stable outpatients with uninvestigated dyspepsia (>= 60 years or alarm features), refractory GERD, dysphagia, or unexplained iron-deficiency anemia undergo elective diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD).

## Optical mechanics and anatomical landmarks: From the cricopharyngeus to the second portion of the duodenum

Diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) inspects the mucosal lining of the upper gastrointestinal tract using a flexible forward-viewing high-definition video gastroscope (typically 8.8 to 9.9 millimeters in outer diameter, or 5.0 to 5.8 millimeters for ultrathin transnasal gastroscopes) equipped with CCD/CMOS optical chips, coaxial air or carbon dioxide insufflation, lens-washing water jets, suction, and an instrument channel for biopsy forceps. A complete diagnostic examination systematically evaluates the upper esophageal sphincter (cricopharyngeus at ~15 to 18 centimeters from the incisors), the tubular esophagus, the diaphragmatic hiatus and squamocolumnar junction (Z-line) / gastroesophageal junction (top of the gastric rugal folds at ~38 to 40 centimeters), the gastric cardia and fundus (mandatory J-maneuver retroflexion view), the greater and lesser curvatures of the gastric body, the incisura angularis, the antrum, the pyloric channel, the duodenal bulb (D1), and the descending second portion of the duodenum (D2) including the major duodenal papilla. [1, 4]

Modern white-light high-definition endoscopy is routinely paired with virtual chromoendoscopy—such as Narrow-Band Imaging (NBI, using 415 nm blue and 540 nm green optical filters absorbed by hemoglobin), Blue Laser Imaging (BLI), or Linked Color Imaging (LCI). By restricting illumination wavelengths to superficial mucosal capillaries (415 nm) and deeper submucosal vessels (540 nm), virtual chromoendoscopy highlights subtle architectural distortion, irregular microvascular loops, and intestinal metaplasia that blend invisibly into pink mucosa under standard white light—guiding targeted biopsies of early esophageal squamous dysplasia, Barrett's neoplasia, and early gastric cancer. [1, 4, 5]

## Pre-procedural preparation, medication washouts, and the GLP-1 agonist aspiration trap

Diagnostic accuracy and procedural safety depend heavily on pre-endoscopic preparation. Standard ASA fasting guidelines require at least 2 hours of fasting after clear liquids and 6 hours after a light meal (8 hours after fried or fatty food) to ensure an empty stomach and prevent pulmonary aspiration during propofol or conscious sedation. However, patients taking glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (such as semaglutide, tirzepatide, or liraglutide) or having diabetic gastroparesis or achalasia exhibit markedly delayed gastric emptying: observational endoscopy cohorts demonstrate retained solid gastric contents in 5 to 24 percent of patients on GLP-1 agonists despite standard overnight fasting, requiring individualized liquid-diet preparation, pre-procedural gastric point-of-care ultrasound, or rapid-sequence endotracheal intubation when solid residue is suspected. [4, 6]

Equally critical is the pre-procedural washout of acid-suppressive and antimicrobial medications when testing for Helicobacter pylori or peptic ulcer etiology. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), bismuth subsalicylate, and systemic antibiotics suppress H. pylori bacterial density and urease activity, shifting organisms from the antrum to the proximal corpus and causing up to 30 to 50 percent false-negative results on rapid urease testing (CLOtest) and routine antral histology. Unless the EGD is performed emergently for bleeding or specifically to assess mucosal healing of severe Los Angeles Grade C/D erosive esophagitis after an 8-week PPI course, PPIs and bismuth must be withheld for at least 2 weeks and antibiotics for at least 4 weeks prior to diagnostic biopsy. [2, 4]

## Why normal-looking mucosa still requires structured biopsy mapping: EoE, Barrett's, Sydney System, and Celiac protocols

A cardinal principle of diagnostic upper endoscopy is that macroscopic visual appearance frequently underestimates or completely misses microscopic mucosal disease. Four standardized biopsy protocols govern modern EGD quality:
- Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE): Up to 10 to 25 percent of patients with histologically active EoE (>= 15 eosinophils per high-power field) have a macroscopically normal esophagus (EREFS score 0) or only subtle edema/furrows. Because eosinophilic infiltration is patchy, ACG guidelines mandate at least 6 biopsies obtained from at least 2 distinct esophageal levels (typically 3 from the distal esophagus ~3 to 5 cm above the GEJ and 3 from the mid/proximal esophagus), placed in separate specimen jars.
- Barrett's Esophagus (Prague C&M and Seattle Protocol): Columnar metaplasia extending >= 1 cm proximal to the top of the gastric folds is graded by its Circumferential extent (C) and Maximum tongue extent (M). Because high-grade dysplasia and intramucosal adenocarcinoma are often flat and focal, the Seattle protocol requires targeted biopsies of any visible nodularity or mucosal irregularity (submitted in individual vials) followed by 4-quadrant biopsies every 2 cm in non-dysplastic Barrett's and every 1 cm in known dysplasia. [1, 7]

In the stomach and duodenum, two additional mapping standards prevent false-negative diagnoses:
- Updated Sydney System & OLGA/OLGIM Staging for Gastritis and H. pylori: To detect patchy H. pylori colonization and stage precancerous gastric atrophy and gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM), the Sydney protocol requires 5 biopsies: 2 from the antrum (greater and lesser curvature 2 to 3 cm from the pylorus), 1 from the incisura angularis (the site of earliest metaplastic transition), and 2 from the gastric corpus (lesser curvature and greater curvature). Separating antrum/incisura from corpus jars allows the pathologist to distinguish antral-predominant H. pylori gastritis from corpus-predominant autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis (AMAG).
- Celiac Disease (Marsh-Oberhuber Classification): Villous atrophy (Marsh 3) and intraepithelial lymphocytosis (> 25 IELs per 100 enterocytes, Marsh 1) are notoriously patchy and may be confined to the duodenal bulb ('ultrashort celiac disease'). Guidelines mandate at least 1 to 2 biopsies from the duodenal bulb (at the 9 or 12 o'clock position to avoid Brunner gland artifact) plus at least 4 biopsies from the post-bulbar second portion of the duodenum. [2, 3, 5]

## Acute upper GI hemorrhage risk stratification: Glasgow-Blatchford Score and Forrest ulcer classification

When upper endoscopy is performed for acute non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding (melena or hematemesis), pre-endoscopic risk stratification using the Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS—incorporating blood urea nitrogen, hemoglobin, systolic blood pressure, pulse, melena, syncope, hepatic disease, and heart failure) identifies very low-risk patients (GBS = 0 or <= 1) who have a < 1 percent risk of requiring endoscopic hemostasis, transfusion, or death and can safely undergo outpatient EGD. For hospitalized patients, ACG and ESGE guidelines recommend performing EGD within 24 hours of presentation after hemodynamic resuscitation (noting that a randomized trial of urgent < 6-hour vs 6-to-24-hour EGD by Lau et al., NEJM 2020, showed no mortality benefit from rushing before resuscitation). [8, 9]

During EGD for peptic ulcer bleeding, the Forrest classification of the ulcer base directly dictates whether dual-modality endoscopic hemostasis (epinephrine injection plus thermal contact coagulation or through-the-scope hemoclipping) is required and predicts the 72-hour rebleeding risk:
- Forrest Ia (spurting arterial hemorrhage) and Ib (oozing hemorrhage): Active bleeding (~55 to 90 percent untreated rebleed risk); mandates immediate dual endoscopic hemostasis plus high-dose intravenous PPI therapy.
- Forrest IIa (non-bleeding visible vessel—a raised red, purple, or translucent sentinel clot plug protruding from an eroded artery in the ulcer floor): 43 percent untreated rebleed risk; mandates dual endoscopic hemostasis.
- Forrest IIb (adherent clot resistant to vigorous water-jet irrigation): 22 percent untreated rebleed risk; managed with targeted clot guillotine/hemostasis or intensive PPI therapy.
- Forrest IIc (flat pigmented hematin spot, < 7 to 10 percent rebleed risk) and Forrest III (clean white fibrinous ulcer base, < 3 to 5 percent rebleed risk): Do not require endoscopic thermal or clip therapy (which risks perforation without benefit); patients can be resumed on a regular diet and transitioned to oral PPI therapy. [8, 9]

## Documented clinical cases: Real-world upper endoscopic diagnostic rescues

A classic clinical scenario documented in celiac disease quality audits (Lebwohl et al., Gastrointest Endosc) involves a 34-year-old woman with profound iron-deficiency anemia (ferritin 4 ng/mL) and elevated tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA 68 U/mL) whose initial community EGD reported 'normal duodenal mucosa' based on two single-forceps bites from the descending duodenum that showed only nonspecific focal villous blunting (Marsh 0/1). On repeat EGD following the structured 6-biopsy protocol (2 duodenal bulb biopsies at 9 and 12 o'clock plus 4 post-bulbar D2 biopsies with single-bite orientation to prevent crush artifact), the duodenal bulb and two of four D2 specimens demonstrated subtotal villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and 45 intraepithelial lymphocytes per 100 enterocytes (Marsh 3b), confirming celiac disease and resolving her anemia on a strict gluten-free diet. [3, 10]

A second documented diagnostic pitfall from upper GI bleeding registries involves a 67-year-old man presenting with syncope and melena (hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL, BUN 48 mg/dL) following nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use. At EGD within 18 hours, a 12-millimeter posterior duodenal bulb ulcer was identified with a smooth, raised 2-millimeter pearl-white mound in the ulcer crater. An inexperienced operator could mistake a pale sentinel plug for a benign clean base (Forrest III); however, close water-jet inspection confirmed a non-bleeding visible vessel (Forrest IIa) overlying the gastroduodenal artery territory. Prophylactic dual hemostasis (epinephrine tamponade plus bipolar multipolar electrocoagulation and over-the-scope clip placement) prevented catastrophic arterial rebleeding. [8, 9]

## Diagnostic boundaries, specimen crush artifacts, and safety verification

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy inspects only the luminal mucosal surface; submucosal stromal tumors (such as gastrointestinal stromal tumors [GISTs] or leiomyomas), extrinsic pancreaticobiliary compression, and intramural motility disorders (such as Type I/II/III achalasia or jackhammer esophagus) can appear completely normal on routine EGD and require Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS) or High-Resolution Esophageal Manometry (HREM) for definitive diagnosis. Furthermore, taking three or four tissue bites with a single pass of standard biopsy forceps ('multi-bite overloading') crushes the delicate villous architecture of duodenal specimens and reduces histological orientation compared with taking one to two bites per forceps pass. [1, 3, 4]

Complications of diagnostic EGD without therapeutic dilation or resection are rare (overall perforation rate ~1 in 2,500 to 1 in 10,000, most commonly at Killian's dehiscence above the cricopharyngeus in patients with Zenker diverticulum or cervical osteophytes, and cardiopulmonary sedation events in ~1 in 200 to 1 in 1,000 procedures). Post-procedural severe retrosternal chest pain, subcutaneous crepitus in the neck, odynophagia, fever, or tachycardia requires immediate upright chest/neck radiography and water-soluble contrast computed tomography to rule out esophageal perforation (Boerhaave/iatrogenic tear) before oral intake is resumed. [4, 6]

# H016: Pregnancy testing with human chorionic gonadotropin

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a pregnancy test actually prove when it becomes positive, stays negative, or refuses to return to negative?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Symptoms determine urgency

Severe abdominal pain, collapse, or major deterioration with possible pregnancy warrants emergency care now, regardless of a previous pregnancy test result.

## From a question to a specimen

A pregnancy test looks for human chorionic gonadotropin, abbreviated hCG. A qualitative result answers whether the specimen meets that test's positive criterion; a quantitative result reports a concentration. Those are different questions, even when both reports use the word pregnancy. A home urine test offers an accessible first measurement, but its usefulness depends on the timing and the instructions for that particular product. The FDA advises following the collection and reading directions, checking the result at the specified time, and recognizing that early testing can be negative despite pregnancy. First morning urine may improve detection because it is usually more concentrated. A negative test followed by continued suspicion may need repetition or clinical assessment. Recording the test date and the expected menstrual date makes that follow-up more informative. Repeatedly inspecting a cassette outside its permitted reading window does not turn it into a quantitative assay or establish how far a pregnancy has progressed. [1]

## The hormone and the laboratory signal

hCG is a glycoprotein, a protein with attached sugars, composed of alpha and beta subunits. Its pregnancy-related action includes maintaining the corpus luteum, the ovarian structure supporting early hormone production. The beta portion helps distinguish hCG from related pituitary hormones. What reaches a report depends on the antibodies and molecular forms the assay recognizes. Mayo's THCG serum pregnancy service measures intact hCG plus its beta subunit using an electrochemiluminescent immunoassay: two antibodies capture the target, particles secure the complex, and electrically stimulated light provides the analytical signal. This is a particular Roche-based laboratory implementation, not a description of every hCG instrument. The service specifies serum preparation and acceptable specimen condition, including rejection of grossly hemolyzed samples. Its pregnancy assay is explicitly not intended for tumor monitoring. Thus the order name, specimen type, and method matter before any number is compared with a prior result or used for a different clinical purpose. [2]

## How a line appears on a urine cassette

A lateral-flow test converts molecular recognition into a visible line. In the FDA submission for the Wunder urine cassette, liquid moves through a membrane by capillary action. Mobile antibodies recognizing beta hCG and immobilized antibodies recognizing the alpha portion form a capture system; accumulated labeled complexes produce the test line. A separate control line checks successful migration and reagent function. The device's specified positive cutoff is 25 mIU/mL, a decision limit for that product rather than a universal biological boundary between pregnant and not pregnant. Its experiments near that boundary produced mixed positive and negative readings below the claimed cutoff. A control line therefore validates aspects of test operation, not pregnancy exclusion at every concentration. The submission concerns a prescription point-of-care cassette with defined specimen and performance claims. Its results cannot automatically validate another brand, a photograph of a faint line, or a use outside the manufacturer's instructions. [3]

## Why biology sets the earliest detection time

Before considering instrument sensitivity, there must be a detectable biological signal. Wilcox and colleagues followed women with daily urine sampling and a sensitive research assay in their 1999 implantation study, DOI 10.1056/NEJM199906103402304. Among pregnancies that resulted in live birth, the first urinary hCG signal appeared six to twelve days after ovulation, most often on days eight through ten. This was a biochemical marker associated with implantation, not direct observation of the first cells attaching to the uterus. The study also related later first detection to greater early loss in its cohort. That association should not become a personal prognosis from the date a home test first turns positive: home devices differ from the research assay, ovulation is often uncertain, and sampling frequency changes the apparent first day. The valuable lesson is the existence of a biological interval when conception may have occurred but even a technically excellent test has little or no hormone to detect. [4]

## Worked example a negative result on the expected day

Consider an explicitly hypothetical group of 100 pregnancies that will become clinically recognizable. This is a teaching calculation, not a new patient series. Wilcox's 2001 analysis, DOI 10.1001/jama.286.14.1759, estimated that the highest possible sensitivity of hCG screening on the first expected day of a missed period was about 90%, because some pregnancies had not yet implanted. Applied illustratively, about ten of these 100 could still be biologically undetectable that day even before imperfect kit performance is added. The estimated ceiling increased to about 97% one week later. These are cohort-based estimates tied to expected menstrual timing, not guarantees for an individual or measured accuracy claims for a particular current kit. A very sensitive analytical detection limit cannot eliminate variation in ovulation, implantation, or menstrual dating. The practical reasoning is to distinguish a test performed too early from a test that has malfunctioned, rather than assuming every early negative has one explanation. [5]

## A positive result does not give an address

An ectopic pregnancy develops outside the uterine cavity. When hCG is positive but ultrasound has not established the location, pregnancy of unknown location describes an unresolved finding, not a final diagnosis. NICE advises that such a pregnancy could be ectopic until its location is determined and specifically says not to use serum hCG to determine location. Clinical symptoms take precedence over an apparently reassuring hormone pattern. Its pathway uses serial serum measurements approximately 48 hours apart together with ultrasound and subsequent review, rather than a single value as a map. New or worsening symptoms require reassessment regardless of earlier results. Severe abdominal pain, collapse, or major clinical deterioration with possible pregnancy warrants emergency care now; waiting for another home test or the next scheduled blood draw is unsafe in that setting. In stable follow-up, the central questions are what remains unknown, when reassessment will occur, and what symptoms should trigger earlier help. [6]

## Worked example a rise is not a location diagnosis

Imagine two serum results from the same method, 1,000 and 1,600 mIU/mL approximately 48 hours apart. These invented values illustrate arithmetic, not a reported patient or a treatment rule. The increase is (1,600 minus 1,000) divided by 1,000, or 60%. Failure to double does not by itself establish pregnancy failure. Barnhart's 2016 study, DOI 10.1097/AOG.0000000000001568, examined 285 symptomatic women initially presenting with pregnancy of unknown location who ultimately had a normal intrauterine pregnancy. The lower expected rise depended on the starting concentration; the investigators did not find one minimum percentage suitable for all initial values. Because the cohort was selected for its eventual outcome, it cannot make a rising value prove that an unlocated pregnancy is intrauterine or viable. The calculated change is one piece of longitudinal evidence. The unresolved clinical questions still require symptoms, imaging, and follow-up rather than a pass or fail label attached to the percentage alone. [7]

## Why a discriminatory level is not an automatic verdict

A discriminatory level is a proposed serum hCG concentration above which an intrauterine gestational sac should be visible with a particular ultrasound approach. It is a decision concept, not a reference interval for healthy pregnancy. Doubilet and Benson's 2011 study, DOI 10.7863/jum.2011.30.12.1637, identified 202 pregnancies with no intrauterine fluid collection on the initial scan but a subsequently documented live intrauterine pregnancy. Nine initially had hCG above 2,000 mIU/mL, a commonly proposed boundary at that time. The selected design cannot estimate the chance of a normal outcome among all people with an empty uterus and high hCG. It does demonstrate that the combination is not infallible proof of ectopic pregnancy. Ultrasound quality, the stage of development, and the clinical situation must be considered before irreversible decisions. The careful question is whether the evidence establishes location or viability sufficiently, rather than whether a number has crossed a memorable line. [8]

## Published cohort when a negative urine test was insufficient

Kleinschmidt and colleagues studied 10,924 emergency-department visits with a negative point-of-care urine pregnancy test, DOI 10.1002/emp2.12427. Review identified 171 pregnancies already present at the index visit, including twelve ectopic pregnancies. Among 2,732 visits involving abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding, 97 pregnancies were present despite the negative urine result. These are retrospective findings from one service and testing pathway, not universal failure rates for all home products. Importantly, a pregnancy present despite negative urine was not necessarily missed by the treating team: concurrent serum testing recognized many. The study therefore supports a diagnostic pathway that can override a urine result when the clinical question remains unresolved. It does not justify treating every negative test as equally unreliable or replacing all home testing with emergency testing. For an individual presentation, the issue is whether the specimen, timing, and method are sufficient for the decision being made. [9]

## When more hormone can produce less visible signal

False negatives are not confined to very early pregnancy or dilute urine. hCG exists in multiple molecular forms, and a device may recognize them unequally. Gronowski's 2009 investigation, DOI 10.1373/clinchem.2008.121210, examined three patient urine specimens that produced false-negative results on one qualitative platform. The investigators measured hormone variants and added purified hCG beta core fragment to positive specimens. Excess fragment reproduced false negatives in two tested devices. This fragment is a breakdown product that can accumulate in urine; its interaction with the antibody system can prevent the intended capture sandwich from forming properly. The finding is method-specific evidence, not proof that all cassettes fail at the same gestational stage or concentration. A discordant result may require laboratory-directed testing with a different method. The mechanism explains why simply insisting that more hormone must always make a darker line is wrong. It does not provide a reliable home dilution experiment or a way to date pregnancy from line intensity. [10]

## A persistent positive deserves a structured investigation

The 2026 ACOG Clinical Consensus on positive hCG in nonpregnant patients replaces its older 2002 committee opinion. It begins by evaluating pregnancy, including ectopic pregnancy, before classifying a persistent signal as a nonpregnancy finding. When the clinical picture and assay disagree, direct discussion with the clinical laboratory is central. Heterophilic antibodies, antibodies that react with assay reagents rather than the intended target, can generate an apparent serum hCG signal. The guidance describes comparison with another assay, paired urine testing in an appropriate analytical context, and laboratory assessment of how the result behaves on dilution. None is a universal stand-alone answer without considering sensitivity and specimen conditions. A serum-positive, urine-negative pair can have several explanations when concentrations are low. The aim is to establish whether the signal represents genuine hCG and, if so, its source before undertaking invasive investigation or cancer treatment. A repeated number is not independent confirmation when the same interference repeatedly enters the same assay. [11]

## Published cases the consequences of accepting a false signal

Rotmensch and Cole reported twelve women diagnosed with presumed malignant disease on the basis of persistent hCG, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(00)01324-6. The accessible primary abstract does not provide all individual ages. Most underwent surgery, chemotherapy, or both without a meaningful reduction in the apparent hormone concentration. Investigation using different assays, urine findings, and other analytical comparisons showed that the positive results were false. The outcome documented is the correction of the diagnoses after substantial unnecessary intervention; the accessible account does not establish each woman's later fertility, recovery, or long-term health. An erratum notes that six cases had been reported previously, so this is not twelve entirely new cases. The series illustrates the harm of allowing a laboratory signal to substitute for coherent evidence of disease. It cannot establish the present-day frequency of false positives or make all persistent hCG benign. Its strongest lesson is to verify an unexplained result before interpreting persistence as treatment-resistant cancer. [12, 17]

## The pituitary can make a small genuine signal

Not every unexplained positive is an assay error. The pituitary, the hormone-regulating gland at the base of the brain, can produce hCG, particularly around and after menopause. Gronowski and colleagues investigated the usefulness of follicle-stimulating hormone, FSH, in perimenopausal women with low positive hCG, DOI 10.1373/clinchem.2007.100305. Their study concerned a defined age group and a narrow initial hCG range; its FSH findings must not be transplanted into all ages, assay systems, or pregnancy scenarios. FSH offers evidence about the reproductive hormonal setting rather than locating a pregnancy or proving cancer absence. The distinction between a genuine pituitary signal and a falsely generated analytical signal is clinically important because their explanations and follow-up differ. Useful questions include whether age and menstrual history fit the proposed source, whether pregnancy has been evaluated, and whether the measured forms and units are comparable. A small concentration should be investigated in context rather than assumed to mean either an early pregnancy or a malignancy. [13]

## Published forensic case the positive test did not establish location

Jayanth and colleagues' fatal ectopic-pregnancy report appeared online in 2018 and in the 2019 journal issue, DOI 10.1177/0025817218795337. A 26-year-old woman with abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding arrived unresponsive and in shock; resuscitation was unsuccessful. The history included eight weeks without menstruation and a positive pregnancy test. Autopsy established a ruptured tubal ectopic pregnancy. This account is limited to the accessible primary abstract: it does not report a quantitative hCG value, assay identity, or a diagnostic ultrasound result. There was therefore no measured hormone threshold available to explain when rupture occurred or to establish which earlier clinical choices would have changed the outcome. The forensic finding supplies anatomical information that the reported positive test had not supplied. A fatal case is powerful evidence that pregnancy location matters, but it is not evidence that every positive test represents an emergency or that a home line can distinguish an intrauterine pregnancy from one in the fallopian tube. [14]

## Published case a rare ectopic pregnancy with negative serum hCG

Kopelman and colleagues described a 23-year-old woman with sudden abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and subsequent syncope, DOI 10.1016/j.crwh.2021.e00288. Serum qualitative testing was negative and quantitative hCG was below 5 mIU/mL. Imaging showed blood in the abdomen and a right-sided lesion. Surgery found a bleeding tubal mass; right salpingectomy, removal of the affected fallopian tube, was performed, and about 500 mL of blood was evacuated. Pathology identified chorionic villi, confirming ectopic pregnancy. Her postoperative course was uncomplicated and she left hospital on day two. A presumed completed miscarriage two months earlier had never been confirmed as intrauterine. The authors proposed a chronic ectopic pregnancy, but did not prove that explanation. This rare case does not estimate the risk after a negative serum test in an asymptomatic population. It shows why an acute abdominal emergency still requires investigation when a laboratory result makes one diagnosis less likely; the clinical deterioration and imaging were consequential evidence. [15]

## Following a fall to an actual resolution

A declining concentration also needs an endpoint. Barnhart's 2004 study, DOI 10.1097/01.AOG.0000142712.80407.fd, examined symptomatic pregnancies initially of unknown location and identified 710 that ultimately met its miscarriage definition. The expected decline varied with the starting hCG concentration, with faster falls generally occurring from higher values. The study's curves describe a selected population and its specified follow-up outcomes, not a universal percentage that makes every falling result safe. A fall indicates changing hormone production or clearance; it does not by itself provide anatomical proof of where a pregnancy was or establish that all clinically important tissue has resolved. Follow-up should therefore answer the question that prompted it, rather than stop simply because a value moved in the hoped-for direction. Explanatory questions include whether location was ever established, what counts as resolution in this pathway, whether symptoms have changed, and who will review subsequent results. The objective is a clinically resolved uncertainty, not merely a reassuring trend on a graph. [16]

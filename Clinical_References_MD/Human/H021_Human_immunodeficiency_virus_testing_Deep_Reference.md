# H021: Human immunodeficiency virus testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can HIV testing detect infection early and resolve results that initially seem contradictory?*

## What the testing pathway is for

Human immunodeficiency virus, abbreviated HIV, can be present for years before a person recognizes symptoms. Testing can identify infection while treatment can still prevent substantial immune damage. A diagnosis of HIV does not itself mean that someone has AIDS, the advanced stage of the disease. Current United States CDC guidance recommends at least one routine test for people aged 13 to 64, with repeat screening for ongoing exposure risk and testing during pregnancy. These population recommendations do not prohibit testing outside that age range when clinically appropriate. This chapter explains the United States laboratory pathway; other countries use validated national algorithms. Testing should include an agreed way to receive results and reach follow-up care, because an unanswered report does not complete the diagnostic task. [1]

## Three biological signals, three different questions

An antigen is a component of the virus; an antibody is a protein the immune system makes in response. Laboratory combination assays look for HIV-1 p24 antigen together with antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2. In Mayo's diagnostic serum assay, selective binding reactions are converted into an electrically triggered light signal, called electrochemiluminescence. The instrument compares that signal with an assay boundary. It is not looking at intact viruses under a microscope. A reactive combination result does not necessarily identify which component produced the reaction, and its signal is not a viral count. The laboratory must continue the appropriate sequence. This particular catalog also distinguishes diagnostic patient testing from blood-donor screening, which has its own requirements. [2]

A nucleic acid test, or NAT, looks for viral genetic material. In a reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction assay, RNA is converted into DNA, and selected target sequences are amplified until they can be detected. Mayo's qualitative HIV-1/HIV-2 RNA assay reports detection rather than a numerical viral load. Internal controls help identify failure or inhibition of the reaction. An inconclusive result means the test could not reliably settle presence or absence; it is not equivalent to negative. Specimen handling also matters: unsuitable storage or processing can impair RNA detection. The patient usually experiences a routine blood draw, while the collection service and laboratory manage the platform-specific requirements and determine whether another sample is needed. [3]

## The window period belongs to the exact test

After an exposure, HIV markers take time to become detectable. CDC gives usual detection ranges of 10 to 33 days for NAT, 18 to 45 days for a laboratory antigen/antibody test using venous blood, 18 to 90 days for a rapid fingerstick antigen/antibody test, and 23 to 90 days for antibody tests. These ranges describe test categories, not a promise that every infection is detectable on the first day listed. A test on the day of an exposure cannot exclude infection acquired that day. The clinician relates the assay and collection date to the last possible exposure and arranges repeat testing when indicated. Antiretroviral prevention or treatment requires additional interpretation, discussed below. [4]

## Why a reactive screen starts a sequence

The CDC and Association of Public Health Laboratories algorithm begins with a laboratory HIV-1/HIV-2 antigen/antibody immunoassay. A reactive result proceeds to an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay, which determines whether the antibody pattern supports HIV-1 or HIV-2. When that supplemental assay is negative or indeterminate, diagnostic nucleic acid testing resolves whether the first signal represents early HIV infection or a false reaction. Early infection can produce detectable virus before the antibody pattern has developed fully. Repeating only the original screening test does not answer that biological question. The tests complement one another because they examine different markers; the sequence is more informative than collecting several copies of the same preliminary result. [5]

A good report states each assay result and the interpretation of the completed laboratory algorithm. The Association's October 2025 reporting guidance emphasizes the distinction between a final result from one instrument and the overall diagnostic interpretation. A portal may display the reactive screen before supplemental results appear. Pending testing should be communicated as pending, with a clinician responsible for completing it. The updated document also recognizes newer diagnostic NATs and supplemental assays, so older advice that every positive screen requires a Western blot is outdated for this pathway. Its standard reporting framework is not intended to manage people taking pre-exposure prophylaxis; those patients need the specific clinical testing approach described later. [6]

## Indeterminate does not mean partly infected

Indeterminate describes an assay pattern that does not meet its established positive or negative criteria. It is not a measurement of a fraction of an infection. New York's current testing guideline calls for prompt RNA testing after a reactive screen with a negative or indeterminate differentiation result when this has not already occurred automatically. Reflex testing means the laboratory adds the next indicated test without requiring another appointment or order. A newly collected specimen may be needed when the pattern remains unresolved. The ordering team should establish what has actually been performed, rather than assume that a laboratory comment is a completed test. Recent symptoms, exposures and medicines help determine the next step and the urgency of review. [7]

HIV-2 makes assay identity particularly important. It is concentrated in West Africa and occurs elsewhere through migration and transmission networks. Standard HIV-1 RNA assays do not reliably detect or quantify HIV-2. Therefore, an undetectable HIV-1 viral load cannot exclude HIV-2 when serology and exposure history suggest it. Even an HIV-2-specific RNA test may be undetectable in an untreated person with HIV-2, and immune deterioration can still occur. Specialist laboratories can help resolve atypical patterns using appropriate HIV-2 testing. Distinguishing the types also changes treatment choices because their drug susceptibility differs. Geography informs the assessment; it should neither replace laboratory confirmation nor become a reason to overlook HIV-2 in someone without an obvious travel history. [8]

## False positives and the denominator

Specificity is the ability to classify uninfected people correctly. Even a highly specific screen can produce false reactions, particularly when testing many people in a population where infection is uncommon. Consider a hypothetical example: among 10,000 people, assume 10 have HIV, sensitivity is 100 percent, and specificity is 99.9 percent. There would be about 10 true reactive results and 10 false reactive results. This is invented arithmetic, not a performance estimate for a named assay or an actual screening program. It explains why a reactive screen needs confirmation. Biological cross-reactions, specimen errors and technical problems are different possible causes; a person cannot determine which applies by examining the screening number alone. [9]

## What a self-test can and cannot finish

An oral-fluid self-test can make testing private and accessible, but the specimen and method matter. The FDA-authorized OraQuick test detects antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2; it does not provide the early RNA information of a NAT. FDA describes an approximately three-month window for this product. Its clinical study sensitivity was approximately 92 percent, a figure specific to the studied home-use setting rather than all HIV assays. A reactive self-test needs medical laboratory follow-up. A negative result obtained too soon after exposure cannot provide the reassurance of a correctly timed clinical pathway. An invalid result means the device did not give an interpretable answer. Follow the current product instructions and contact a testing service when the result or timing is uncertain. [10]

## Symptoms can change the testing request

Acute HIV can resemble influenza or another viral illness, with fever, sore throat, enlarged lymph nodes or rash; some people notice little or nothing. Symptoms alone cannot diagnose it. CDC advises HIV RNA testing when acute infection is suspected despite a negative or indeterminate initial pathway. Telling the clinician about a recent exposure can therefore change which test is ordered. A generic negative antibody result is insufficient when the clinical question is whether infection is too recent for antibodies to have formed. Conversely, a rash after an exposure is not proof of HIV. The useful response is clinical assessment with appropriately timed testing, together with investigation of other possible causes of the illness. [11]

## Prevention medicines can change marker development

Pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, is medicine used to prevent HIV in people without infection. If infection occurs despite prevention, antiretroviral activity can suppress viral RNA and delay antibody development, making results difficult to interpret. CDC's current PrEP guidance bases testing on recent medication history, including oral and long-acting agents, and advises a new blood specimen when results are ambiguous. Oral rapid tests are less suitable for this setting because they may miss recent infection. Decisions about continuing or changing prevention belong to the treating team while confirmation is arranged. A reader should report the exact product and dates, including injections received elsewhere; the laboratory cannot infer those exposures from the specimen. [12]

The HPTN 083 investigation documented the consequences. It examined HIV infections among cisgender men and transgender women who have sex with men in a prevention trial. Cabotegravir exposure sometimes suppressed virus and delayed antibody expression, so study-site testing recognized infection later than retrospective testing. Five cabotegravir-arm cases had mutations associated with resistance to integrase inhibitors, a class of HIV drugs. In these five, site detection occurred a median of 47 days after the first HIV-positive visit identified retrospectively, and participants received additional injections before diagnosis. This was a specific diagnostic and resistance outcome, not proof that every person using injectable prevention will have delayed detection. The findings support carefully designed follow-up rather than abandonment of effective prevention. [13]

## Recent exposure needs assessment before a test becomes positive

Post-exposure prophylaxis, or PEP, is emergency prevention after a potentially significant exposure. CDC's 2025 guidance recommends starting indicated nonoccupational PEP as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after exposure; laboratory results should not delay the first treatment decision. That is a prevention deadline for this circumstance, not a rule that symptoms must develop within 72 hours. A baseline negative test cannot exclude acquisition from the event that prompted the visit. Follow-up generally combines laboratory antigen/antibody testing and diagnostic NAT at four to six weeks and at the final twelve-week visit, with specified exceptions for interim testing. Antiretroviral exposure can delay detection. The clinician must connect timing to the actual exposure and treatment course. [14]

## Pregnancy and infancy require their own pathway

Pregnancy testing aims to protect both the pregnant person and infant. NIH guidance recommends expedited testing during labor when HIV status is undocumented, or when renewed risk was not followed by appropriate repeat testing. A reactive result triggers immediate supplemental maternal testing and infant assessment while the delivery team makes time-sensitive prevention decisions. An initial reactive result still requires confirmation; urgency and diagnostic accuracy must proceed together. Maternal infection acquired after an earlier negative prenatal test is one reason that old records cannot always settle the present question. Decisions about infant feeding and preventive treatment should be made with the obstetric and pediatric teams while results are clarified, rather than improvised from a preliminary portal notification. [15]

In an infant with perinatal exposure, antibodies transferred across the placenta can make an antibody test positive without infection of the infant. NIH therefore recommends virologic RNA or DNA testing for diagnosis below 18 months in this population. A positive virologic result is confirmed on a separate blood sample. Testing follows an age- and risk-specific schedule because a birth specimen cannot detect every infection acquired around delivery, and preventive medicines may reduce assay sensitivity. Breastfeeding creates an additional exposure timeline requiring its own follow-up. Occasionally maternal antibodies persist beyond 18 months, so a positive antibody result in an exposed older infant may still need NAT clarification. Adult window-period charts cannot substitute for this pediatric pathway. [16]

## A published false-reactive screen during autoimmune liver disease

Robinson and colleagues reported a 51-year-old woman with abdominal pain, jaundice and severe liver-enzyme elevation. Her HIV combination screen remained reactive on repeat, but HIV-1 NAT was negative. Liver biopsy and other findings supported autoimmune hepatitis, an immune attack on the liver. She improved with corticosteroid treatment and was discharged for gastroenterology follow-up. The report did not document a completed HIV differentiation assay because it was unavailable locally, and long-term follow-up was not supplied. The authors considered assay interference associated with the autoimmune illness plausible; the precise mechanism was not established. This case illustrates a misleading preliminary signal and the limits of the documented workup, not a license to dismiss reactive HIV screens in everyone with liver disease. [17]

## A molecular result altered by cell therapy

Hauser and colleagues described a 16-year-old girl treated with engineered immune cells, called CAR T cells, for leukemia. Before bone marrow transplantation, HIV serology was nonreactive while one RNA platform repeatedly reported low positive results. A different platform targeting another viral region found no RNA, and the team attributed the original results to interference from the lentiviral vector, a modified viral delivery system carrying genetic instructions, used to engineer the cells. Shared target sequences could trigger the assay without actual HIV infection. She proceeded to transplantation and achieved remission, but leukemia relapsed four months later. The interference was specific to therapy and assay targets; repeating an affected method alone would have reinforced the wrong impression. [18]

## Undetectable RNA can coexist with established infection

Fadl and colleagues reported a 62-year-old woman whose hospital screening showed confirmed HIV seropositivity, preserved CD4 cells and undetectable RNA without documented antiretroviral treatment. Earlier records contained positive HIV results dating to 2015, but the authors found no documented disclosure, counseling or treatment. They interpreted her course as elite control, an uncommon capacity to suppress replication naturally. Reported laboratory follow-up through August 2025 continued to show undetectable RNA; a subsequent treatment outcome was not provided. The case cannot establish why each earlier encounter failed. It illustrates two distinct problems: undetectable RNA does not automatically invalidate established serologic evidence, and storing a correct result in disconnected records does not ensure that the person understands or receives care for the diagnosis. [19]

## A transmission investigation requires more than a positive test

CDC's New Mexico investigation identified five linked HIV diagnoses during 2018–2023: four former clients of an unlicensed spa and one client's sexual partner. Viral sequences were closely related, and investigators found serious infection-control deficiencies. Evidence supported probable transmission through platelet-rich plasma microneedling for three clients; the couple's established infections likely preceded those services. Three people already had advanced disease at diagnosis. A specific contamination event was not confirmed, and the report did not provide individual treatment outcomes. This matters for forensic interpretation: a positive test establishes a person's diagnostic status, whereas attributing acquisition to a particular procedure requires timing, records, laboratory comparison and competing explanations. The report should not be simplified into five proven procedure-acquired infections. [20]

A separate forensic investigation used viral gene sequences from a man and two women to explore an alleged transmission relationship. The published abstract describes analyses of different genome regions and emphasizes that unsampled infections and the method's inherent limits prevented unambiguous proof of direct person-to-person transmission. The full report was not available for this chapter, so no court verdict or patient outcome is inferred. A phylogenetic tree groups related viral sequences; it is not a human DNA fingerprint identifying a single perpetrator. Close relatedness can support an epidemiological hypothesis, but an intermediary person may not have been sampled. Clinical diagnosis, public health linkage and legal attribution are different conclusions with different evidence requirements. [21]

## After diagnosis, testing measures different outcomes

Once HIV is confirmed, viral load and CD4 count answer different questions. Plasma RNA estimates current viral replication, while the CD4 count assesses a key part of immune function. NIH's current initial-assessment guidance also includes tests for coinfections and conditions relevant to safe treatment. A high CD4 count does not negate HIV, and a falling viral load does not mean antibodies should disappear. Follow-up schedules depend on treatment stage, clinical stability and other findings. The useful record includes assay, units, date and medication history. Comparing an antibody screening index with RNA copies per milliliter would compare different measurements, not two estimates of the same quantity. A clinician explains which result is being followed and why. [22]

Resistance testing examines viral genetic changes that can reduce the effectiveness of particular medicines. NIH recommends it at entry into care to guide treatment, including appropriate integrase testing when prior prevention or treatment makes that resistance relevant. Genotyping is not the test that initially proves everyone has HIV, and obtaining it should not unnecessarily delay indicated treatment. The team can adjust a regimen when results arrive. A negative resistance report does not guarantee that every possible resistant variant has been excluded, especially when viral RNA is too low for a successful assay. This distinction helps readers understand why diagnosis, selection of treatment and measurement of treatment response may require several related laboratory orders. [23]

Successful treatment can reduce HIV RNA below a laboratory's detection limit. This means the assay cannot detect circulating RNA at its stated sensitivity, not that HIV has been eradicated from every cell. CDC affirms that people taking treatment who achieve and maintain viral suppression do not transmit HIV through sex. That prevention evidence should be communicated clearly rather than undermined by fear about the word positive on an antibody report. It does not turn an old undetectable result into a guarantee about current suppression, prevent other sexually transmitted infections, or supply an equivalent zero-risk conclusion for breastfeeding or shared injection equipment. Regular care connects the diagnosis to sustained health and to the particular outcome each test can actually establish. [24]

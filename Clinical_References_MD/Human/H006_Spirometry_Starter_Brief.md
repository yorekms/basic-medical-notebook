# H006: Spirometry

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Respiratory function
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft. No independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: Spirometry test, Forced expiratory spirometry

## Plain-Language Overview

Spirometry measures a strong outward breath to show how much air comes out and how quickly. The operator coaches the person through repeated efforts because the quality of the breathing maneuver affects the result.

## Clinical Purpose

Measures how much air a person exhales and how quickly it leaves the lungs. It contributes to assessment and monitoring of breathing disorders.

## How the Test Works

The device measures expired volume or airflow over time. Forced vital capacity, or FVC, is the amount exhaled during a full forced expiration; FEV1 is the amount exhaled in its first second. Their ratio relates early airflow to total expired volume. Flow-volume and volume-time curves let the operator inspect the maneuver as well as its numerical outputs.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

The person seals their lips around a mouthpiece, inhales deeply, and blows out forcefully as directed. Testing may be repeated after a bronchodilator, an inhaled medicine that opens the airways.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Wear comfortable clothing and follow the testing service's instructions about meals, activity, smoking, and inhalers. Do not withhold medicines without instructions.

## Interpreting Results

Measured airflow and expired volumes are compared with suitable reference values and considered with the person's symptoms and history.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Review acceptability and repeatability before interpreting physiology. Cough, leaks, an inadequate initial inhalation, or early termination can distort measurements. A low FEV1/FVC ratio supports airflow obstruction when judged against appropriate reference values. A low FVC with a preserved ratio can suggest restriction but does not confirm it; lung volume measurement may be needed to determine total lung capacity.

## Limitations & Confounders

Performance requires cooperation and adequate effort. Very young children may have difficulty. Spirometry alone does not identify every lung disease or replace other lung function tests.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Forceful breathing may cause temporary coughing, tiredness, dizziness, or lightheadedness.

## Species & Population Notes

Human voluntary spirometry does not describe veterinary respiratory testing or validate animal measurement methods.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Record whether testing was before or after bronchodilator and which medicines were withheld. Keep lung volumes and gas diffusion testing as separate linked procedures. Retain maneuver curves, quality grades, reference equations, and pre- and post-bronchodilator status. Document equipment quality assurance and operator assessment; repeatable numbers alone do not remove the need to inspect curve quality.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

Lung volume testing assesses total lung capacity, including air not measured by a simple forced exhalation. Diffusion testing evaluates gas transfer. Bronchial challenge testing addresses airway responsiveness and is a separate procedure requiring its own entry and precautions.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How do 21 scalp electrodes record microvolt post-synaptic potentials across six square centimeters of cortex—and why does a completely normal 30-minute routine EEG never rule out epilepsy, while continuous ICU video-EEG unmasks silent non-convulsive status epilepticus?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergent ICU continuous EEG for unexplained coma / non-convulsive status epilepticus vs outpatient epilepsy classification

In any patient who fails to awaken within 15 to 30 minutes after a generalized convulsive seizure, or in an ICU patient with unexplained altered mental status, subtle ocular/facial twitching, or post-cardiac arrest coma, emergent Continuous Video-EEG (cEEG) is mandatory to detect Non-Convulsive Status Epilepticus (NCSE), where ongoing cortical electrical seizures destroy neurons without visible limb convulsions.

## Neurophysiological origin of the scalp EEG: Pyramidal post-synaptic dipoles and the 6 cm2 synchronization rule

Contrary to a common clinical misconception, scalp Electroencephalography (EEG) does NOT record neuronal axonal action potentials: action potentials are too brief (< 1 to 2 milliseconds) and their quadrupolar extracellular fields attenuate too rapidly with distance to penetrate the meninges, cerebrospinal fluid, skull bone, and scalp. Instead, scalp EEG records the summated extracellular currents of slow (10 to 100 milliseconds) Excitatory and Inhibitory Post-Synaptic Potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs) generated along the vertically oriented apical dendrites of Layer III and Layer V cortical pyramidal neurons. Because cortical pyramidal cells are aligned perpendicular to the pial surface like columns of trees, an excitatory synaptic input at the superficial apical dendrites creates an extracellular current sink (negative potential) near the pial surface and a current source (positive potential) at the deep soma—forming a radial cortical dipole. [1, 3]

Because the human skull acts as a thick resistor that smears and attenuates electrical fields by roughly 10- to 100-fold compared with direct cortical electrocorticography, classic simultaneous cortical-and-scalp recording experiments by Cooper, Winter, Crow, and Walter (1965) and Tao et al. (2005) established a fundamental physical boundary of scalp EEG: at least 6 to 10 square centimeters of gyral cerebral cortex must fire in synchronous phase for an interictal epileptiform spike to become visible on a scalp electrode! Consequently, small cortical seizure foci (< 6 cm2), deep mesial temporal (amygdalohippocampal), orbitofrontal, interhemispheric cingulate, or insular discharges—whose dipoles point tangentially or lie deep inside sulcal valleys far from the scalp convexity—frequently fail to register on routine surface EEG even while the patient is experiencing a focal aware (aura) seizure! [1, 3, 4]

## The International 10–20 electrode placement system and bipolar phase-reversal vs referential localization

Standard clinical EEG uses the International 10–20 System, which positions 21 scalp electrodes at proportional distances (10 percent and 20 percent) along anatomical skull landmarks—from the nasion (bridge of the nose) to the inion (occipital protuberance) sagittal midline, and between the left and right preauricular points coronally. Each electrode label pairs a lobar letter (Fp = frontopolar, F = frontal, C = central, P = parietal, O = occipital, T = temporal, A/M = auricular/mastoid) with a number: odd numbers (1, 3, 5, 7) overlie the LEFT hemisphere, even numbers (2, 4, 6, 8) overlie the RIGHT hemisphere, and 'z' (Fz, Cz, Pz) denotes the zero midline. Every EEG channel employs a differential amplifier that subtracts the voltage at Input 2 from Input 1 (V1 minus V2): by international neurophysiology convention, if Input 1 is MORE NEGATIVE than Input 2, the tracing deflects UPWARD; if Input 1 is more positive than Input 2, the tracing deflects DOWNWARD. [1, 5]

Digital recording allows the neurophysiologist to reformat the same raw EEG into complementary montages in real time. In a Longitudinal Bipolar Montage ('double banana,' linking adjacent electrodes in anterior-to-posterior chains such as Fp1-F7, F7-T7, T7-P7, P7-O1), a focal negative surface spike located directly beneath electrode F7 makes F7 the most negative point in the chain: in channel Fp1-F7 (where F7 is Input 2), negativity at Input 2 drives a DOWNWARD deflection, whereas in the immediately adjacent channel F7-T7 (where F7 is Input 1), negativity at Input 1 drives an UPWARD deflection—creating the diagnostic 'phase reversal' (two tips pointing toward each other at the shared electrode F7) that pinpoints the electrical maximum! In contrast, a Referential Montage compares every active scalp electrode (Input 1) against a common quiet reference (Input 2, such as the ipsilateral/contralateral ear, Cz, or a mathematical average reference): there is no phase reversal in a pure referential montage unless the reference itself is contaminated; instead, the electrode with the tallest upward amplitude deflection identifies the negative field maximum. [1, 5]

## Sensitivity of a single routine EEG vs serial EEGs, sleep-deprived EEG, and long-term video-EEG monitoring

The single most important clinical rule when ordering an outpatient routine 20- to 30-minute EEG is that a normal interictal EEG NEVER rules out epilepsy! Because interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs: spikes lasting 20 to 70 milliseconds or sharp waves lasting 70 to 200 milliseconds that disrupt background activity and are followed by an after-coming slow wave) occur intermittently, a single first routine 30-minute awake EEG captures interictal epileptiform abnormalities in only 29 to 50 percent of patients who truly have clinical epilepsy. Conversely, roughly 0.5 to 2.0 percent of healthy adults and 2 to 3.5 percent of healthy children without seizures exhibit incidental epileptiform discharges on a screening EEG—meaning epilepsy can never be diagnosed from an interictal spike alone without a clinical seizure history. [2, 6, 7]

Diagnostic yield increases substantially through three physiological and temporal strategies: (1) Repeating routine EEGs—across classic cohorts (Salinsky et al., Ajmone Marsan and Zivin), cumulative sensitivity for interictal epileptiform discharges rises from ~50 percent on the first routine EEG to ~80 to 85 percent by the third serial EEG and ~90 percent by the fourth EEG (with little added yield from further brief routine recordings beyond four); (2) Recording during NREM sleep (particularly Stage N1 and N2 transition via a sleep-deprived EEG), which synchronizes thalamocortical networks and activates interictal epileptiform spikes in an additional 30 to 40 percent of patients whose waking EEG was completely normal; and (3) Inpatient video-EEG Epilepsy Monitoring Unit (EMU) admission or 24- to 72-hour ambulatory EEG, which records actual habitual clinical spells on synchronized video and EEG to differentiate epileptic seizures from psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES / functional seizures, characterized by preserved posterior-dominant alpha rhythm before and immediately after the spell, closed eyes resisting forced opening, asynchronous side-to-side head shaking or pelvic thrusting, and absence of ictal rhythmic evolution or post-ictal slowing). [2, 6, 8, 9]

## Classical activation procedures (hyperventilation and photic stimulation) and pathognomonic epilepsy syndromes

Every routine EEG includes two provocative activation maneuvers unless medically contraindicated. Three to five minutes of vigorous Hyperventilation lowers arterial carbon dioxide tension (hypocapnia), inducing cerebral vasoconstriction that slows background rhythms and reliably provokes generalized 3-Hertz spike-and-wave absence seizures (accompanied by clinical staring and unresponsiveness) in untreated Childhood Absence Epilepsy! However, hyperventilation is strictly contraindicated in patients with recent intracranial hemorrhage, severe cerebrovascular stenosis, Moyamoya disease (where hypocapnic vasoconstriction precipitates ischemic stroke!), sickle cell disease, or unstable cardiopulmonary disease. Intermittent Photic Stimulation flashes a strobe lamp across frequencies from 1 to 30 Hertz: while a physiologic photic driving response locked over the occipital cortex is normal, a 'photoparoxysmal response'—generalized frontocentral polyspike-and-wave bursts that outlast the flash train, classic at 14 to 20 Hz in Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy (JME) or Dravet syndrome—confirms photosensitive generalized epilepsy. [1, 5, 10]

Several EEG signatures are virtually pathognomonic when paired with clinical age and semiology: (1) Generalized 3-Hz (2.5 to 3.5 Hz) regular, symmetric frontally predominant spike-and-slow-wave discharges triggered by hyperventilation in Childhood Absence Epilepsy; (2) Generalized 4- to 6-Hz polyspike-and-wave discharges upon morning awakening in Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy; (3) Generalized slow (< 2.5 Hz, typically 1.5 to 2.0 Hz) spike-and-wave complexes plus nocturnal paroxysmal fast (10 Hz) tonic rhythms in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome; (4) Hypsarrhythmia—a chaotic, mountainous high-voltage (> 200 to 300 microvolts) multifocal spike and disorganized slow-wave background interrupted by electrodecremental voltage attenuation during epileptic spasms—in infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (West syndrome); and (5) Centrotemporal (Rolandic) horizontal dipole spikes (negative at C3/C4/T7/T8 with simultaneous frontal positivity at F3/F4) that amplify dramatically during drowsiness and NREM sleep in Self-Limited Epilepsy with Centrotemporal Spikes. [1, 10, 11]

## Benign epileptiform variants and artifacts that cause misdiagnosis of epilepsy

Over-reading benign normal EEG variants or physiological/electrode artifacts as 'epileptiform spikes' is the single most common cause of misdiagnosing healthy patients with epilepsy and prescribing unnecessary antiseizure medications. As detailed by Benbadis and Tatum, normal spiky-appearing variants that carry ZERO association with epilepsy include: wicket spikes (single or brief 6- to 11-Hz mu-like monophasic arciform temporal waves in drowsiness that neither disrupt background rhythm nor have an after-coming slow wave); benign sporadic sleep spikes / small sharp spikes (BETS: tiny, diphasic < 50 microvolt, < 50 ms spikes across anterior/mid-temporal regions during Stage N1/N2 sleep); 14- and 6-Hz positive bursts; 6-Hz ('phantom') spike-and-wave; rhythmic mid-temporal theta bursts of drowsiness (RMTD); and Subclinical Rhythmic Electrographic Discharge of Adults (SREDA). [12, 13]

Similarly, non-cerebral artifacts must be recognized by their field distribution and biophysical characteristics: (1) Eye blink (Bell's phenomenon upward roll of the positively charged cornea producing symmetric high-amplitude downward-deflecting slow waves restricted to Fp1 and Fp2) and lateral rectus spike artifact; (2) Glossokinetic artifact (the negatively charged tongue tip touching the palate during swallowing or speaking, creating diffuse frontal delta waves verified by submental EMG); (3) Myogenic temporalis and frontalis EMG artifact (> 30 to 100 Hz dense high-frequency 'grass' that resolves with jaw relaxation); (4) Electrocardiographic QRS complexes picked up in obese or short-necked patients (verified by simultaneous 1-lead ECG channel); and (5) Electrode 'pop' artifact—an abrupt vertical piezoelectrical discharge restricted to a single poorly impedance-matched electrode (violating biological field drop-off across adjacent scalp electrodes!). [1, 12, 13]

## Critical care continuous EEG (ACNS terminology): Non-convulsive status epilepticus, LPDs, GPDs, and the ICTAL-interictal continuum

In the neurological and medical intensive care unit, the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society (ACNS) Standardized Critical Care EEG Terminology classifies rhythmic and periodic patterns by location (Generalized [G], Lateralized [L], Bilateral Independent [BI], or Multifocal [MF]) and morphology (Periodic Discharges [PDs], Rhythmic Delta Activity [RDA], or Spike-and-Wave/Sharp-and-Wave [SW]). Per the Salzburg Consensus Criteria for Non-Convulsive Status Epilepticus (NCSE), definite electrographic seizures in a comatose patient are diagnosed when epileptiform discharges average > 2.5 Hertz for >= 10 seconds, OR when discharges <= 2.5 Hz (or rhythmic activity > 0.5 Hz) show clear spatiotemporal evolution in frequency, morphology, or location, OR show both clinical and EEG improvement after an intravenous antiseizure trial. [2, 14, 15]

Lateralized Periodic Discharges (LPDs, formerly called PLEDs—sharp or complex discharges recurring periodically every 0.5 to 4.0 seconds over one hemisphere) indicate acute focal destructive cortical injury and carry a high (40 to 60 percent) risk of heralding or coexisting with focal seizures; their classic clinical causes are herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) temporal lobe encephalitis, acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, and rapidly growing brain tumors or abscesses. Generalized Periodic Discharges (GPDs) occur in three distinct settings defined by repetition rate and background: (1) ~1-Hertz (every 0.5 to 1.0 second) sharp triphasic GPDs in rapidly progressive dementia are classic (~90 percent sensitivity/specificity in advanced disease) for sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob prion disease; (2) slow GPDs recurring every 4 to 15 seconds ('Radermecker complexes') are pathognomonic for Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE from persistent measles virus); and (3) GPDs with a completely suppressed (< 10 microvolt) inter-burst background ('burst-suppression' or post-anoxic myoclonic GPDs) carry a grave prognosis in post-cardiac arrest hypoxic-ischemic brain injury (when not induced pharmacologically by propofol, midazolam, or barbiturate infusions). Finally, generalized 'triphasic waves' (GPDs with triphasic morphology at 1.5 to 2.5 Hz with an anterior-to-posterior phase lag) occur classically in hepatic, uremic, or toxic-metabolic encephalopathy—though because metabolic triphasic waves and NCSE can overlap, careful clinical correlation is required. [14, 15, 16, 17]

## Documented clinical cohorts: Claassen et al. ICU cEEG study and TELSTAR randomized trial

How long must an ICU patient be monitored on continuous EEG to detect non-convulsive seizures? In Claassen and colleagues' landmark Columbia University cohort of 570 consecutive patients who underwent continuous video-EEG monitoring for unexplained altered mental status or suspected subclinical seizures (Neurology 2004), electrographic seizures were detected in 110 patients (19 percent overall, and 92 percent of those seizures were completely non-convulsive—invisible without EEG!). Crucially, among non-comatose patients who had seizures, 95 percent had their first seizure detected within the first 24 hours of recording, whereas among comatose patients, only 80 percent had their first seizure detected by 24 hours and 87 percent by 48 hours—establishing the clinical rule that a 30-minute routine EEG misses over half of ICU non-convulsive seizures and that comatose patients require at least 24 to 48 hours of continuous EEG monitoring. [2, 18]

In post-cardiac arrest coma, distinguishing treatable seizures from futile hypoxic-ischemic periodic discharges was tested in the multicenter Dutch-Belgian TELSTAR randomized controlled trial (Ruijter et al., New England Journal of Medicine 2022). Among 172 comatose adult cardiac arrest survivors who developed rhythmic or periodic EEG patterns on continuous EEG randomized to intensive stepwise antiseizure medication plus sedative suppression for at least 48 hours versus standard care alone, complete suppression of rhythmic/periodic EEG activity for 48 hours was achieved in 56 percent vs 2 percent, yet poor neurological outcome at 3 months (Cerebral Performance Category 3 to 5: severe disability, vegetative state, or death) occurred in 90 percent of the antiseizure-treatment group versus 92 percent of the control group (difference 2 percentage points; p = 0.68)—demonstrating that generalized periodic discharges after anoxic cardiac arrest largely reflect irreversible diffuse cortical neuronal death rather than a reversible epileptic encephalopathy, although exploratory subgroup analyses suggested potential benefit in the small subset with true evolving non-GPD electrographic seizures. [17, 19]

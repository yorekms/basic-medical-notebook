> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Is iron missing from the body unavailable to developing blood cells or accumulating where it can cause harm?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Active bleeding changes the priority

Vomiting blood with faintness confusion breathing changes or signs of circulatory compromise warrants emergency assessment.

## Iron studies ask where the iron is

Iron studies investigate whether the body has enough iron, whether stored iron is available to developing blood cells, or whether excess iron may be accumulating. Iron helps hemoglobin in red cells carry oxygen and supports other proteins and enzymes. Too little can impair function before anemia becomes obvious. Anemia means a reduced hemoglobin concentration for the relevant population and clinical setting; iron deficiency describes inadequate iron. They are related conditions, rather than interchangeable names. A person can have depleted stores before hemoglobin falls, and anemia can have causes other than iron deficiency. The NIH review describes a progression from reduced stores through restricted supply to iron-deficiency anemia. The value of an iron panel is to locate the problem within that progression and connect it to a cause, rather than label every tired person as needing an iron supplement. [1]

## Serum iron transport capacity and a calculated percentage

Serum iron mainly reflects iron circulating on transferrin, its transport protein. TIBC, total iron-binding capacity, estimates how much iron that transport system could carry. Transferrin saturation is serum iron divided by TIBC, multiplied by 100 when compatible units are used. In a hypothetical example, iron 40 mcg/dL and TIBC 400 mcg/dL give 10% saturation. Mayo's SFEC service releases iron chemically and measures a colored complex; it measures transferrin by turbidity and calculates TIBC and saturation. These are method-specific operations. Serum iron varies with time and food or supplement exposure. The laboratory prefers a morning collection and specifies preparation and acceptable specimens. Readers should obtain the actual collection instructions and discuss prescribed treatment with the ordering team. A low serum iron alone is an unstable foundation for diagnosing depleted stores, and a transiently higher value after recent iron intake does not establish that a deficiency has been corrected. [2]

## Ferritin is a storage signal with an inflammatory complication

Ferritin is a protein that stores iron safely inside cells. Its serum concentration usually provides a useful indication of iron stores, which is why it is often the first iron-status test. Mayo's FERR1 assay uses antibodies to capture ferritin and an electrically triggered light signal to quantify it. This measures the ferritin protein, not a direct weighing of all iron throughout the body. Ferritin is also an acute phase reactant: inflammation can increase it independently of adequate usable iron. Liver disease and several other disorders can raise it as well. Consequently, a low ferritin strongly supports depleted stores, while an apparently normal or high ferritin needs context when inflammation is present. Method-related interference also matters; the service gives specific preparation advice concerning biotin-containing supplements. Laboratory reference intervals and clinical diagnostic thresholds should not be assumed to serve identical purposes. [3]

## Symptoms help define the question but do not supply the diagnosis

NHLBI describes fatigue, dizziness, pale skin, cold hands or feet, breathlessness and chest pain among possible features of iron-deficiency anemia. Mild or moderate disease can produce few symptoms, and none of those features is unique to iron deficiency. The history therefore looks for a mechanism: blood loss, reduced absorption, inadequate intake or a period of greater requirement. Menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding and medical conditions affecting absorption all deserve consideration according to the person's circumstances. A complete blood count supplies hemoglobin and red-cell information; iron studies investigate the iron contribution. A small red-cell size can support the pattern, but it cannot independently identify the cause. The aim is to explain why oxygen delivery or iron-dependent function may be impaired and why the problem developed, rather than match a symptom list to a supplement advertisement. [4]

## Why published ferritin thresholds differ

A threshold is chosen for a particular population and purpose. WHO's 2020 guidance uses ferritin below 15 micrograms/L to identify deficiency in apparently healthy adults and recognizes that higher cutoffs may be appropriate when infection or inflammation is present. It also specifies different considerations for young children and pregnancy. The units ng/mL and micrograms/L are numerically equivalent for ferritin, which prevents one common apparent discrepancy between reports. A more important discrepancy is biological context: a value during an inflammatory illness cannot be read as though the person were otherwise healthy. WHO also cautions that a concentration suggesting possible iron overload requires further assessment. Its guidance supports clinical and population work; it does not make one number an all-purpose diagnosis. Readers comparing recommendations should check the population, inflammatory conditions and intended decision before deciding that one document contradicts another. [5]

## The 2026 hematology guidance makes context explicit

The ASH diagnosis guideline, first released in September 2026, suggests a ferritin threshold of 30 ng/mL or lower rather than 15 or lower for nonmenstruating, nonpregnant adults. It labels this a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence. Additional recommendations address menstruating people, pregnancy and young children, with remarks allowing higher thresholds in specified symptomatic or higher-risk settings. For anemia of inflammation, ASH suggests measuring ferritin together with transferrin saturation instead of relying on ferritin alone. These recommendations make the tradeoff visible: a higher threshold can identify more deficient patients but also increases the chance of labeling someone without the target condition. A reader should therefore avoid treating a laboratory's unflagged result as automatic reassurance, or treating one threshold as an instruction to self-treat. The interpretation belongs to the person's population, symptoms, inflammatory state and the clinical decision under consideration. [6]

## Finding deficiency should begin a search for its cause

The British Society of Gastroenterology guideline emphasizes confirming iron deficiency and investigating why it occurred. In adult men and postmenopausal women, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia can signal gastrointestinal disease, including malignancy, even without obvious digestive symptoms. Celiac disease, urinary blood loss, medicines and other factors can enter the assessment. Treatment to replenish iron and evaluation of the cause are complementary tasks. A hemoglobin rise after iron therapy can support an iron-responsive component, but it does not show that ongoing bleeding or another underlying disease has disappeared. This distinction gives a routine test appropriate seriousness without implying that every low ferritin means cancer. The next investigation depends on age, sex, menstrual history, symptoms, comorbidity and prior findings. An improvement on replacement therapy is meaningful, but it should not automatically close an otherwise unexplained case. [7]

## A gastrointestinal decision threshold answers a narrower question

The American Gastroenterological Association's 2020 gastrointestinal guideline recommends a ferritin cutoff of 45 ng/mL rather than 15 when diagnosing iron deficiency in patients who already have anemia. It also recommends upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluation in asymptomatic men and postmenopausal women with iron-deficiency anemia. For premenopausal women its recommendation is conditional, allowing shared decisions that consider plausible alternative explanations and the burdens of investigation. These are recommendations for a defined evaluation pathway, not a universal screening threshold for all people with normal hemoglobin. The guideline also recognizes that inflammation and kidney disease can require additional markers. Its value for readers is to show how a test result connects to a further question about hidden blood loss or absorption. The ferritin number helps open that inquiry; it does not itself locate the bleeding or establish a tumor diagnosis. [8]

## An additional marker can help when the usual signals conflict

Soluble transferrin receptor, abbreviated sTfR, reflects a fragment of the receptor cells use to obtain transferrin-bound iron. Developing red cells account for much of this receptor activity. When iron is scarce, receptor expression can rise, so sTfR can help assess deficiency when inflammation makes ferritin hard to interpret. It is less affected by inflammatory cytokines than ferritin, but it is not immune to biological ambiguity. Increased red-cell production, including in some hemolytic disorders and thalassemias, can also elevate it. Reduced marrow activity can lower it. Mayo therefore reserves its assay for situations where confounding disease makes ordinary assessment uncertain, rather than recommending it for every routine iron check. This is an example of an additional test being useful because it answers a defined unresolved question, rather than because a longer panel must necessarily be better. [9]

## High ferritin is not a diagnosis of hemochromatosis

EASL's hemochromatosis guideline starts with ferritin and transferrin saturation when iron overload is suspected. Hemochromatosis involves inappropriate iron loading, often related to inherited regulation of absorption, and requires assessment beyond an isolated high ferritin. Inflammation, liver injury, alcohol-associated disease and metabolic conditions can raise ferritin without the same pattern of progressive iron loading. Persistent abnormalities may lead to appropriately selected genetic testing and assessment of liver iron by MRI, with other investigations according to the case. The guideline also distinguishes genotypes and diagnostic circumstances, so a genetic variant is not automatically a measure of organ injury. A reader receiving a high result should understand which question is being tested: excess stored iron, inflammatory activity, tissue damage or a combination. Iron depletion treatment requires an established clinical rationale and monitoring; the ferritin flag alone does not provide one. [10]

## Why taking more iron is not a simple absorption solution

Stoffel and colleagues studied iron absorption using randomized schedules in iron-depleted women. Stable isotope methods allowed the investigators to track absorbed iron, rather than infer absorption from tablet consumption alone. The studies found higher hepcidin responses with some more frequent schedules and greater fractional absorption with alternate-day than consecutive-day administration in the first experiment. Hepcidin is a hormone that limits iron entry into the circulation. The research helps explain why swallowing more or splitting tablets into repeated doses does not guarantee proportionally more usable iron. These were controlled absorption studies in a selected population, and the authors called for confirmation in iron-deficient anemic patients. They are not a personal dosing plan. Symptoms, tolerance, degree of anemia, ongoing losses and the cause of deficiency still govern a clinician's treatment choice and follow-up. [11]

## Monitoring should show that treatment is reaching its goal

The AGA's 2024 Clinical Practice Update links the route of replacement to tolerance, absorption and observed response. It advises considering intravenous iron when oral treatment is not tolerated, ferritin fails to improve during a trial, or absorption is unlikely to be adequate. Conditions after bariatric surgery and active inflammatory bowel disease are examples requiring particular attention. This is expert clinical guidance for management, not evidence that every patient needs an infusion. Readers should understand the planned outcome: better symptoms where attributable to deficiency, correction of anemia when present, replenishment of stores and control of ongoing loss. Failure to improve should lead to reassessment of the diagnosis, treatment delivery and underlying disease. Merely documenting that iron was prescribed does not establish that iron reached the marrow or that the cause of the deficiency was corrected. [12]

## A published case of severe deficiency without visible bleeding

Singhai and colleagues reported a 50-year-old woman with abdominal symptoms and substantial anemia, despite no reported vomiting of blood or black stool. Hemoglobin was 2.4 g/dL, serum iron 4 mcg/dL and transferrin saturation 0.81%, with a high TIBC. Endoscopy identified a large hiatal hernia and a Cameron ulcer, a lesion within the herniated stomach that the team considered responsible for blood loss. She received transfusion, iron replacement and acid suppression. After five days, symptoms improved and hemoglobin had risen to 6.4 g/dL. This was improvement from an extremely low starting concentration, not normalization or proof of a permanent cure. The report does not supply a long-term outcome that would justify such a claim. The case illustrates how iron studies can establish a deficiency pattern while a separate investigation identifies a plausible source, and why an absence of obvious external bleeding cannot exclude substantial chronic loss. [13]

## A published case where absorption changed the investigation

Gasecka and colleagues described a 36-year-old man with an acute myocardial infarction who received a coronary stent but continued to have chest pain, tachycardia and fainting. He had several cardiovascular risk factors, so the case should not be simplified to anemia as the sole cause of his heart attack. Iron was 21 mcg/dL and ferritin 6 ng/mL; hemoglobin later fell to 6.5 g/dL despite prior oral iron. Further investigation found celiac disease, an immune disorder damaging the intestinal lining and impairing absorption. Care included transfusion, intravenous iron, a gluten-free diet and treatment of other illnesses. Follow-up showed correction of anemia and relief of ischemic symptoms. Because several interventions occurred together, one cannot attribute the outcome to diet alone. The lesson is to reconsider absorption and the underlying diagnosis when replacement fails, even when diarrhea and other typical digestive symptoms are not prominent. [14]

## A forensic case shows why a normal ferritin cannot exclude poisoning

Vandoorne reported a fatal case involving ingestion of iron and paracetamol, also called acetaminophen. The woman presented with drowsiness, weakness, severe metabolic acidosis and liver impairment. Ferritin was within that laboratory's reference interval at 58 micrograms/L, while the serum iron test was rejected because the specimen quantity was insufficient. She received hospital treatment including chelation and treatment for paracetamol toxicity but died about 24 hours after admission. The author-repository case summary provides these facts; it does not supply a measured serum iron concentration that can be reconstructed from ferritin. This mixed exposure also prevents assigning all injury to one substance without qualification. The example concerns acute toxicology, a different question from measuring chronic iron stores. It demonstrates that a reassuring-looking storage marker and an unavailable key assay cannot exclude severe acute poisoning, and that missing results must remain explicitly missing in a forensic account. [15]

## A forensic death can reveal an unexpected route of chronic loss

Nara and colleagues described a man in his late seventies who died outdoors in winter. Their history and autopsy investigation attributed lethal hypothermia to severe iron-deficiency anemia associated with prolonged blood-sucking body lice infestation. The published abstract links the infestation, anemia and fatal vulnerability to cold. It does not report a premortem ferritin, transferrin saturation or a documented opportunity when a particular panel could have prevented death. Those details should not be invented. The broader forensic lesson is that nutritional and blood-loss mechanisms must be investigated in the person's actual circumstances, including living conditions and access to care. This unusual report does not establish that ordinary lice infestation generally causes fatal anemia. It shows how an apparently environmental death may involve an underlying chronic medical problem that changes the interpretation of the scene. [16]

## Blood donation separates feeling well from having full stores

The INTERVAL randomized trial studied approximately 45,000 whole-blood donors assigned to different donation frequencies. More frequent donation collected more blood over two years, but also produced lower average hemoglobin and ferritin, more low-hemoglobin deferrals and more donation-related symptoms. Major differences in overall quality of life, activity or cognitive function were not demonstrated over that period. This combination is informative: a person can function well while iron stores become progressively depleted. A normal eligibility check or the absence of dramatic symptoms should not be mistaken for a direct measurement of reserves. The findings concern the trial's donation schedules and population, not permission to choose a personal interval outside a blood service's rules. Donation history belongs in an iron assessment because it supplies a measurable source of iron loss that may otherwise be omitted from the medical history. [17]

## Urgency follows the illness and evidence of active bleeding

An outpatient low ferritin usually calls for a planned assessment of cause and replacement. Active bleeding with signs of deterioration is a different situation. NHS guidance says vomiting blood always requires medical help, and directs emergency assessment when it accompanies features such as faintness, confusion, rapid or shallow breathing, cold clammy skin, abdominal pain or black stool. In such circumstances, waiting to obtain a complete iron panel can delay assessment of the immediate problem. Blood loss and circulatory compromise require attention on their own evidence. The iron studies may later help explain a chronic component, but they cannot grade a person's stability from a distance. Keeping those time scales separate allows appropriate urgency without turning every mildly abnormal storage result into an emergency or allowing a previous normal result to overrule new dangerous symptoms. [18]

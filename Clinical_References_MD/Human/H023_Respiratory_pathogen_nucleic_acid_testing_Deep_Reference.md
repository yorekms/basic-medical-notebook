# H023: Respiratory pathogen nucleic acid testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and molecular diagnostics
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a positive respiratory PCR establish when the same signal might represent a new infection, lingering genetic material or an unexpected laboratory finding?*

## The clinical question defines the test

Respiratory nucleic acid testing searches for selected genetic material from organisms in an airway specimen. Nucleic acids are DNA and RNA, the molecules carrying genetic instructions. Finding a target can identify an infection that changes treatment, precautions or an outbreak investigation. It does not by itself explain every cough or measure lung damage. CDC describes multiplex testing for influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, abbreviated RSV, and SARS CoV 2, the virus causing COVID 19, together because their illnesses overlap. The clinician starts with the person's condition, exposure history and the decision a result could change. [25]

The name respiratory panel is not a universal inventory. Mayo's nasopharyngeal panel includes common viruses and selected bacteria, but its ordering guidance recommends more targeted tests for otherwise healthy people likely to have a mild, self limited illness. An extensive panel can be useful in a seriously ill or vulnerable patient without being necessary for every cold. Its target list and specimen approval should be read literally. A report combining rhinovirus and enterovirus does not identify which of those related viruses is present. A broad panel also does not replace investigations for organisms outside its list, or establish a noninfectious explanation such as asthma. [2]

## The specimen has an address and a date

An anterior nasal swab samples the front of the nose; a nasopharyngeal swab reaches the region behind it. Saliva, throat material, sputum and specimens from deeper airways are different materials. A test validated for one is not automatically validated for all the others. CDC emphasizes matching collection to the manufacturer's instructions and the laboratory's acceptance rules. Correct identity, collection site and time belong with the specimen. Poor sampling can leave too little material for a reliable answer even when a sophisticated instrument works perfectly. Nasopharyngeal collection and invasive lower airway sampling are professional procedures, rather than home alternatives to an approved self collection kit. [4]

Timing changes how much target is available. Influenza specimens generally give the best yield when obtained early in illness, while lower respiratory material can remain informative in severe lung disease after upper airway detection declines. CDC also stresses approved collection materials, transport and storage. Excessive delay or unsuitable handling can reduce detection; the laboratory's instructions should govern refrigeration or other storage rather than a rule borrowed from another assay. Antigen tests recognize viral proteins, while molecular tests recognize nucleic acid and are generally more sensitive. A rapid result can therefore come from either method. Speed alone does not identify the technology or its limitations. [5]

## How a small genetic signal becomes detectable

PCR means polymerase chain reaction. After extraction releases and purifies genetic material, short DNA guides called primers select a target sequence for repeated copying. RNA viruses require reverse transcription, conversion of RNA into DNA, giving the name RT PCR. Multiplex testing follows several targets within one system. Some platforms use successive amplification stages and identify products through their melting characteristics. The BIOFIRE RP2.1 instructions describe a particular closed pouch system; its operation should not be assumed for every molecular test. Its usual output is a qualitative finding, detected or not detected, rather than a count of infectious particles. [3]

That system includes an RNA process control carried through extraction and amplification, plus a separate amplification control. Failed controls can invalidate the run and require laboratory investigation. A passing control supports the analytical process; it cannot prove the original swab reached the right place or contained the patient's pathogen. An invalid report therefore is not a negative report. [3]

## Performance numbers belong to a study population

Leber and colleagues evaluated an earlier panel, RP2, using 1,612 prospective nasopharyngeal specimens. At least one target was detected in 1,020. Most specimens came from children, and many came from emergency or inpatient care. This is a detection frequency in that selected population, not the chance that an average person has a respiratory infection. Performance was assessed target by target against comparison methods, with additional investigation of disagreements. Rare targets provided less clinical evidence than common ones. A high overall agreement can conceal uncertainty for a seldom encountered organism; the report's exact target and validation matter more than a single advertised percentage for the whole panel. [8]

## A negative result does not examine the whole lung

When symptoms persist despite a negative result, the question is why. The target may be absent, below the detection limit, outside the panel, or missed because of site or timing. Coinfection also occurs: a positive influenza test does not exclude COVID 19. Current CDC guidance specifically recommends lower respiratory nucleic acid testing for critically ill, mechanically ventilated patients suspected of influenza or COVID 19 whose diagnosis remains unconfirmed, including after negative upper specimens. Clinicians can use material obtained through the patient's existing airway care when appropriate. This does not mean everyone with a negative nose swab needs bronchoscopy. The potential information must justify the procedure and fit the laboratory's validated specimen types. For hospitalized patients with suspected influenza, CDC also advises starting indicated antiviral treatment promptly without waiting for test results. [1]

A Massachusetts General Hospital study illustrates the difference. After an initial negative result, 13 of 669 repeated lower respiratory tests were positive, compared with 67 of 6,484 repeated upper respiratory tests. These denominators are tests, not independent patients or universal sensitivity estimates. The 13 people diagnosed through lower specimens had compatible symptoms and abnormal chest radiographs. Testing was selected by clinical suspicion, so the groups were not interchangeable randomized samples. The finding supports reconsidering the anatomical site when pneumonia remains plausible. It does not show that a routine negative nasal test commonly misses infection in everyone, or provide individual treatment outcomes for all those detected. [20]

## Cycle threshold is not a portable infectiousness score

Some real time PCR instruments record a cycle threshold, abbreviated Ct: the amplification cycle when a signal crosses the assay's decision threshold. In the same method under comparable conditions, a lower value usually reflects more starting target. Collection quality, specimen volume, extraction, target design and instrument settings also affect it. A Ct of 30 on one platform cannot simply be equated with 30 on another. The Infectious Diseases Society of America, or IDSA, concludes that nonstandardized signal values such as Ct are insufficient alone to establish SARS CoV 2 infectiousness. Its guideline also discourages automatic repeat molecular testing after every initial negative result, while allowing repeat investigation when timing, collection or evolving illness warrants it. [7]

## A positive signal can outlast the infectious episode

CDC notes that SARS CoV 2 RNA can remain detectable for up to 90 days after a positive test and advises against using NAATs to retest people who tested positive during that interval. NAAT means nucleic acid amplification test, the broader family that includes PCR and other amplification methods. Reinfection and persistent detection must be considered through the clinical testing pathway rather than by declaring every later positive a new episode. Antibody testing answers a different question about the immune response and is not a substitute for acute respiratory detection. A repeatedly positive genetic test should not become an unsupervised requirement to keep testing until every signal disappears. [6]

In an early German study, Wolfel and colleagues followed nine hospitalized patients with generally mild COVID 19. Respiratory RNA sometimes persisted after symptoms resolved, but their attempts to recover infectious virus were unsuccessful in specimens obtained after day eight of symptoms. Growing virus in suitable cells supplies evidence of viable virus under those laboratory conditions; PCR supplies evidence of its genetic material. The divergence is the useful observation. Nine patients infected early in the pandemic cannot define a safe day or Ct threshold for every variant, illness severity or immune condition. Failure to grow virus also depends on specimen quality and culture conditions, rather than measuring every possible transmission event. [9]

## Impaired immunity can change the interpretation

Avanzato and colleagues described a woman with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and reduced antibody levels who remained without COVID 19 symptoms despite prolonged detection. Infectious virus was recovered from nasopharyngeal specimens collected 49 and 70 days after her initial positive test; viral RNA was detected up to day 105. After a second convalescent plasma treatment, RNA eventually became undetectable, but one case cannot establish that the treatment caused clearance. The important outcome was documented prolonged viable virus shedding in an immunocompromised person, not RNA persistence alone. Such patients may need specialist infection control assessment. Conversely, this exceptional course should not be projected onto every otherwise healthy person with a late positive PCR. [10]

## RSV detection depends on age and specimen

RSV can cause bronchiolitis in infants, an illness affecting small airways, and serious lower respiratory illness in older adults or people with vulnerable hearts, lungs or immunity. CDC describes molecular assays as highly sensitive and antigen assays as generally less sensitive. The assay instructions determine acceptable specimens and whether the result distinguishes RSV types. A positive result can support precautions and clinical assessment, but it does not directly measure breathing effort, oxygenation or the need for hospital care. These require examination and monitoring. A negative antigen result and a negative well collected molecular specimen should not be treated as equivalent pieces of evidence. [14]

The Multispecimen Study's final analysis included 3,669 hospitalized adults with a valid study nasopharyngeal specimen and at least one additional study specimen. Adding sputum, saliva or paired blood antibody testing detected RSV infections missed by the nasopharyngeal specimen alone. Paired serology compares an acute blood sample with a later sample for a changing immune response; it can improve research ascertainment while arriving too late for an immediate bedside decision. Not every participant supplied every specimen, and the study population was largely older with substantial underlying illness. Its results support awareness of underdetection in adults, rather than automatically ordering every specimen for every infant or outpatient. [15]

## Finding an organism and assigning symptoms are separate tasks

Rhinovirus is a frequent cause of colds, but a later detection needs a timeline. Loeffelholz and colleagues followed infants through their first year and used viral genetic typing to distinguish repeated detection of the same strain from a new strain. Among 179 evaluable infections, eight showed the same strain for more than 30 days. This was uncommon in that otherwise healthy infant population. The observation explains why two positive rhinovirus results are not enough to decide whether one infection persisted: a new infection can intervene. It also shows why symptoms, interval and immune status belong beside the molecular result. Genetic typing in a research study is more detailed than a routine combined rhinovirus enterovirus report. [16]

Spuesens and colleagues enrolled 405 asymptomatic and 321 symptomatic children in a Dutch observational study. Mycoplasma pneumoniae DNA was found in 21.2 percent of tested asymptomatic children and 16.2 percent of tested symptomatic children; the difference was not statistically significant. Culture, quantitative PCR and antibody tests did not reliably separate carriage from symptomatic infection in this population. Carriage means an organism is present without establishing it as the cause of the current illness. This does not make Mycoplasma harmless or PCR useless. It means a detected upper airway target cannot alone prove that a child's pneumonia, wheezing or fever is attributable to that bacterium, and that these older local percentages are not universal current prevalence estimates. [13]

## A target can be shared or introduced by contamination

Pertussis testing demonstrates why sequence choice matters. Bordetella pertussis causes whooping cough, but a commonly used repeated DNA target, IS481, also occurs in some related Bordetella species. Multiple targets can improve specificity. CDC advises testing people with compatible symptoms and emphasizes the first three weeks of cough as the period of best PCR sensitivity. Inappropriate swabs and late sampling can reduce detection. DNA from environmental contamination can also produce a misleading signal, including residual bacterial DNA associated with vaccine preparation areas. That is a specimen integrity issue, not evidence that vaccination caused pertussis. Laboratories interpret weak or equivocal signals alongside clinical and epidemiological information. [17]

In a New Hampshire hospital investigation in 2006, 134 personnel met a suspected pertussis definition: 98 through positive or equivocal PCR and 36 through symptoms alone. None of 192 specimens submitted for culture yielded B pertussis. Retesting 111 available DNA extracts at CDC found only one positive for both confirmatory targets. The combined laboratory, clinical and community findings argued against pertussis as the main cause of the apparent outbreak. Nevertheless, 978 ill personnel had been tested, treated and furloughed pending negative results, and 1,311 contacts received preventive antibiotics. This was a costly diagnostic and public health outcome. The investigation does not show all PCR platforms are unreliable; it shows the need to verify a surprising cluster through independent evidence before attributing every illness to one target. [18]

## Lower respiratory panels and bacterial culture have different jobs

A pneumonia panel is a separate assay for lower airway material, with its own bacterial targets and reporting rules. The original United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, assessment of the FilmArray Pneumonia Panel describes approximate bacterial DNA quantity categories. These are genetic copies per milliliter, not colony forming units, which count organisms capable of producing colonies in culture. Detection may reflect colonizing airway bacteria rather than the cause of pneumonia. A resistance gene can inform investigation, but its absence does not prove susceptibility to a drug, and its presence may not identify which organism carries it in a mixed sample. Culture remains important for isolates, additional identification and susceptibility testing. [19]

The FDA assessment also emphasizes prevalence, how common the target is in the tested population. Even a highly specific assay can produce a false positive, and an unexpected positive during low circulation deserves careful interpretation. This changes the probability that a result reflects a true finding; it does not mean all positives should be dismissed whenever an organism is uncommon. [19]

Legionella often needs a specific request. CDC recommends a urinary antigen test paired with culture or molecular testing of a lower respiratory specimen. The usual urine assay detects Legionella pneumophila serogroup one, whereas suitable respiratory methods can detect a wider range. Culture can produce isolates for comparison with environmental samples during an outbreak. PCR may still detect the target after antibiotics have reduced growth. Collecting useful specimens should not delay necessary treatment. A negative ordinary nasal viral panel therefore cannot exclude Legionnaires' disease, and a positive diagnostic test alone cannot prove which building's water system was responsible. Exposure history, environmental findings and strain comparison may be needed. [22]

## Changing viruses can change assay performance

A mutation is a change in genetic sequence. If it occurs where an assay's primers or detection probes recognize the virus, performance can decline. FDA notes that tests targeting several regions are generally more resilient than tests targeting one region, although this is not a guarantee against every problem. When suspicion remains high, the laboratory may consider a different assay with different genetic targets. Routine SARS CoV 2 detection usually does not identify a variant by name. An unusual pattern can prompt further investigation, while sequencing, reading a longer genetic sequence, provides a different level of characterization. Clinical detection, variant surveillance and proof of transmission should remain distinct conclusions. [21]

## Faster answers help only when care responds

The ResPOC randomized trial enrolled 720 adults presenting to a UK hospital with acute respiratory illness. Its main analysis included 360 assigned rapid molecular testing and 354 assigned usual care. Antibiotics were given to 301 of 360 and 294 of 354, respectively, so testing did not reduce the proportion receiving them. Among antibiotic recipients, however, single doses or courses shorter than 48 hours occurred in 50 of 301 versus 26 of 294. Appropriate influenza antiviral use was also higher with rapid testing. The trial distinguishes a useful change in some treatment decisions from a claim that viral detection automatically eliminates antibiotics. Clinicians still considered bacterial disease and many treatment decisions preceded results. [11]

FluPOC studied 613 hospitalized adults during two influenza seasons, allocating 307 to rapid molecular testing and 306 to routine care. Influenza was identified by study assessment in 100 and 102 participants, respectively. All 100 influenza cases in the rapid group were recognized during care, compared with 60 of 102 in routine care. Antivirals reached 99 of 100 versus 63 of 102, and median time to treatment was one versus six hours. These are documented diagnosis and treatment outcomes from a test and care strategy. They do not establish a mortality benefit for every panel or a universal six hour treatment deadline. The practical lesson is to connect a result promptly to a clinical response. [12]

## Forensic diagnosis requires more than a positive swab

Edler and colleagues examined 80 consecutive deceased people with SARS CoV 2 infection in Hamburg. Most deaths were classified within categories involving COVID 19, with differing certainty or competing causes; four were considered unrelated to the infection. The latter included cardiac deaths and sepsis from necrotizing fasciitis, a destructive deep soft tissue infection; three of those four infections were first detected postmortem. Autopsy findings, imaging and medical history supplied the causal assessment that PCR alone could not. This selected group of deaths cannot estimate the fatality risk among infected people. Nor can its classification be transferred unchanged to another death. It provides a direct forensic example of why detecting viral RNA, establishing tissue injury and deciding the cause of death are related but different evidentiary steps. [24]

## Urgency comes from the person as well as the pathogen

Difficulty breathing, persistent chest pressure, new confusion or inability to wake normally warrant emergency assessment regardless of a pending or negative panel. In children, fast or labored breathing, bluish lips, poor alertness or dehydration are warning signs. CDC advises prompt medical attention for these changes. People at higher risk who develop a respiratory illness should also contact their clinical team early because treatment opportunities can be time sensitive. There is no single testing deadline that covers every organism or every patient. The useful result records the test, target, collection site and date, and is interpreted alongside current illness. A reassuring laboratory word must never override obvious deterioration. [23]

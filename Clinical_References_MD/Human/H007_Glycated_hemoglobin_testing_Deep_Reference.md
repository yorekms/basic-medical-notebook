# H007: Glycated hemoglobin testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Endocrinology and glucose metabolism
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 2)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft; clinical review pending. Human evidence and decision limits must not be transferred to animals. Published cases and hypothetical examples are explicitly separated.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can a blood test remember months of glucose exposure yet give false reassurance to someone whose glucose is repeatedly high?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Routine assessment with emergency exceptions

Seek emergency care now for persistent vomiting with deep or difficult breathing or altered consciousness in possible diabetes; do not wait for HbA1c.

## A memory carried by circulating cells

Hemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein inside red blood cells. Glucose attaches to it as those cells circulate; the resulting glycated fraction is called HbA1c, A1C, or glycated hemoglobin. This is a chemical record of exposure rather than a measurement of glucose at the instant the needle enters the vein. The usual description, an average over about three months, is useful but incomplete: recent weeks contribute more than earlier weeks. A meal immediately before collection does not invalidate an isolated HbA1c test, and fasting is ordinarily unnecessary for that test alone. Conversely, one recent week of better glucose readings cannot erase the earlier exposure represented by the existing red-cell population. The question being asked is therefore about a time window, not simply whether today's blood sugar is high. It is essential to identify that question before comparing HbA1c with a fasting laboratory glucose or a home meter reading. [1]

## From a person to a usable specimen

The routine specimen is whole blood because the information is inside cells, not just in the surrounding liquid. Mayo Clinic Laboratories' HBA1C service, for example, specifies EDTA whole blood; EDTA is an anticoagulant that prevents the sample from clotting. Its collection, transport, and stability requirements belong to that particular service and should not be substituted for another laboratory's instructions. The patient contributes an equally important part of the specimen history: recent bleeding, transfusion, a blood disorder, and a major recent change in glucose control can alter the meaning of the number. At the laboratory, identification, acceptable storage, and suitability must be checked before analysis. The sample then enters a method designed to distinguish glycated from other hemoglobin. This service uses ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography, abbreviated HPLC, a separation method; the report's method name is useful information when an unexpected result needs investigation. [2]

## Watching a separation become a number

A different bench design illustrates why the name HbA1c does not identify one instrument. The CDC's NHANES Premier Hb9210 procedure uses boronate-affinity HPLC. A boronate-containing column selectively retains glycated hemoglobin because the attached sugar supplies chemical groups that can bind the column material. Nonglycated material passes through first; another reagent releases the retained glycated material. A light detector records the separated components, and the software integrates the areas under the resulting peaks. The result is calibrated to the HbA1c reporting system rather than being an unqualified measurement of every sugar attachment in blood. Reviewing the chromatogram, the plotted detector signal, and quality-control results is part of producing a credible result. This example explains an analytical principle, not a universal laboratory recipe: ion-exchange, affinity, immunochemical, and enzymatic services require their own validated procedures and checks. [3]

## Worked example converting the reporting units

Hypothetical worked example, not a reported patient: one laboratory reports 53 mmol/mol using the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry system, while a prior report uses percent in the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program, or NGSP, system. Its master equation is percent = 0.09148 × IFCC mmol/mol + 2.152. Substitution gives 0.09148 × 53 + 2.152 = 7.00044%, conventionally rounded to 7.0%. Thus 53 mmol/mol and 7.0% describe essentially the same HbA1c result; they are not two different degrees of glucose exposure. The mmol/mol unit here refers to a fraction of hemoglobin, not a glucose concentration. NGSP links results to the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial system, while the IFCC network uses higher-order reference methods and purified calibrators. Converting units can reconcile reports, but cannot correct a biological or analytical distortion already present in the measurement. [4]

## A decision limit answers a defined clinical question

For nonpregnant individuals, ADA's 2026 diagnostic criteria include laboratory HbA1c at or above 6.5%, equivalent to 48 mmol/mol, using a standardized method. This is a diagnostic decision limit, not a universal treatment target or an emergency threshold. In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, diagnosis requires confirmation with a second abnormal result, obtained by repeating the test or using another accepted diagnostic test as appropriate. A result just across the boundary should therefore trigger a confirmation plan and review of possible distortion. ADA also restricts point-of-care diagnostic use to appropriately approved devices and qualified testing settings; an office or home device cannot be assumed suitable merely because it displays an HbA1c. When red-cell turnover or another condition alters the relation between HbA1c and glucose, plasma glucose criteria should be used for diagnosis. HbA1c alone does not determine the underlying diabetes type. [5]

## When the cells change the apparent memory

A perfectly functioning analyzer can produce a misleading estimate of glucose exposure when circulating cells have an unusual age distribution. Hemolysis, premature destruction of red cells, or recovery after substantial blood loss can lower HbA1c because younger cells have had less time to acquire glucose. Changing to another instrument does not restore the missing exposure time. Significant iron-deficiency anemia can instead raise HbA1c, and iron replacement can lower it without that fall necessarily representing improved glucose control. These effects make the direction and timing of a change important. The relevant question is not merely whether the hemoglobin concentration is normal, but whether cell production, survival, or replacement has changed. A transfusion history and evidence of increased cell turnover therefore belong beside the glucose history. Apparent improvement should be checked against direct glucose observations when the blood-cell population is changing. [6]

## When the method changes the answer

Hemoglobin variants create a different problem: the molecular form of hemoglobin may interact differently with a particular assay. NGSP's interference table identifies methods by manufacturer and platform, including software versions for some instruments, rather than declaring all HbA1c assays equally affected. A person with sickle cell trait may have a valid result on a suitable method and a misleading result on another. Elevated fetal hemoglobin can also matter. The first practical investigation is to ask which method produced the report and whether that exact method has a documented interference with the suspected variant. A generic statement that HPLC is accurate, or that immunoassay avoids every variant, is inadequate. Method interference and shortened cell survival can coexist; resolving one does not prove that the other is absent. The laboratory should help distinguish a distorted signal from an accurately measured fraction whose biological interpretation is unreliable. [7]

## Published case a low result exposes hemolysis

Finan and Joseph's 2018 report, DOI 10.1136/bcr-2018-227668, describes a 37-year-old woman whose type 2 diabetes had been diagnosed from high glucose despite repeatedly low HbA1c. At referral HbA1c was 19 mmol/mol, hemoglobin 12.2 g/dL, and reticulocytes, young red cells, 11.9%. Low haptoglobin, a protein consumed during hemolysis, and spherocytes, unusually spherical red cells on the blood film, led to investigation for hereditary spherocytosis. An eosin-5-maleimide binding test supported that diagnosis. Her hemolysis was compensated: increased production maintained the hemoglobin concentration despite shortened cell survival. She was already taking metformin for diabetes; the mild blood disorder was considered unlikely to require specific intervention. The reported follow-up was an arrangement to screen her children and sibling. The paper does not provide a later diabetes outcome. This single case demonstrates a mechanism for false reassurance, not its frequency or a correction formula for all patients. [8]

## Worked example estimating average glucose

Hypothetical worked example, not a reported patient: an HbA1c of 7.0% is inserted into the ADAG study's equation, estimated average glucose in mg/dL = 28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7. The arithmetic gives 154.2 mg/dL, usually displayed as about 154. This is a population-based estimate, not a second glucose measurement. Nathan and colleagues' 2008 study, DOI 10.2337/dc08-0545, analyzed 507 participants with adequate HbA1c and glucose data over three months, including people with type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and no diabetes. Its relationship had substantial explanatory power but also individual prediction error. The participants generally had stable glycemia, and predefined conditions such as anemia excluded some people. Therefore the calculated estimate should not be forced to match every meter reading or treated as validated during a disorder that changes red-cell survival. It also cannot reveal whether that average arose from steady glucose or alternating highs and lows. [9]

## Why the same average can conceal different risks

An HbA1c result compresses a glucose history into one fraction. It cannot show the daily timing of high values, nocturnal low values, or the spread around the average. Continuous glucose monitoring, CGM, samples glucose repeatedly in interstitial fluid and can reveal patterns that HbA1c conceals; capillary blood glucose monitoring provides another complementary perspective. ADA's 2026 monitoring guidance therefore uses these observations together rather than treating HbA1c as sufficient for every decision. A diagnostic boundary and a treatment goal serve different purposes. Goals should account for expected benefit, hypoglycemia risk, functional and cognitive status, other illnesses, resources, and treatment burden. A lower HbA1c is not automatically safer if it accompanies recurrent dangerous hypoglycemia. Likewise, an apparently satisfactory average does not establish that every part of the day is safe. The clinically useful discussion asks both how much exposure occurred and how it was distributed. [10]

## Kidney disease changes the confidence in the number

Chronic kidney disease adds several interacting sources of uncertainty. KDIGO recommends HbA1c for long-term monitoring but notes declining accuracy and precision in advanced disease, especially during dialysis. Anemia, altered cell survival, transfusions, iron treatment, and medicines that stimulate red-cell production can shift the relation to actual glucose. These are not all solved by eliminating an analytical interference from chemically modified hemoglobin. When HbA1c and directly observed glucose or symptoms disagree, CGM-derived information can help assess glycemia. A glucose management indicator, GMI, is an estimate derived from CGM data; it should not be renamed a laboratory HbA1c or expected to match it exactly. KDIGO also emphasizes individualized goals and the value of daily glucose information when hypoglycemia is a concern. An unexpected number in kidney failure calls for reconciliation with the clinical history and monitoring record, rather than a reflex assumption that either the patient or the device must be wrong. [11]

## Published trial what improving exposure accomplished

The 1993 Diabetes Control and Complications Trial, DOI 10.1056/NEJM199309303291401, randomly assigned 1,441 people with insulin-dependent diabetes, now generally termed type 1 diabetes, to intensive or conventional insulin treatment. Its published abstract reports delayed onset and slower progression of diabetic eye, kidney, and nerve complications with intensive therapy. This is important outcome evidence behind measuring long-term glycemic exposure; HbA1c is clinically useful because it connects an otherwise invisible exposure history with risks that matter to people. The trial also reported a two-to-threefold increase in severe hypoglycemia with the intensive strategies used then. Those findings support balancing benefit and harm, not pursuing the lowest possible number in every population. Randomization tested treatment strategies, not the isolated act of ordering HbA1c. The accessible primary abstract supports these trial facts; this account does not reconstruct individual participants, modern-device performance, or unreported long-term outcomes from it. [12]

## Published forensic case the clue and the missing measurement

Byard and Gilbert's report, DOI 10.1007/s12024-024-00815-6, appeared online in 2024 and in the 2025 journal volume. A 19-year-old man was found dead after weeks of illness and vomiting without seeking medical attention. Autopsy identified obesity, velvety thickening of skin folds called acanthosis nigricans, and aspiration of gastric contents. Vitreous humor, the eye's internal fluid, contained glucose 62.9 mmol/L and beta-hydroxybutyrate, a ketone, 13.54 mmol/L. The authors attributed death to aspiration pneumonia complicating diabetic ketoacidosis on a background of severe obesity. No treatment occurred before discovery, and HbA1c was not reported. The case therefore illustrates a clue that prompted investigation of occult disease, not a demonstrated HbA1c result. Its postmortem fluid measurements must not be compared with living-person diagnostic limits as though the specimens were interchangeable. Neither the skin appearance nor this single fatal outcome establishes an inevitable progression, the diabetes type, or that one earlier test would have prevented death. [13]

## The emergency question belongs to the present

Diabetic ketoacidosis, DKA, combines increased ketones and metabolic acidosis with diabetes or hyperglycemia; hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state involves severe hyperglycemia, dehydration, and increased blood concentration of dissolved particles. These acute syndromes require current clinical assessment, glucose, electrolytes, ketones, and acid-base testing, rather than waiting for a long-term exposure marker. The 2024 international consensus also recognizes DKA with relatively low glucose, particularly in relevant medication contexts such as SGLT2 inhibitor use. A recent reassuring HbA1c cannot exclude such a crisis. Persistent vomiting with deep or difficult breathing, marked dehydration, or altered consciousness in someone with possible diabetes warrants emergency care now. Clinicians assess and treat the precipitating illness as well as the metabolic disturbance. The fact that HbA1c is routinely ordered in stable outpatient care does not make every elevated result an emergency; urgency comes from the person's current condition and accompanying measurements. [14]

## Other proteins can answer a shorter term question

When red-cell biology undermines HbA1c, fructosamine or glycated albumin may help monitor glucose exposure. Fructosamine measures glycated serum proteins, whereas glycated albumin focuses on albumin. Their shorter protein turnover gives them a shorter observation window than HbA1c; they are not simply alternative units for the same measurement. The AACC and ADA laboratory guideline supports considering these markers when hemoglobin variants or abnormal cell turnover compromise interpretation, while noting limitations in the evidence linking these alternatives to chronic complications. Choosing a substitute therefore begins with the reason HbA1c is unreliable and the clinical question being asked. Useful explanatory questions include: Is the goal diagnosis or monitoring? Is the disagreement consistent over time? Is an acceptable HbA1c method available for this hemoglobin? Would a different protein marker or direct glucose record answer the unresolved question? An additional result is valuable when it resolves uncertainty, not merely because it adds another number. [15]

## Agreement is more than a high correlation

A small 2024 method-comparison study in Annals of Clinical and Laboratory Science examined 77 clinical samples using Abbott Alinity c and Premier Hb9210 assays. The results were highly correlated, but the authors still found negative bias with Alinity c at clinically relevant decision points. The paper's title calls the Alinity c method an immunoassay, while its methods describe an enzymatic assay; that inconsistency is a reason to be cautious about repeating its method label. Its limited experiment does not establish a universal correction or superiority of every HPLC platform. It does illustrate a general measurement lesson: correlation describes whether results rise together, while agreement asks whether the actual values are close enough for the decision. Near a boundary, even a small systematic difference may matter. The report should therefore be read with its units, laboratory method, prior results, and clinical context, with unexplained discordance discussed with the performing laboratory. [16]

# H019: Stool pathogen testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and parasitology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can a stool test establish about diarrhea when finding a germ and proving the cause are different questions?*

## The question comes before the container

Stool pathogen testing investigates whether bacteria, viruses or parasites may explain an intestinal illness. A pathogen is an organism capable of causing disease, but detecting one does not automatically prove it caused every symptom. The 2017 guideline from the Infectious Diseases Society of America, or IDSA, recommends targeted investigation for features such as bloody diarrhea, fever, severe abdominal tenderness or sepsis, and broader testing during suspected outbreaks. Many short, uncomplicated diarrheal illnesses need no extensive laboratory search. This chapter concerns human testing, mainly in United States practice. The purpose is to explain how a result becomes useful evidence alongside the person's illness and exposures, rather than to provide a treatment recipe. [1]

The phrase stool test hides several different examinations. A test for hidden blood, a marker of intestinal inflammation and a pathogen panel do not answer the same question. Even among infection tests, a routine molecular panel has a defined list of targets. UC Davis separates routine protozoal testing from investigations for worm eggs and larvae in people with appropriate risks. A person who has lived in an endemic area or has impaired immunity may need an investigation that an ordinary panel does not include. A negative report therefore means that the targets sought were not detected under that method's conditions. It does not establish that every infectious or noninfectious cause of diarrhea has been excluded. [23]

## Following the specimen into the laboratory

The collection process can change what reaches the laboratory. The supplied clean, leakproof container helps prevent contamination by toilet water, urine or soil. Collection time, patient identity and the requested examination belong with the sample. CDC's parasitology guidance emphasizes that preservatives are selected for a purpose: material preserved well for microscopic appearance may be unsuitable for a molecular assay. Follow the collecting laboratory's kit instructions rather than adding household chemicals or choosing a preservative independently. Tell the ordering team about recent antimicrobials and other medicines. This information may explain reduced recovery or guide selection of a different test, and should never become a reason to postpone urgent care. [2]

Culture means attempting to grow living bacteria on selected laboratory media. The organisms sought, atmosphere, incubation and follow-up identification depend on the laboratory's service. Texas public health laboratory guidance, for example, lists several enteric bacteria and notes that outbreak investigations can alter the screening approach. Its catalog also explains that improper transport and storage reduce recovery. A culture result may take longer than a molecular panel because growth takes time. Its particular value is a living isolate, an identified bacterial population that can undergo further characterization or antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The label routine culture is not a promise that all organisms causing diarrhea were sought or would grow. [3]

A multiplex molecular panel searches for several genetic targets in one run. PCR, polymerase chain reaction, copies selected DNA sequences until an instrument can detect them; viral RNA targets require conversion into DNA first. A prospective FilmArray study examined 1,556 specimens and detected at least one potential pathogen in 832. Among those positive specimens, 262 contained more than one target. That result illustrates why a long positive list needs interpretation: it does not rank the organisms by responsibility for the illness. The study examined a particular platform, target list and clinical population. Its detection frequency is neither the prevalence of infection in the general public nor the probability that every detected organism caused disease. [4]

## Antigens microscopy and organisms beyond the panel

An antigen is a recognizable component of an organism. Antigen assays use antibodies as molecular recognition tools, sometimes creating a colored signal in a rapid cartridge or a measurable signal in a laboratory plate. Direct fluorescent antibody microscopy attaches a glowing label to selected parasite structures, helping the observer recognize them. CDC describes these approaches for Giardia and Cryptosporidium, while warning that preparation and preservation must match the kit. These methods differ from simply looking for anything parasite-like on a routine slide. Antigen detection does not usually produce a living organism for later culture or a detailed genetic fingerprint. Its clinical usefulness depends on the organism, specimen and validated assay. [5]

Norovirus illustrates why the organism matters when comparing methods. Its genome is RNA. CDC prefers reverse transcription quantitative PCR, usually shortened to RT qPCR, for laboratory detection. Reverse transcription makes DNA from the viral RNA; quantitative PCR then measures the accumulating signal. Some stool panels include common norovirus groups. Rapid antigen enzyme immunoassays exist, but CDC notes their lower sensitivity and advises against replacing molecular testing with them in outbreak investigations. A negative rapid test can therefore leave a different degree of uncertainty than a suitable molecular test. The speed of a visible result alone does not describe how reliably an assay detects its intended target. [6]

Cryptosporidium is a tiny intestinal parasite whose oocysts, its environmentally resistant stages, can be identified by specialized staining, fluorescent antibody testing, antigen methods or PCR. CDC's 2025 guidance explains that shedding is intermittent, so collection over three days may improve detection. A laboratory's general microscopy request may not automatically include every specialized method. Routine diagnostic detection also differs from the additional genetic work needed to distinguish species or subtypes during an epidemiological investigation. This matters when people ask whether an infection came from a swimming pool, another person or an animal. A positive diagnostic test alone rarely supplies that complete chain of evidence. [7]

Giardia also may be absent from the particular bowel movement sampled despite ongoing infection. CDC recommends three stool specimens collected over several days for accurate investigation and recommends retesting after treatment only if symptoms continue. Available methods include fluorescent antibody microscopy, antigen assays and molecular testing. The word cyst describes one transmissible stage, rather than a cyst in the person's tissues. Finding Giardia in stool confirms infection, while the clinician still considers the full explanation for continuing symptoms. Repeated collection in this setting addresses intermittent shedding; it should not be confused with automatically repeating a broad panel every day or testing indefinitely until every result becomes negative. [8]

Cyclospora requires another specific question. CDC's September 2026 guidance notes that not all gastrointestinal panels include it and routine parasite examinations may not detect it unless requested. The oocysts can be sparse and shed intermittently. Molecular methods search for its DNA; special staining and ultraviolet fluorescence provide other recognition methods. Its structures naturally fluoresce under appropriate illumination, unlike the common Cryptosporidium species. Prolonged diarrhea, travel and a suspected food-associated cluster can prompt the clinician to request this additional investigation. A report saying parasite testing negative is incomplete without knowing which organisms and methods it covered. The history can reveal that the right target was never examined. [9]

## C difficile requires a clinical diagnosis

Clostridioides difficile, often shortened to C difficile or C diff, can be carried without causing the current illness. A PCR assay typically detects a gene associated with toxin production; it does not directly measure toxin already present in the stool. A glutamate dehydrogenase, or GDH, antigen test detects a bacterial component that is not limited to toxin-producing strains. A toxin enzyme immunoassay looks for toxin proteins but is less sensitive than molecular detection. CDC also warns that toxin can deteriorate during delayed, unrefrigerated handling. These differences explain why laboratories use combinations of tests, and why a PCR positive but toxin negative report requires assessment rather than an automatic diagnosis or dismissal. [11]

The diagnostic guideline from the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America, or SHEA, and IDSA identifies unexplained, newly occurring diarrhea with at least three unformed stools in 24 hours as the preferred testing population. Laboratories may use a multistep algorithm incorporating toxin, or an appropriate molecular approach when agreed submission criteria select likely cases. Age changes interpretation: routine testing of infants is discouraged because carriage is common. For children aged one to two years, alternative explanations should first be excluded. These boundaries concern who should be tested and how a result should be interpreted; they are not rules for a parent to diagnose or treat a child independently. [12]

Polage and colleagues followed 1,416 hospitalized adults tested at one academic center. Of 293 PCR positive patients, 162 had no toxin detected by the clinical immunoassay. None of those 162 developed the study's C difficile related complications, compared with ten of 131 toxin positive patients. One toxin negative patient had recurrent infection considered a contributor to death; the group was not risk free. Outcomes overall resembled those of test negative patients despite limited treatment. This observational study supports caution about overdiagnosis, but does not prove that every toxin negative patient can safely remain untreated. Disease severity, test limitations and the particular patient's course remain necessary to the decision. [10]

## Multiple positives and repeat results

Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli, or EPEC, is another result whose meaning varies. A 2022 retrospective study of children and adults associated atypical EPEC with presentations ranging from no symptoms to severe or prolonged diarrhea. Some people remained positive for months to years or experienced repeated infection. Higher stool bacterial loads were associated with symptoms, but did not reliably predict the disease or its severity. The word atypical describes a bacterial subgroup, not an unusual patient. A positive EPEC result should therefore be interpreted with symptoms, other detected organisms and competing causes. It should not become an explanation for every subsequent abdominal complaint merely because its name remains in the medical record. [13]

During a 2013 Rhode Island shigellosis outbreak, Prakash and colleagues detected ten Shigella or enteroinvasive E coli targets by panel testing, whereas culture identified six Shigella sonnei cases. Independent PCR and sequencing supported the additional molecular detections. The panel itself could not distinguish Shigella from enteroinvasive E coli, a closely related target group. The investigation demonstrates that culture negative does not always mean the molecular result was false. It also shows why species resolution and recoverable isolates mattered for the public health definitions then in use. Individual recovery outcomes were not the focus of this report, so it cannot show that the extra detections improved each patient's eventual health. [14]

A Stanford retrospective study examined follow-up panel testing within four weeks. Of 80 follow-up tests after an initial positive result, 43 still detected the same target. That is a count of tests, not 43 newly infected people. Most follow-up tests after a negative result remained negative. The authors discouraged routine use of this panel as a test of cure. Persisting genetic detection need not mean continuing viable infection, while new symptoms or a specific concern about poor initial sampling can justify a different decision. This study addressed one platform and practice setting. It does not prohibit organism-specific follow-up requested for public health or a carefully reassessed clinical problem. [15]

For C difficile, the guideline advises against routine repeat testing within seven days during the same episode and against testing asymptomatic people as a test of cure. Persistent positivity can outlast clinical improvement. A new deterioration still needs reassessment of dehydration, complications, medicines or another diagnosis. The question is whether another specimen will resolve a new uncertainty, rather than prolong treatment because a sensitive assay remains positive. [12]

## When delay can change the outcome

Shiga toxin producing E coli, or STEC, changes the immediate clinical question. CDC recommends stool culture for E coli O157 together with testing that detects Shiga toxins or their genes to cover non O157 STEC. The result can affect management because antimicrobial treatment may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome, or HUS, and drugs that slow bowel movement should be avoided in STEC infection. HUS involves injury to small blood vessels, breakdown of red blood cells, low platelets and kidney injury. Clinicians assess hydration and blood tests alongside stool findings. A person with bloody diarrhea should seek medical assessment rather than start leftover antibiotics or wait for a panel result before addressing serious illness. [16]

HUS can develop in someone who has had diarrhea even as attention shifts away from the bowel symptoms. CDC identifies warning signs including little or no urine, marked pallor, unexplained bruising, blood in the urine, extreme tiredness and reduced alertness. These warrant immediate medical care. A stool test identifies evidence of an intestinal organism; blood counts and chemistry assess complications affecting the kidneys and circulation. Neither test replaces the other. This is an appropriate situation for urgency in an educational account: a changing person's condition matters more than a reassuring laboratory result obtained earlier. It does not mean that every episode of loose stool represents an emergency. [17]

The spring 2018 United States outbreak linked to romaine lettuce gives these complications a documented scale. CDC's final notice reported 210 infections across 36 states. Among 201 people with information available, 96 were hospitalized, including 27 who developed HUS; five deaths were reported. Whole genome sequencing showed closely related bacterial isolates, while interviews and traceback evidence implicated romaine from the Yuma growing region. These are the reported outcomes of that particular outbreak, not the risk for every person with STEC. Keeping the denominator visible also matters: the hospitalization proportion used the 201 people with available information, rather than assuming complete follow-up for all 210. [18]

Madden and colleagues reported severe colitis with lower gastrointestinal bleeding in which a multiplex stool PCR detected Entamoeba histolytica. Two conventional stool microscopy examinations were negative. The molecular result was repeated because the finding was unusual locally and remained positive; treatment was followed by a rapid clinical response. At a year of follow-up the patient had no features suggesting inflammatory bowel disease and had not redeveloped anemia. The authors considered the PCR useful in reaching the diagnosis promptly. This is a successful individual outcome, not proof that every positive amoebic target is definitive or that molecular testing alone explains all colitis. Symptoms and response supported the laboratory evidence. [21]

## Forensic evidence and the limits of a microbial match

A forensic report described a previously healthy 32-year-old man who died overnight after profuse vomiting. Postmortem culture of gastric contents yielded abundant pure Salmonella Typhimurium variant Copenhagen. The investigators integrated this with histology, toxicology and scene findings to support fatal infectious gastroenteritis. They proposed severe dehydration, electrolyte disturbance and arrhythmia as the most plausible mechanism, but vitreous was not collected and electrolytes were not measured. The food source remained unidentified. This was gastric sampling after death, not a routine living patient's stool test. It demonstrates both the value of microbiology and the need to distinguish an identified organism, an attributed disease and a proposed final physiological mechanism. [20]

The investigation of the 1984 Oregon salmonellosis outbreak eventually combined clinical illness, stool cultures, exposure comparisons and criminal evidence. Torok and colleagues reported 751 people meeting clinical or culture-based case criteria; not every one was culture confirmed. A bacterial strain recovered in the subsequent investigation was indistinguishable from the outbreak strain. The forensic conclusion required the broader investigation, rather than arising from a patient's stool culture alone. Likewise, today's positive Salmonella report cannot identify who contaminated food, establish intent or assign responsibility to a restaurant. A laboratory match can support a link while timing, exposure records, alternative sources and independent evidence determine what that link means. [19]

Whole genome sequencing reads bacterial DNA in far more detail than a routine diagnostic panel. PulseNet uses these comparisons to recognize clusters that may share a source and to investigate antimicrobial resistance. Think of the routine result as identifying the kind of organism sought, while sequencing compares much more of its genetic identity. Even close genetic relatedness is interpreted alongside epidemiology. Specimen identity, collection dates, retained isolates and documented transfers allow investigators to assess whether laboratory findings belong together. A clinical test is useful for care; a forensic or regulatory conclusion may require additional evidence and handling records. Those purposes should remain clear when discussing a result with public health investigators. [24]

## What better testing can and cannot deliver

Faster detection is useful only if it changes an appropriate decision. A randomized trial of 60 children with bloody diarrhea compared rapid multiplex testing with standard investigation. Although the technology produced faster, broader information, the investigators did not find that the advances necessarily improved the measured clinical outcomes. The study was small, so it cannot rule out benefits in every subgroup. Its lesson is that an instrument's speed, diagnostic yield and effect on patient health are separate measurements. When reviewing a result, ask what was sought, whether the specimen was suitable, how the finding fits the illness and what action follows. Those questions keep the test connected to the person it is meant to help. [22]

# H028: Cervical cytology and human papillomavirus screening

- **Domain**: Human

- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *{'text': 'An HPV test looks for a viral warning signal; cervical cytology looks for altered cells. Prevention depends on understanding both results and completing the right follow-up.', 'sources': [1]}*

## Two tests with different jobs

The cervix is the lower part of the uterus, opening into the vagina. Cervical screening seeks changes before symptoms develop, especially precancer: abnormal cells that could eventually become invasive cancer. Cervical cytology, commonly called a Pap test, examines collected cells under a microscope. Human papillomavirus testing, abbreviated HPV testing, looks for infection with selected virus types associated with cervical cancer. Cotesting uses both approaches. A screening result estimates whether further investigation is needed; it does not by itself establish that cancer is present or absent everywhere in the reproductive tract. Benefit requires adequate sampling, reliable interpretation, completed follow-up and appropriate treatment. [1]

## Why persistence matters more than one positive result

HPV is a large family of viruses. Types described as high risk can contribute to cancer, while the types commonly causing genital warts are different. Most infections are controlled by the immune system without cancer developing. The important concern is infection with a cancer-associated type that persists over years and produces progressive changes in the cells it infects. A positive high-risk HPV result therefore means that a risk factor was detected, not that malignant cells were found. Conversely, a negative result means that the targeted viral material was not detected in that sample at that time. It greatly reduces risk within an appropriate screening programme, but cannot supply a lifetime guarantee. [2]

## What the laboratory does with cervical cells

With liquid-based cytology, a clinician-collected cervical sample goes into a preservative, and the laboratory prepares a thin layer of cells on glass for assessment. The observer examines cell shape, nuclear appearance and arrangement, looking for patterns of abnormal growth. Cells from the cervix are important: a vaginal sample validated for molecular HPV testing does not automatically provide an adequate cervical cytology preparation. Laboratories also check identification, the collection material, sample quality and the compatibility of the sample with the chosen test. England’s National Health Service laboratory guidance, updated in August 2026, uses HPV testing first and cytology to triage appropriate HPV-positive cervical samples. Other programmes can use a different sequence. [3]

## What a molecular HPV result measures

Molecular assays detect viral genetic material rather than antibodies in blood. For example, the United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, describes the cobas HPV assay as a laboratory test detecting DNA from fourteen high-risk HPV types. Its software can produce positive, negative or invalid results. The approved collection device, transport material and testing platform form a validated combination; exchanging a swab or fluid can change performance. In May 2024, the FDA expanded this assay’s indication to include specified self-collected vaginal specimens obtained in a healthcare setting when a cervical sample cannot be obtained. That particular authorization did not itself authorize every home collection kit or turn a vaginal sample into a Pap test. [4]

## Reading the language on the report

A Pap report reading negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy, abbreviated NILM, means that the examined cells show no such abnormality. It does not mean that an accompanying HPV test must also be negative. Atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance, or ASC-US, describes uncertain changes in the flat surface cells. Low-grade and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions, LSIL and HSIL, describe increasingly concerning cellular patterns, but are cytology categories rather than a cancer stage. Atypical glandular cells, AGC, concern the mucus-producing glandular cells and need their own assessment. The laboratory wording, HPV type if reported, age and previous results should be interpreted together; the word “abnormal” alone cannot determine the next step. [5]

## An unsatisfactory sample needs an answer

An unsatisfactory cytology result means there was insufficient usable material for a reliable cellular assessment. Too few cells, blood, inflammation, atrophy or preparation problems can contribute. This is a sampling limitation rather than a reassuring normal result. The American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, abbreviated ASCCP, updated its advice: with unsatisfactory cytology and absent, unknown or negative HPV results, repeat age-appropriate screening as soon as convenient and no later than four months. A negative HPV result obtained alongside the unsatisfactory cytology should not be treated as valid reassurance in that situation; both tests are repeated when applicable. Another unsatisfactory cytology result calls for colposcopy. Symptoms still require their own investigation while sampling is resolved. [6]

## Missing transformation-zone cells is a different issue

The transformation zone is the region where one cervical cell type meets and is replaced by another, and where many precancers arise. A report may state that endocervical or transformation-zone cells were absent even though the cytology is otherwise satisfactory. That is not the same finding as an unsatisfactory sample. Age, pregnancy and other biological factors influence which cells appear in a specimen. ASCCP notes that absence of these cells alone has not been shown to increase the subsequent risk of high-grade disease. Management of an otherwise negative cytology result depends on age and HPV findings. Automatically repeating every such specimen immediately can therefore add testing without following the actual risk-based recommendation. [7]

## Follow-up uses the whole history

ASCCP’s risk-based framework combines the current result with previous HPV tests, cytology, biopsy findings and treatment history. Two people with the same minor abnormality may appropriately receive different recommendations because one has a recent reassuring HPV test and the other has persistent infection or previous high-grade disease. Possible actions include scheduled repeat testing, colposcopy, or treatment after sufficiently concerning findings. A request to return for surveillance means that a defined interval is part of active care, not that the result was ignored. Conversely, a general screening interval should never replace the closer surveillance required after an abnormal result. Bring earlier reports when changing clinicians, because an undocumented history is different from a documented negative history. [8]

## Self-collection can change access, but the pathway matters

Home collection is now a real United States option within authorized pathways. The FDA granted the Teal Wand De Novo request on 9 May 2025 for home collection and transport of vaginal specimens for an approved HPV molecular assay. This is a specimen-collection device: the laboratory performs the HPV analysis. It does not inspect cervical cells at home, diagnose all causes of bleeding, or provide a general sexually transmitted infection screen. The approved device and laboratory pathway must be used as labelled, with an identified clinician responsible for ordering, results and follow-up. An internet product described simply as a “home Pap” should not be assumed equivalent to this authorized use. [9]

## A newer home pathway illustrates device-specific validation

The FDA cleared the Onclarity Self-Collection Kit on 2 April 2026. Its decision summary describes a prescription device for self-collection and transport of vaginal specimens from a home or private setting. Performance was validated with the specified BD Onclarity HPV assay, not with every molecular test. These authorizations are examples of available pathways, rather than an exhaustive catalogue of all current devices. Patients and clinicians need to check the particular assay, collection setting, age and other label restrictions when arranging screening. A usable home sample can reduce an access barrier, but an invalid sample still needs resolution and a positive result may require a subsequent clinician-collected cervical sample or examination. [10]

## The SELF-CERV trial: comparable detection is not perfect detection

In the 2025 SELF-CERV clinical validation study, 599 participants from sixteen United States sites contributed to the study analysis comparing a self-collected vaginal sample with clinician collection. Among 48 participants with tissue-confirmed cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade two or worse, CIN2+, self-collection detected HPV in 46; clinician collection also detected 46. Among 29 with grade three or worse, CIN3+, the corresponding detections were 28 and 29. Participants collected in a simulated home environment, and the study was not a trial of long-term cancer prevention. The manufacturer-supported study supports the particular validated collection method while preserving its limits: neither “equivalent” nor high sensitivity means every affected person is detected. [11]

## What happens after a self-collected positive result

The 2025 Enduring Consensus recommendations prefer clinician-collected cervical specimens but accept validated self-collected vaginal HPV samples for screening appropriate asymptomatic, average-risk people. After a negative self-collected HPV screen, they recommend repeat testing in three years. Follow-up of a positive result depends on genotype, meaning the viral type or group identified. HPV16 or HPV18 generally prompts colposcopy, while many other positive results need a clinician-collected cervical specimen for cytology or dual-stain triage before deciding on colposcopy. Some genotype-specific pathways differ. Evidence is more limited when using self-collection for surveillance after previous abnormalities, colposcopy or precancer treatment, so clinician collection is preferred in those settings. Collection is the beginning of the pathway, not its completion. [12]

## There is no single screening timetable for everyone

The American Cancer Society’s current average-risk guidance begins at age twenty-five and continues to at least sixty-five for people with a cervix. It prefers clinician-collected primary HPV testing every five years; an approved self-collected vaginal HPV test is repeated every three years. Cotesting every five years or Pap testing every three years remain alternatives when the preferred approach is unavailable. These intervals assume an appropriate negative result and no history requiring a different pathway. Stopping at sixty-five depends on documented previous normal testing and relevant history, not the birthday alone. Previous high-grade precancer, cancer, immune suppression, or uncertainty about earlier screening changes the discussion. After hysterectomy, whether the cervix remains and why surgery occurred also matter. [13]

## Why United States recommendations can look different

The Health Resources and Services Administration, HRSA, supports a separate Women’s Preventive Services Initiative framework. Its updated cervical guideline retains cytology every three years at ages twenty-one through twenty-nine and prefers primary high-risk HPV testing every five years at ages thirty through sixty-five, with specified alternatives and patient collection options. The updated guidance becomes effective for relevant insurance plan years beginning in 2027; that timing is distinct from when an FDA-authorized device exists. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists’ July 2026 statement addresses this updated framework with qualifications. Thus an age-twenty-one recommendation and the Cancer Society’s age-twenty-five recommendation can reflect different organizations. The clinician should identify which current guideline and personal-risk pathway are being followed. [14, 15]

## Programme rules also vary by country

England’s cervical screening programme offers screening to women and other people with a cervix aged twenty-five to sixty-four. Under the current programme, an HPV-negative routine screen generally leads to another invitation in five years. If high-risk HPV is detected in an appropriate clinician-collected sample, cytology helps determine referral or surveillance. Previous abnormal results can lead to earlier testing. These are programme-specific rules, not instructions to transplant an English interval into every healthcare system. Someone moving between countries should transfer the actual reports and next due date, including whether the preceding test was cytology, clinician-collected HPV or self-collected HPV. The programme invitation is useful, but it cannot replace assessment of new symptoms. [16]

## Higher-risk people need a tailored pathway

Human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, can impair control of HPV and changes cervical screening recommendations. The United States National Institutes of Health guidance, reviewed in March 2026, provides specific algorithms for people with HIV and recommends continuing screening through life, with decisions in older people considering life expectancy and cancer risk. It also notes that self-testing in this population is still being studied; general-population self-collection advice cannot simply be assumed to apply. Other forms of immune suppression likewise require an individual plan. This is why a laboratory result cannot be interpreted solely from an age chart. The relevant question is whether the person belongs to the population in which that test and follow-up interval were validated. [17]

## HPV FOCAL: finding precancer earlier changes later detection

The Canadian HPV FOCAL randomized trial assigned 19,009 women to primary HPV testing or cytology-based screening. Of these, 16,374 completed the study: 8,296 in the HPV arm and 8,078 in the cytology arm. At the forty-eight-month exit round, CIN3+ was detected at 2.3 per thousand in the HPV arm versus 5.5 per thousand in the cytology arm. HPV screening had detected more important lesions earlier, and both groups received HPV and cytology testing at the exit visit. The lower exit-round rate should not be relabelled as a difference in all lesions found cumulatively across the trial. These are precancer-detection outcomes within an organized follow-up system, rather than a guarantee that an individual negative screen prevents every cancer. [18]

## European trials followed the outcome that matters most

A pooled follow-up of four European randomized trials examined invasive cervical cancer rather than precancer alone. It included 176,464 women aged twenty to sixty-four, followed for a median of 6.5 years, with 107 invasive cancers identified. Across follow-up, the invasive-cancer rate in HPV-based screening was 0.60 times the rate with cytology-based screening, equivalent to a forty-percent relative reduction; the statistical uncertainty range was 0.40 to 0.89. The difference emerged more clearly after the first 2.5 years. This was evidence for a screening strategy coupled to investigation and management, not a comparison proving every HPV assay interchangeable. It also measured cancer incidence, not a separately demonstrated mortality reduction in this particular report. [19]

## Colposcopy and biopsy answer the next question

Colposcopy is a magnified examination of the cervix. A speculum holds the vaginal walls apart while the magnifying instrument, the colposcope, stays outside the body. It helps the clinician identify areas needing tissue sampling. A biopsy removes a small piece of tissue so a pathologist can assess cells in their structural setting and determine whether there is precancer, invasive cancer, or another explanation. This differs from examining dispersed cells in a Pap preparation. Some abnormalities extend into the cervical canal and require additional specialist assessment. A biopsy or excision can reveal disease missed by a limited surface sample. The planned procedure, pain-control options, possible bleeding, pregnancy considerations and expected result date should be explained before consent. [20]

## Dual stain is a triage tool, not a viral-load test

Some HPV-positive cervical samples can undergo p16/Ki67 dual staining. These stains look for two cellular proteins appearing together in the same cell: a pattern suggesting disrupted growth control. They do not measure how much virus is present or establish an infection date. The 2024 Enduring Consensus guidance allows the validated dual-stain assay to help triage selected HPV-positive results, including certain normal or low-grade cytology findings. Positive and negative dual-stain findings can therefore lead to different follow-up. However, HPV16, HPV18 and high-grade or concerning glandular cytology retain their specific management requirements. A negative dual stain should not be used to downgrade those findings, and results from an unvalidated substitute stain cannot automatically inherit the same recommendations. [21]

## Vaccination and screening protect at different points

HPV vaccination prevents new infections with the types covered by the vaccine; it does not remove an established HPV infection or treat a precancer. Protection is strongest when vaccination precedes exposure. An HPV-positive screen after vaccination is not, by itself, evidence that the vaccine was useless or improperly given. Vaccination history should be recorded so the clinician can review which preventive opportunities have already been completed. [22]

Vaccinated people still need cervical screening because vaccines do not cover every cancer-associated HPV type. The appropriate test and interval depend on age and health history. Vaccination and screening therefore complement each other rather than providing interchangeable forms of protection. [1]

## England’s vaccination outcome: strong protection, continuing follow-up

Falcaro and colleagues used English cancer-registration data with 13.7 million person-years of follow-up among women aged twenty to under thirty. Compared with a reference cohort not eligible for vaccination, the cohort offered vaccination at ages twelve to thirteen had an estimated eighty-seven-percent reduction in cervical-cancer rates and a ninety-seven-percent reduction in CIN3 rates. Across vaccine-eligible cohorts, the investigators estimated 448 fewer cervical cancers than expected by June 2019. This observational comparison concerns age at vaccine offer, not proof of each individual’s vaccination status or future immunity. It documents population benefit while leaving room for residual disease. Continuing the appropriate screening pathway remains part of prevention rather than evidence that vaccination failed. [23]

## One patient: persistent glandular concern despite limited biopsies

A 2017 report described a fifty-nine-year-old woman whose screening cytology showed atypical glandular cells favouring neoplasia. Repeated directed biopsies and a cervical cone specimen identified low-grade changes and an unusual glandular proliferation, rather than demonstrating the eventual malignancy. Because the glandular abnormality remained concerning, hysterectomy was performed; the larger specimen revealed gastric-type adenocarcinoma with lymphatic invasion. She subsequently received pelvic radiotherapy, and no clinical or imaging evidence of disease was reported through December 2016. This uncommon tumour type is HPV-independent, but the report does not establish a negative molecular HPV screen in this particular patient. Its lesson is the need to reconcile discordant cytology, tissue sampling and clinical findings, not that routine screening is generally unreliable. [24]

## When symptoms change the urgency

Bleeding after sex, between periods or after menopause, persistent unexplained watery or bloody discharge, and pelvic pain require clinical assessment even when screening is up to date. These symptoms have many non-cancer causes, but a negative screening test should not close the investigation. British Columbia’s current guidance explicitly separates symptomatic evaluation from routine screening: a vaginal HPV sample alone is inappropriate for symptomatic patients, and a suspicious cervical finding warrants urgent colposcopic referral without waiting for screening results. Urgency follows the symptoms and examination, rather than HPV positivity alone. Someone with new symptoms should arrange assessment instead of waiting for the next routine invitation or repeatedly ordering home samples in search of reassurance. [25]

## A positive HPV test cannot identify a culprit

The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention emphasizes that HPV is common, often shared between partners, and may become detectable many years after initial exposure through reactivation. A positive test therefore cannot reliably date acquisition, identify which partner transmitted it, or demonstrate infidelity. These limits also matter if a result is discussed in a legal or safeguarding context: a screening assay is not a reconstruction of events. Supportive, neutral explanation can reduce shame and make follow-up easier. Ask for the exact result, whether additional sampling is needed, the recommended timescale and who will track completion. A clear plan is more useful than treating a laboratory word as a verdict about cancer or personal conduct. [26]

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a tuberculosis test remember about infection, and what must clinicians establish before calling that infection active disease?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical symptoms require assessment

A negative screening test must not delay assessment of a compatible illness.

## What an immune test can establish

Tuberculosis immune testing looks for evidence that a person's immune system recognizes tuberculosis related proteins. The two familiar approaches are the tuberculin skin test, or TST, and blood tests called interferon gamma release assays, or IGRAs. They help investigate infection even when someone feels well. Latent tuberculosis infection means infection without evidence of active tuberculosis disease after an appropriate medical evaluation. People with latent infection do not spread TB to others, although some later develop disease. An immune result alone cannot make that distinction. It does not show where bacteria are located, count them, measure lung damage or establish whether the person is contagious. This chapter concerns human testing, with practical interpretation mainly drawn from United States guidance; veterinary TB tests and other national pathways require their own standards. [1]

Testing works best when it answers a reasoned question about exposure and risk. CDC recommends targeting people with increased likelihood of infection or progression, rather than indiscriminately screening everyone. A hypothetical calculation explains why: among 10,000 people, suppose 100 are infected and a test has 95 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity. It would produce about 95 true positives and 99 false positives. Only about half the positive results would represent infection. These are invented teaching assumptions, not performance claims for a particular IGRA. Sensitivity describes detection among infected people; specificity describes correct exclusion among uninfected people. In a low risk group, even uncommon false positives can be important. The history therefore changes the meaning of the result before any laboratory number is considered. [2]

## How the two tests detect immune memory

An IGRA exposes collected blood cells to selected antigens, meaning recognizable protein fragments, and measures their response. Memory T cells are white blood cells that can respond after previous sensitization. When they recognize the supplied antigens, they may release interferon gamma, an immune signaling protein. QuantiFERON TB Gold Plus measures the amount released, while T SPOT TB counts cells producing a detectable response. Neither method searches the blood for intact TB bacteria. The selected antigens improve discrimination from BCG vaccination and most other mycobacteria, but the test remains an indirect measure. A result also requires responsive cells and appropriate controls. A negative response can reflect absence of infection, recent exposure before immune recognition develops, impaired immunity or a problem affecting the specimen. [3]

The skin test measures a delayed immune reaction to purified protein derivative placed in the skin by a trained professional. The reader measures induration, the firm swelling, rather than the surrounding redness, 48 to 72 hours after placement. Interpretation combines that measurement with risk. Under CDC criteria, at least 5 mm is positive in selected groups such as recent contacts and people with HIV or substantial immune suppression. A 10 mm threshold applies to other specified risk groups, and 15 mm to people without known risk factors. These are United States criteria, not a universal self-reading chart. A person should attend the planned reading even if the site looks unremarkable. Failure to react does not exclude infection or active disease in someone with impaired immunity or serious illness. Correct placement, trained reading and the clinical context all contribute to the result. [4]

## Vaccination timing and specimen handling

BCG is a tuberculosis vaccine commonly given in countries where TB is more prevalent. It can cause a positive conventional skin test, and the appearance of that reaction does not reliably distinguish vaccination from infection. CDC therefore advises risk based evaluation of a positive TST even when BCG was received. An IGRA is generally preferred for a BCG vaccinated person because BCG itself does not induce a positive IGRA. This distinction concerns which antigens the tests use, rather than a blanket statement that one method is always correct. Keep vaccination history with the test record. It is relevant to choosing and interpreting the test, but it should not be used to dismiss symptoms or automatically explain away a positive result without assessment. [5]

After a recent exposure, the immune system may not yet produce a detectable response. A negative result obtained during that window can precede a later positive result. CDC contact investigation guidance calls for repeat testing when the first test was performed before eight to ten weeks had elapsed since the last exposure. Last exposure matters because contact may continue for some time. This planned repeat is not a reason to delay evaluation of symptoms. Young children and people with substantial immune suppression may need additional assessment and preventive management during the window under specialist care. The test date, exposure dates and current clinical findings belong together. Without that timeline, a laboratory report can appear reassuring while answering the question too early. [6]

Blood collection is only the beginning of an IGRA. The sample contains living immune cells, so collection materials, transport and timely processing affect whether those cells can provide an interpretable response. Mayo's QFT4 catalog specifies special handling and processing requirements and identifies situations that can compromise interpretation. These instructions are laboratory and platform specific; the patient should use the collection service arranged by the ordering team. This is different from a chemistry test in which a stable dissolved substance is simply measured later. An indeterminate result may lead the clinician and laboratory to review the sample pathway as well as the person's immune condition. Repeating the same collection problem would not resolve the underlying uncertainty, even if a fresh report appeared in the medical record. [7]

## Understanding controls and uncertain results

QuantiFERON uses a Nil control to measure background interferon gamma and a Mitogen control to test whether the cells can produce an adequate response to a general stimulus. The antigen response is assessed against that background. The manufacturer's interpretation resource explains that high background or an inadequate control response can prevent a useful positive or negative classification. The report may then say indeterminate. It means the test has not resolved the likelihood of infection; it does not establish either that infection is absent or that the person has severe TB. The clinician considers immune suppression, illness and specimen handling before deciding what to do next. The numerical interferon value is a measure of immune response, not a bacterial count or a direct scale of contagiousness. [8]

A second test can be useful for a specific reason. ATS, CDC and IDSA diagnostic guidance suggests confirmatory testing when someone aged five or older who is unlikely to be infected nevertheless has a positive screening test. In that low risk setting, both tests should be positive before the person is considered infected under the recommended approach. This is not a rule that every positive result must be ignored until duplicated, particularly after a significant exposure or in a symptomatic patient. The same guideline emphasizes that neither TST nor IGRA separates latent infection from active disease. The purpose of confirmation is to reduce unnecessary diagnoses and treatment in a population where false positives can outweigh true positives. Clinical evaluation remains necessary when the likelihood of disease is high. [9]

## Repeated results and laboratory investigations

Metcalfe and colleagues studied repeat QuantiFERON Gold In Tube testing on the same patient samples. Among 366 patients with valid paired results, 28, or 8 percent, changed classification between the first and second measurement. Results near the decision boundary were particularly vulnerable to a small numerical change altering the label. Because the same sample was retested, this was not evidence that those people acquired or cleared infection between two blood draws. The study examined an earlier assay generation in a particular screening setting; its percentage cannot be transferred to every current platform. It demonstrates why the exact assay, numerical response and clinical risk are useful when interpreting a change. A conversion or reversion is a description of test results, not a complete biological explanation. [10]

A separate 2012 laboratory investigation reported a sudden increase in positive QuantiFERON Gold In Tube results from about 10 to 31 percent. The team associated the cluster with a particular batch of antigen tubes and compared results using another batch. Among 81 employees retested after initial positive results in the suspect period, 91 percent tested negative with the new batch. The exact manufacturing or chemical cause remained unresolved despite investigation. This was a real quality problem with consequences for staff and follow-up services, rather than proof that all positive tests from all batches are unreliable. Its useful lesson is organizational: an unexpected cluster can justify coordinated laboratory review. An individual should discuss an unexpected result with the clinician, rather than independently decide that it must be a defective test. [11]

## Published cases showing different explanations

Patel and colleagues reported a 61 year old man taking adalimumab, an immune modifying medicine, who developed an ulcerated forearm lesion. A previously negative IGRA became positive, but culture and DNA identification of the skin specimen identified Mycobacterium marinum, a related environmental organism. The authors interpreted the immune result as cross-reactivity, in which shared antigens provoke a response. Treatment had to be adjusted for antimicrobial susceptibility and adverse effects, including diarrhea and blurred vision. The lesion resolved during the reported course, and the ocular symptoms resolved after five months of treatment. This was not an uncomplicated response to the first antibiotic. The case shows why identifying an organism at the diseased site can change the meaning of an immune test. It does not make IGRA a recommended diagnostic test for M. marinum or prove that every positive result in someone with a skin lesion is cross-reactive. [12]

Goire and colleagues described a 52 year old man with chest pain and fluid around the lung. He had a positive QuantiFERON result in 2018 but a negative result during his 2022 illness. Testing of respiratory and pleural samples, including a molecular assay on pleural biopsy tissue, initially failed to identify TB. Mycobacterial culture of pleural tissue eventually grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis. He completed six months of treatment with resolution of symptoms and pleural fluid and remained well at twelve months. A later QuantiFERON result was still negative. The observed outcome was recovery from confirmed pleural disease despite a negative immune result. It does not establish the exact biological cause of reversion or date the infection. The lesson is to continue investigating a plausible clinical problem when indirect immune testing and early direct tests do not settle it. [13]

## A forensic investigation needs more than immune testing

Templeton and colleagues investigated exposure to a patient whose tuberculosis had not been recognized before death. None of 40 previously skin test negative workers who had provided ward care showed a conversion. Among five previously negative personnel present during the autopsy, all five became skin test positive. Two subsequently had positive sputum cultures eight weeks later. The organisms from those two people and the deceased patient had matching DNA fingerprints. Thus immune testing identified a change requiring investigation, while cultures and organism comparison strengthened the evidence linking the illnesses. This small historical event cannot quantify the risk at every modern autopsy or prove that ordinary ward contact is safe. It demonstrates why records from before and after exposure matter and why an immune result alone cannot identify who transmitted an infection. The report's culture outcomes should not be expanded into an unreported long term recovery or legal finding. [14]

Auld and colleagues examined United States surveillance data from people with culture confirmed TB, including 11,393 with recorded IGRA results. Thirteen percent of those IGRAs were negative and 6 percent indeterminate. Negative or indeterminate immune results were associated with worse outcomes than positive results, and an indeterminate result was associated with disseminated disease and death. Disseminated means disease involving multiple body sites. These were observational associations in patients already known to have TB, not the predictive value of screening a healthy person. The data could not determine whether the pattern reflected impaired immune responses, delayed recognition, other illness or several factors together. The study reinforces a practical boundary: in someone seriously ill with a compatible presentation, an unhelpful immune result does not justify closing the investigation. [15]

## Outcomes depend on what follows the result

Finding infection can create an opportunity to prevent disease, but benefit and harm depend on the treatment actually delivered. In Sterling and colleagues' randomized prevention trial, completion was 82.1 percent with a shorter rifapentine plus isoniazid regimen and 69.0 percent with a longer isoniazid regimen. Investigator assessed drug related liver toxicity occurred in 0.4 and 2.7 percent, respectively. The trial supported the shorter regimen's effectiveness for preventing TB in the studied high risk population. Those findings are historical trial evidence, not a prescription for an individual reader. Drug interactions, age, pregnancy, resistance considerations and other clinical factors affect regimen selection. The example connects testing to real outcomes: a positive immune result is useful when it leads to the correct evaluation, an appropriate treatment decision and follow-up that includes adverse effects. [16]

Children require a separate assessment because progression after exposure can be rapid and severe, particularly in the very young. Current CDC guidance generally favors TST below age five while acknowledging that some experts use IGRAs in younger children. Choice also depends on the exposure, vaccination history, the ability to obtain blood and local expertise. A young child's negative test soon after contact does not automatically settle the management decision. CDC describes window preventive treatment for selected exposed children after TB disease has been excluded, with repeat testing and specialist guidance. This is a professional prevention pathway, not permission to start or stop medication at home. Families should keep the planned follow-up and tell the care team about new symptoms even if the first test was negative. [17]

## Evaluating disease and preserving a useful record

Evaluation for active TB joins the history, examination, chest imaging and appropriate microbiology. Culture attempts to recover the organism, while nucleic acid amplification tests look for its genetic material. A positive immune test can support evidence of infection but cannot replace those investigations. Conversely, some patients with active disease have negative immune tests. Specimen choice follows the suspected site: a respiratory sample and tissue from another affected organ do not answer exactly the same question. Before treating latent infection, clinicians must assess for active disease because the treatment approaches differ. The resulting explanation should state what has been demonstrated, what remains suspected and what evidence is still awaited. A report marked positive should not be allowed to compress all of that work into a single unexplained label. [18]

Persistent cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue or coughing blood can prompt assessment for TB, although other illnesses can produce similar symptoms. CDC describes a cough lasting three weeks or longer among common pulmonary features; that description is not a requirement to wait three weeks before seeking help when symptoms are concerning. Tell the service about possible TB exposure when arranging assessment so that it can plan the visit appropriately. Someone becoming acutely unwell needs prompt clinical care rather than waiting for a routine screening result. A prior negative test cannot override a changing clinical picture. Symptoms outside the lungs can also occur, so the location and course of the illness matter when the team selects further investigations. [19]

Keep the original test name, date, skin measurement or blood interpretation, exposure history and follow-up plan. CDC's occupational guidance distinguishes a two-step baseline skin testing process, which can reveal a boosted response from older immune memory, from repeat testing after a new exposure. Those timelines answer different questions. Written documentation of an earlier positive result or completed treatment is also valuable because immune tests may remain positive and repeated testing often adds little. A positive result alone cannot establish treatment failure, a new infection date or current infectiousness. Occupational requirements may vary with local regulations and the person's duties. A clear record allows the next clinician to interpret the result in context instead of repeating tests simply because the earlier explanation has been lost. [20]

# H027: Chromosome analysis

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Genetics
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When chromosomes are counted, photographed or sampled with probes, which kinds of change does each method actually reveal?*

## One name covers several different measurements

Chromosomes are the packages that organize DNA, the genetic material, within cells. A karyotype examines their number, size and visible structure. Most nucleated human cells normally have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs, although biology and clinical testing include important exceptions. An extra chromosome is a trisomy; a missing one is a monosomy. Chromosome analysis can investigate developmental differences, a recognizable syndrome, infertility or pregnancy loss. The same broad laboratory discipline also studies acquired changes in cancer cells, but the specimen, culture and interpretation for that question differ. Testing for a chromosome condition present from birth and testing a tumor should not be treated as interchangeable. [1]

Structure matters as well as count. A deletion removes chromosome material, a duplication adds a copy, and a translocation moves material between chromosomes. A balanced translocation has no net gain or loss apparent at the assessed resolution; an unbalanced one does. A balanced carrier may be healthy yet have a reproductive risk because eggs or sperm can receive an unbalanced combination. Some rearrangements can also disturb genes. Neither the total number of chromosomes nor the word balanced, by itself, answers every question about an individual or future pregnancy. The precise rearrangement and family context determine counseling. [3]

## Karyotyping shows the arrangement in dividing cells

For a conventional blood karyotype, the laboratory grows selected white blood cells and examines chromosomes when they are condensed during division, at metaphase. Staining creates bands used to distinguish chromosomes and recognize larger changes. Mayo's congenital blood study generally analyzes twenty metaphase cells, with additional work according to the indication. It requires viable cells: unsuitable transport, temperature extremes or other specimen problems can prevent adequate culture. The request should identify the clinical concern and any known familial rearrangement. A failed culture means that an interpretable result was not obtained; it is not a normal chromosome result. [2]

Resolution describes the scale of change that a method can distinguish. NHS guidance gives a typical karyotype range of about five to ten megabases, meaning millions of DNA base pairs, compared with roughly fifty to two hundred kilobases, meaning thousands, for many arrays. These are practical ranges, not guaranteed cutoffs for every specimen or region. The physical arrangement may still be more informative on a karyotype, even when an array resolves smaller gains and losses. Higher resolution and broader kinds of detectable change are different advantages. [23]

Culture can change what is observed. An unusual cell may reflect a change arising during growth in the laboratory, called pseudomosaicism, rather than a cell population present in the patient. Conversely, one cell line may grow less well than another. European cytogenomic guidance therefore requires appropriate work-up of suspected mosaic findings and attention to tissue, independent cultures and the referral question. An isolated unexpected cell requires that assessment before a constitutional mosaic diagnosis. Cultured cell percentages do not necessarily represent the whole body. [17]

## FISH answers a question selected in advance

Fluorescence in situ hybridization, abbreviated FISH, uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes that bind to selected matching sequences. A microscope shows the probe signals within cells. Some probes address a particular chromosome region, some help count chromosomes, and others paint larger chromosome segments. FISH can examine cells between divisions as well as condensed chromosomes, depending on the test. This can make it useful for a focused, relatively rapid question or for evaluating a suspected mosaic finding. The signal identifies the region targeted by that probe; it is not a complete reading of every chromosome or gene. [4]

Mayo's 22q11.2 FISH assay illustrates that boundary. Its method uses a probe in the HIRA region and a control elsewhere on chromosome 22. A missing target signal supports deletion of the region tested. A normal pattern does not exclude every chromosome 22 change, abnormalities outside the probe's location, or a different genetic explanation for the symptoms. The request and report should name the target rather than simply say that FISH was normal. Broader copy-number testing may be appropriate when a small deletion or duplication is suspected but the correct region is not already established. [5]

## Microarray measures DNA dosage across many regions

Chromosomal microarray, abbreviated CMA, uses many probes distributed across the genome to assess gains and losses of DNA. It does not require a photograph of dividing chromosomes. Small deletions and duplications invisible on a routine karyotype may therefore become detectable. A single-nucleotide-polymorphism array, or SNP array, also examines patterns at variable DNA positions that can reveal long regions where the two copies are alike. A normal result on Mayo's constitutional array cannot rule out balanced rearrangements or all mosaicism. Its resolution depends on probe coverage and the assay's reporting rules, rather than one universal smallest detectable abnormality. [6]

The array's scanner measures fluorescence at probe locations, rather than spelling out the patient's complete DNA sequence. In a comparative array, differently labeled patient and reference DNA compete for binding; relative signals indicate a possible loss or gain. SNP arrays combine intensity with patterns of the alternative DNA letters at selected sites. A duplicated region can be detected without showing where the extra copy is located: beside the original, on another chromosome or elsewhere. Likewise, SNP patterns can suggest some forms of uniparental disomy but do not exclude every parent-of-origin abnormality. A normal dosage result is not a sequence analysis. [24]

The underlying signal needs quality checks. In comparative genomic hybridization, the patient's DNA is compared with reference DNA; SNP-based systems use additional patterns of alternative DNA letters and reference datasets. Software identifies changes across neighboring probes, and laboratory staff review whether the signal meets validated criteria. DNA quality, probe behavior and analysis settings affect what can be called reliably. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, abbreviated ACMG, reaffirmed its 2021 technical standard in 2025. It requires validation of the relevant abnormalities and mosaic levels. Modern SNP information can help detect some changes in ploidy, the number of whole chromosome sets, that a copy-number comparison alone may miss. A claimed capability must belong to the actual validated platform. [7]

## The best first test depends on the clinical setting

A major 2010 consensus evaluated the then-available evidence for children with unexplained developmental disabilities or congenital anomalies. It favored CMA over routine banded karyotyping for detecting relevant small gains and losses, while retaining karyotyping for recognizable chromosome syndromes, familial rearrangements and selected reproductive histories. This is historical evidence for choosing a method, not a promise of a fixed yield for every child today. A visibly normal karyotype cannot be read as proof that the smaller-scale genetic causes of developmental difficulties have been excluded. A targeted test can still be preferable when the clinical question is already specific. [9]

The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2025 report places genome sequencing, or exome sequencing with CMA, in its first-tier pathway for many children with global developmental delay or intellectual disability. This reflects complementary information rather than a contest in which one technology always wins. Sequencing can identify letter-level changes, while a validated chromosome dosage analysis addresses missing or extra regions. Whether a genome assay already provides the needed copy-number information should be clarified before ordering overlapping studies. Developmental regression or other important clinical findings still require prompt clinical assessment and appropriate investigations while genetic testing proceeds. [10]

## Prenatal screening and diagnostic testing are different

Cell-free DNA screening analyzes DNA fragments in maternal plasma; its pregnancy-related component comes predominantly from the placenta. The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine's 2025 guidance describes it as screening, including for common trisomies. A positive result needs counseling and appropriate diagnostic follow-up; it should not be equated with a complete fetal chromosome diagnosis. Chorionic villus sampling, or CVS, obtains placental tissue, whereas amniocentesis obtains fluid containing fetal cells. Placental and fetal findings can differ. The choice of follow-up depends on the suspected condition and clinical circumstances. Even a reassuring screen does not assess every chromosome change or replace evaluation of a fetal anomaly. [19]

Wapner and colleagues compared prenatal microarray with karyotyping in a prospective study. Of 4,406 participants with chorionic-villus or amniotic-fluid samples, 4,391 had material suitable for array analysis and 4,340 had successful array results. Among normal-karyotype samples with ultrasound anomalies, additional clinically relevant findings occurred in 45 of 755; the corresponding counts were 34 of 1,966 for maternal-age indications and 12 of 729 after positive Down-syndrome screening. These are subgroup diagnostic yields, not population rates or predictions of childhood severity. The study's primary comparison excluded 58 samples mosaic on karyotyping. Its array analysis did not detect balanced translocations or triploidy; its SNP information was masked, so that result should not be generalized to every modern SNP platform. [11]

## Mosaicism is also a sampling problem

A person can have two or more genetically distinct cell populations, called mosaicism. The number and type of cells examined determine what can be detected. The 2024 Turner-syndrome guideline recommends counting at least thirty metaphases for the initial chromosome analysis; this can detect approximately ten-percent mosaicism with 95-percent confidence under the relevant sampling assumptions. That is not a guarantee that a ten-percent cell line in another tissue will be found in blood. When the clinical features strongly suggest Turner syndrome despite a normal blood result, additional cells or another tissue may be appropriate. The specimen and uncertainty should remain visible in the interpretation. [16]

In a 2016 report, a girl with developmental delay had normal results in all fifty blood metaphases. Cheek-cell, or buccal, FISH found an additional chromosome 12 signal in 30 percent of at least 200 nuclei, supporting the authors' diagnosis of Pallister-Killian syndrome. Testing skin fibroblasts, connective-tissue cells, was not performed. By age four she had seizures resistant to treatment and loss of acquired abilities; growth-hormone treatment was not started. This individual case shows why a reassuring blood result, or a mosaic fraction in one sampled tissue, cannot establish a mild prognosis for the whole child. [15]

Sparks and colleagues interviewed 44 families affected by mosaic trisomy 16 or trisomy 16 confined to the placenta. One terminated pregnancy and one stillbirth were excluded from the 42-child obstetric and neonatal analyses: 30 of 42 births were preterm and 31 of 42 infants had birth weight below the tenth percentile. Yet the study also reported favorable later outcomes for many children, with most school-aged children in mainstream classes. Median age at interview was 5.8 years. This was a registry-recruited, parent-reported cross-sectional study, and original genetic records were obtained for only 45 percent of the cohort. It neither guarantees a favorable outcome nor supports assuming severe disability from a mosaic prenatal result alone. [14]

## Apparently balanced does not always mean biologically silent

Halgren and colleagues followed 41 liveborn people with prenatally detected de novo balanced rearrangements and no abnormal first-trimester screening or ultrasound indication. Registry follow-up averaged seventeen years. Adding clinical follow-up identified neurodevelopmental or neuropsychiatric disorders in 11 of 41. Arrays in 32 found no pathogenic imbalances, while sequencing identified gene disruption or other breakpoint complexity in some carriers. De novo means newly arising. The study concerns this selected category of rearrangements; it is not a 27-percent risk estimate for every inherited balanced translocation. Its long follow-up matters because some outcomes became apparent well after birth. A chromosome arrangement can be copy-number neutral while disrupting an important gene or its regulation. [13]

## Contamination can answer the wrong person’s question

Maternal cell contamination means that a prenatal specimen contains maternal cells or DNA as well as pregnancy-derived material. The Association for Molecular Pathology's laboratory guidance emphasizes checking sample identity and maternal contamination alongside prenatal analysis. Contamination need not be visibly bloody, and an apparently normal result may otherwise reflect the wrong mixture of cells. The maternal comparison specimen and the sensitivity of the contamination assay matter. Culture can reduce some contaminating blood cells but does not eliminate every source of contamination or growth bias. An inconclusive mixture may require another specimen or another analysis, rather than interpreting the result as a normal fetus. Collection is a clinical procedure with its own counseling and risks. [18]

## Some SNP patterns raise another inheritance question

A long region of homozygosity is a stretch where both chromosome copies carry the same pattern at examined positions. Many such regions across chromosomes can suggest shared parental ancestry; a pattern involving one chromosome can raise the possibility of uniparental disomy. ACMG's 2021 revision explains that these are inferences with confounding factors. The pattern does not independently prove a particular parental relationship, misconduct or a specific disease. Clinical context, the distribution of regions and appropriate follow-up determine what can be concluded. Such potentially sensitive findings should be anticipated in counseling, rather than presented as a definitive family or legal conclusion. [20]

Uniparental disomy means that both copies of a chromosome or segment came from one parent. It can matter because some genes are imprinted: their activity depends on whether the copy came from an egg or sperm. A person can therefore have the usual number of chromosome copies but an important parent-of-origin problem. Methylation testing assesses relevant chemical marks and may be needed in an appropriate diagnostic pathway. A chromosome count alone does not answer that question. Conversely, uniparental disomy does not always cause illness; the chromosome, genes and inheritance mechanism determine its significance. [21]

## Interpret the change separately from its implications

The ACMG and Clinical Genome Resource framework classifies constitutional copy-number variants as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign or benign. Classification considers gene content, dosage sensitivity, population and case evidence, inheritance and other relevant data. The framework separates the evidence-based classification from its meaning for this particular person. A large change is not automatically harmful, and a small change can be important. An inherited finding is not automatically harmless merely because a parent appears well; incomplete penetrance, meaning that some carriers remain unaffected, or differences in severity can complicate the comparison. An uncertain copy-number finding needs explanation and follow-up appropriate to the evidence, without being converted into a definitive diagnosis. [8]

## Postmortem testing contributes evidence, not a complete cause of death

Reddy and colleagues studied 532 stillbirths. Microarray produced results in 87.4 percent, compared with 70.5 percent for karyotyping, and detected abnormal chromosome counts or pathogenic copy-number findings in 8.3 versus 5.8 percent. Its ability to use nonviable tissue helped explain the difference. The authors nevertheless reported limitations, including missed low-level mosaicism, imperfect tissue comparisons and unavailable parental DNA. This is a documented postmortem diagnostic outcome, not proof that each detected variant independently caused death. Fetal examination, placental findings and other investigations remain part of determining what happened. A failed karyotype and a normal array also have different meanings and should remain separately recorded. [12]

## The report should leave a usable next step

Consent should explain the intended question, possible uncertain or unexpected findings, implications for relatives and how results will be communicated. Families need an opportunity to discuss the information before testing. A chromosome test may be technically straightforward while its result has complex emotional or reproductive implications. Those implications deserve counseling rather than an unexplained positive-or-negative label. [22]

A useful report identifies the specimen, method, result, limitations and appropriate follow-up. Chromosome coordinates need the reference-genome version. Next steps may include parental studies, another tissue, another method or clinical surveillance. A normal result means no reportable abnormality was found within the stated scope; it does not exclude every genetic explanation. [17]

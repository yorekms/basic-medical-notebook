> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When does a positive allergy test identify a dangerous trigger, and when does it identify an immune response that causes no illness?*

## Start with the reaction and the exposure

Allergy testing answers a clinical question about a particular exposure, rather than searching for a hidden explanation for every symptom. Immunoglobulin E, abbreviated IgE, is an antibody involved in many immediate allergic reactions. Sensitization means that testing detects an IgE response to an allergen, a substance capable of triggering allergy. Clinical allergy requires that exposure actually produces compatible symptoms. The European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology recommends starting food-allergy assessment with a detailed history, then selecting tests for the suspected foods. The account should connect what was eaten, the time symptoms began, the organs affected, previous tolerance and subsequent exposures. A positive result without that connection may create an unnecessary restriction; a convincing reaction deserves investigation even when an initial test is negative. [1]

A rapidly developing reaction with breathing difficulty, throat involvement, collapse or circulatory failure requires emergency assessment. Anaphylaxis is a severe systemic allergic reaction; it can occur without hives. The World Allergy Organization recognizes sudden bronchospasm, airway involvement or hypotension after a likely allergen as potentially diagnostic even when skin findings are absent. Treatment follows the clinical condition, with epinephrine as first-line therapy, rather than waiting for blood results. Someone with a prescribed emergency plan should follow it and summon emergency help. Testing becomes valuable after immediate care has begun, when the team preserves the exposure timeline and investigates the cause. A routine outpatient allergy panel is not a way to decide whether an actively deteriorating person needs help. [19]

## What skin and blood testing actually measure

Skin prick testing measures an immediate skin response to selected allergen extracts. A wheal is the raised, itchy swelling; the surrounding redness is the flare. The Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy emphasizes trained selection, interpretation and the ability to manage systemic reactions. Antihistamines and some other medicines can suppress the skin response, while extensive eczema can make testing difficult. The service should review medicines beforehand and give individualized preparation advice; stopping a necessary medicine without discussion can introduce another problem. Blood testing may be useful when skin testing is unsuitable. [2]

A specific-IgE blood assay measures antibody binding to a selected allergen. In Mayo Clinic Laboratories' peanut assay, peanut material is attached to a solid support. Antibodies in the serum, the liquid remaining after blood clots, can bind that material. An enzyme-linked detection antibody then produces a fluorescent signal proportional to the amount of bound specific IgE. The laboratory converts the signal into its reporting units. The instrument is detecting an immune recognition pattern, not peanuts circulating in the body or a direct measurement of airway swelling. Specimen identity, the exact allergen tested and the assay platform belong on the report. A numerical result is useful only with the test's interpretation and the reason it was ordered. [3]

A larger skin response or higher food-specific IgE may increase the probability of clinical allergy in an appropriate population, but neither reliably predicts the severity of a future reaction. Total IgE combines responses to many targets and does not identify the culprit food. The United States National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases expert panel also distinguishes IgE-mediated reactions from other food disorders. A negative specific-IgE test does not settle conditions driven by different immune mechanisms, including food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome, which can cause delayed vomiting and systemic illness. Likewise, digestive intolerance is not automatically an allergy. The history determines which biological pathway needs assessment, rather than making every meal-related complaint fit one blood test. [4]

## Components can clarify a positive extract result

A whole peanut extract contains several proteins. Component testing asks which individual proteins the antibodies recognize. Mayo's reflex panel distinguishes storage proteins, including Ara h 2, from Ara h 8, which shares features with a birch-pollen protein. Cross-reactivity means antibodies recognize similar structures in different sources. That can explain a positive peanut extract result in someone whose main sensitization is to pollen. Component results refine the clinical assessment; they do not provide an absolute guarantee about reaction severity or replace the exposure history. A negative result for one component also leaves other components untested or negative separately. The practical question is which uncertainty the additional result resolves before avoidance, supervised challenge or another clinical decision is chosen. [5]

Kansen and colleagues prospectively assessed 150 children aged 3.5 to 18 years in Dutch secondary and tertiary care; 113 completed a conclusive reference assessment. At Ara h 2 values of 0.1 allergen-specific kilounits per liter or less, written kUA/L, 26 of 26 evaluated children were peanut-tolerant; at 5.0 kUA/L or higher, 34 of 35 were allergic. The intermediate group needed further evaluation. These findings support probability ranges in that pathway, rather than a universal rule for every child, adult or laboratory. The investigators did not incorporate pretest probability into their accuracy analysis. A reassuring component value therefore belongs within a clinician-selected pathway, not an unsupervised attempt to eat a previously suspected trigger. [6]

## A functional blood test and a supervised challenge answer different questions

The basophil activation test examines living blood cells rather than antibody binding alone. Basophils are immune cells that can respond when their surface-bound IgE encounters an allergen. In Santos and colleagues' peanut study, flow cytometry, a method that measures individual cells, detected activation markers after laboratory exposure to peanut. The primary analysis included 104 children, with independent validation in 65 others. The test helped distinguish allergy from sensitization with tolerance and could reduce the need for challenges. Some participants' cells did not respond adequately to the test controls, limiting interpretation. Fresh-cell handling and specialist laboratory expertise matter. Published accuracy for this selected pediatric peanut population should not be transferred automatically to every allergen or to all commercial implementations. [7]

An oral food challenge observes whether eating a suspected food produces a clinical reaction under medical supervision. The 2024 joint statement from the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology and its European counterpart addresses safety, observation and explicit criteria for continuing, pausing or stopping. Those criteria matter because anxiety, subjective sensations and genuine early reactions can overlap. An open challenge is often sufficient; blinded challenges conceal the exposure to reduce expectation effects when uncertainty persists. The team decides whether the patient's health and prior history make a challenge appropriate and provides rescue capability. A negative result can support reintroduction through an agreed plan. It does not prove lifelong tolerance under every circumstance or identify the cause of unrelated symptoms. [8]

## The harm of testing without a sound question

Food-specific immunoglobulin G, or IgG, is a different antibody class from IgE. Commercial panels sometimes present IgG responses to many foods as evidence of sensitivity or intolerance. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology advises against that use: these responses can reflect ordinary dietary exposure, and food-specific IgG4 may accompany tolerance. A long list of positive foods is therefore not a validated explanation for fatigue, eczema, bowel symptoms or generalized ill health. This does not make those symptoms imaginary. It means the proposed measurement cannot establish the advertised cause. Broad restriction can make eating difficult and reduce nutritional variety while delaying evaluation of the actual problem. The test's clinical validity matters more than the number of foods it reports. [9]

A published infant case shows a concrete consequence of unnecessary restriction. An infant with eczema was placed on an increasingly restricted diet and eventually received only rice milk. He developed generalized swelling, poor growth, profound anemia and very low albumin, a blood protein that helps retain fluid in the circulation. Low positive milk and egg IgE results were reported, but cow's milk was tolerated during hospital care, and later skin tests were negative. Nutrition support resolved the edema; protein and clotting tests normalized by day ten. Tooth demineralization persisted at follow-up through age two. The report concerns an inappropriate diet, not harm from medically necessary allergen avoidance. Its diagnostic lesson is that sensitization and eczema do not establish a need to remove essential foods without nutritional oversight. [10]

## Delayed reactions can require a different target

Alpha-gal is a sugar structure found in non-primate mammalian products. Alpha-gal syndrome can produce delayed reactions after mammalian meat, so asking only about foods eaten immediately before symptoms may miss the pattern. Current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance combines symptoms, timing and tick or outdoor exposure with alpha-gal-specific IgE testing. A positive antibody result alone does not establish the syndrome, particularly where tick exposure and sensitization are common. Testing needs to target alpha-gal specifically when that history suggests it; a conventional food panel cannot be assumed to include every relevant target. The report should preserve the distinction between detectable antibodies and the clinical syndrome, rather than automatically converting a positive result into lifelong dietary instructions. [11]

Commins and colleagues tested the delayed-reaction explanation in a small supervised meat-challenge study. Ten of twelve participants with alpha-gal IgE and a history of delayed severe reactions developed clinical reactions, while controls did not. Reactions began three to seven hours after ingestion, unlike the shorter interval familiar from many other food allergies. Laboratory basophil activation also developed later in the challenge period. The experiment supports a biological reason for the misleading time gap. It does not mean every sensitized person reacts, every episode will have the same delay, or that waiting several hours makes a home exposure safe. The observed outcome was a challenge reaction in a selected group, not a long-term estimate of the chances of a fatal event. [12]

## A penicillin label deserves a precise history

A record saying penicillin allergy can represent immediate allergy, a delayed immune reaction, a viral rash during treatment or a nonimmune adverse effect such as diarrhea. Current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance uses the original symptoms, timing, management and later tolerated exposures to select a validated risk assessment and, when appropriate, skin testing or direct oral challenge. Severe delayed reactions with blistering, widespread skin injury, organ damage or blood-cell injury require specialist assessment and do not belong in a routine challenge pathway. Removing an inaccurate label can restore antibiotic choices; it does not justify ignoring a credible severe reaction. The aim is an accurate, usable record that explains both the suspected reaction and the evaluation actually completed. [13]

The PALACE trial studied selected adults with low-risk penicillin histories at six specialist centers. Of 382 randomized participants, 377 were analyzed: 187 received direct oral challenge and 190 the skin-testing pathway followed by challenge. One person in each group had the immediate immune-mediated primary outcome, about 0.5 percent per group. Nine versus ten immune-mediated adverse events were recorded over the following five days, with no serious adverse events reported. Direct challenge met the prespecified statistical margin for noninferiority: it was not unacceptably worse within the trial's defined comparison. The result did not establish identical risk or test unsupervised exposure, severe delayed reactions or every person with a penicillin label. Delabeling follows a completed risk-based assessment. [14]

Drug testing is specific to the medicine and mechanism. The 2022 practice parameter places greater emphasis on history-based selection and challenge for suitable patients, while recognizing that many drug skin tests have limited validation. A challenge asks whether hypersensitivity occurs; desensitization temporarily modifies responsiveness so an essential medicine can be given through a specialist plan. Successful desensitization does not erase the underlying allergy. A negative test to one antibiotic also cannot clear unrelated medicines automatically. The final record should name the drug, the tested formulation when relevant, the methods used and what future prescribing advice follows. That distinction helps prevent both unnecessary avoidance and a misleading statement that all drug allergy has been excluded. [15]

## A hidden antiseptic illustrates why the exposure record matters

In a 2017 report, a twenty-year-old man developed a rash after fracture surgery, then severe bronchospasm and cardiac arrest during another operation two months later. He recovered after resuscitation and intensive care and was discharged well. Initial skin testing was negative, but chlorhexidine-specific IgE was elevated; repeat skin testing later became positive. Chlorhexidine is an antiseptic and can be overlooked when attention centers on anesthetic drugs. The report supports reviewing disinfectants and other perioperative exposures alongside medicines. It does not establish that every early postoperative rash predicts anaphylaxis, or that one negative skin test excludes the culprit. No subsequent long-term surgical outcome was reported, so discharge recovery is the documented clinical endpoint. [16]

## Patch testing investigates delayed skin allergy

Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed immune reaction in skin, often to substances such as metals, preservatives or workplace chemicals. Patch testing is different from skin prick testing and blood IgE measurement. The published 2026 European Society of Contact Dermatitis guideline connects a positive patch result to the location and course of dermatitis and to actual exposure. A positive response establishes contact sensitization; current clinical relevance still has to be assessed. Irritation can resemble allergy, and immunosuppressive treatment can reduce test responses. Occupational interpretation also requires the work-material history and symptoms away from and back at work. A positive patch test alone cannot identify the responsible employer or prove that every rash has one occupational cause. [17]

## Respiratory allergy testing should change a practical decision

For hay fever and allergic asthma, positive pollen, mite or animal-dander tests need to match where and when symptoms occur. The 2024 inhalant-immunotherapy guideline applies to people aged five and older and recommends selecting clinically relevant allergens that correlate with history and are confirmed by testing. Immunotherapy is treatment intended to modify the allergic response, so treating every positive result can add burden without clarifying the cause of symptoms. The guideline also advises against repeat allergy testing simply to judge whether ongoing immunotherapy works, unless exposure changes or symptom control is lost. Clinical improvement and safety assessment are the relevant outcomes. A falling test number is not, by itself, a certificate that treatment succeeded or that a patient can abandon follow-up. [18]

## Tryptase supports anaphylaxis assessment without replacing it

Tryptase is an enzyme associated with mast cells, immune cells that release mediators during many allergic reactions. The 2023 anaphylaxis practice parameter suggests obtaining an acute sample as early as feasible, ideally within two hours of symptom onset, and a later baseline for comparison. This is a clinical sampling opportunity, not a deadline for seeking help. One widely used comparison is an acute value above 1.2 times the baseline plus 2 nanograms per milliliter. A meaningful rise supports mast-cell activation but does not identify the allergen. A normal result cannot exclude anaphylaxis. Baseline elevation may prompt assessment for a mast-cell disorder or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, an inherited cause of increased baseline tryptase; it does not independently prove a recent reaction. [20]

Dua and colleagues measured tryptase during 160 reactive peanut challenges and 45 placebo challenges in fifty adults. These were repeated challenges, not 160 separate patients, and only nine percent of reactive challenges met the study's anaphylaxis category. Tryptase rose above the laboratory normal range in just four reactions, while comparison with each participant's baseline identified a rise in one hundred. No rise occurred during the placebo challenges. The study shows why a single normal-range value can be falsely reassuring and why the person's baseline adds information. Its proposed study-specific percentage threshold should not replace clinical assessment or be applied automatically to children, injected-drug reactions or postmortem specimens. The laboratory measurement records part of the reaction, rather than every mechanism producing symptoms. [21]

## Venom testing and a forensic case show the limits of one result

After a systemic bee or wasp reaction, testing aims to identify relevant venom sensitization and assess future management needs. The German and Austrian specialist-society guideline recommends connecting venom IgE, component results and, where needed, skin or cellular testing with the sting history. Positive responses to more than one venom can reflect cross-reactivity rather than multiple clinically important allergies. Tests obtained soon after a sting can be negative and require planned reassessment. Baseline tryptase and evaluation for mast-cell disease are particularly relevant after reactions extending beyond skin symptoms. The purpose is to select an appropriate prevention and follow-up plan. A positive venom result in someone without a compatible reaction should not automatically be treated as evidence of a previous dangerous event. [22]

A 2025 forensic report described a forty-one-year-old vineyard worker with systemic mastocytosis who collapsed after a sting and died twelve hours later despite intensive resuscitation. Investigators compared stored blood collected during life with central and peripheral samples obtained six days after death. No sting mark was identified at autopsy. The authors attributed death to venom anaphylaxis with mastocytosis contributing. Sampling sites, the postmortem interval and resuscitation complicated tryptase interpretation. The case supports integrating laboratory, clinical and scene evidence, without establishing a universal fatal concentration or proving preventability. [23]

Postmortem blood needs its own evidential framework. In Tse and colleagues' retrospective study, nine anaphylactic deaths were compared with forty-five controls using femoral blood. A proposed total-tryptase boundary of 53.8 micrograms per liter had estimated sensitivity of 89 percent and specificity of 93 percent in that dataset. Neither measure was perfect, and the small selected groups cannot establish a universal cause-of-death rule. Clinical reference limits from living patients cannot simply be transferred to autopsy material. Forensic interpretation must retain the sampling site, postmortem interval, analytical method, exposure evidence and competing explanations. An elevated tryptase or allergen-specific IgE may support an investigation; neither independently establishes the culprit, a person's intent or legal responsibility. [24]

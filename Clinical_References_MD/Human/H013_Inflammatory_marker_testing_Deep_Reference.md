> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does an inflammation signal tell us about illness, and when can a reassuring result conceal a serious problem?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Symptoms and clinical state determine urgency

Serious deterioration or new visual symptoms need urgent clinical assessment.

## What the tests are trying to detect

Inflammation is the body's response to tissue injury, infection and many other disturbances. It can help contain a problem and support healing, but an excessive or persistent response can also damage tissue. C reactive protein, abbreviated CRP, is made by the liver in response to inflammatory signals. Measuring it can help investigate symptoms or follow an established illness. An increased result does not identify the site or the cause. Infection, autoimmune disease and tissue injury can all produce inflammation, and the patient still needs an explanation of the underlying process. This chapter concentrates on human CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate and procalcitonin. They observe different parts of the response, rather than measure three interchangeable versions of the same substance. Their value depends on which clinical question is being asked and what other evidence is available. [1]

## How the laboratory measures CRP

Mayo Clinic Laboratories describes its routine serum CRP method as immunoturbidimetric. Antibodies recognize CRP, producing a reaction that changes how light passes through the sample; the instrument relates that optical signal to a calibrated concentration. This is a measurement of a protein, not a direct image of inflamed tissue or a count of bacteria. Results must retain their units. In a hypothetical comparison, 10 mg/L and 1 mg/dL describe the same concentration, because a liter contains ten deciliters. Reading those as a tenfold improvement would be a unit error. The example is arithmetic, not a patient or a decision threshold. Reference intervals and the laboratory's method help interpret the report, while the clinician supplies the context. A decimal place on the screen does not eliminate uncertainty about what caused the protein to rise. [2]

## ESR observes the behavior of red cells

The erythrocyte sedimentation rate, or ESR, observes how far red blood cells settle in a standardized blood sample over a defined period. Erythrocyte means red blood cell; sedimentation means settling. Inflammation can change blood proteins in ways that encourage cells to collect together and settle more quickly. The result is commonly expressed in millimeters per hour. It is influenced by more than inflammation, including age, pregnancy, anemia and other blood characteristics. Anemia means a reduced hemoglobin concentration for the relevant patient group. A high ESR therefore cannot be translated directly into a certain amount of infection. It can support the investigation or monitoring of selected conditions, but examination, symptoms and other tests are necessary to interpret it. Equally, an ESR within the reference interval cannot exclude every inflammatory illness. [3]

## The ESR method belongs with the number

The International Council for Standardization in Haematology developed a reference approach based on the Westergren method, specifying the blood preparation and characteristics of the measuring tube. Newer automated methods need comparison with an appropriate reference rather than merely adopting the familiar name. This matters when results from different laboratories or devices are compared over time. Changing the physical system can change the measurement, especially at higher values. A report should therefore be interpreted within the laboratory's validated procedure and reference information. Standardization is the work that makes a result reproducible enough to be useful; it does not make the test specific for one disease. For a patient following a long illness, retaining the laboratory and method alongside the date is more informative than keeping only a list of numbers copied from different reports. [4]

## Procalcitonin has a different biological signal

Procalcitonin, abbreviated PCT, is another blood marker used mainly in seriously ill patients when bacterial infection is a concern. Mayo's listed assay uses electrochemiluminescence, an antibody based measurement with a light producing detection system. The laboratory explicitly cautions that raised concentrations also occur with severe trauma, shock, recent surgery, burns, renal impairment and some other conditions. Conversely, a low result does not exclude all infections or every clinical presentation of bacterial disease. The timing of collection and the patient's broader condition remain important. PCT can change the probability assigned to a diagnosis, but it cannot identify an organism or select a suitable antibiotic by itself. The method's role is to add evidence to a clinical assessment, not provide an independent verdict that the patient does or does not have sepsis. [5]

## What the patient experiences

A procalcitonin measurement generally requires a small blood sample from a vein. The person may notice a brief sting or a small bruise, while the principal clinical burden comes from the illness prompting the test rather than from the measurement itself. The ordering team should explain whether a single result or a sequence is intended. Testing may help assess a seriously ill patient or monitor progress during treatment, and preparation instructions should come from the service performing the test. The result should be connected to the collection time, symptoms and any treatment already given. Keeping these details prevents a later reader from assuming that a measurement taken after resuscitation or antibiotics necessarily describes the patient's state before those interventions. A timeline is part of the evidence, even though it does not appear as another number on the laboratory report. [6]

## Disagreement between markers can be meaningful

Costenbader and colleagues examined 2,069 patients with ESR and CRP measured on the same day. They defined substantial disagreement by one result falling in the highest third of values and the other in the lowest third, finding 87 such cases. Kidney impairment, infection and low albumin were associated with particular patterns of disagreement. These findings concern the study's strict definition, rather than the frequency of every mildly mismatched pair in general practice. They show why one marker should not simply be used to overrule the other. Different biology, the timing of the response and other patient characteristics may be involved. The useful next question is whether the pattern fits the illness being investigated. Repeating both tests without a defined question can generate more data while leaving the underlying uncertainty unchanged. [7]

## Starting treatment and stopping treatment are different decisions

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign's 2026 adult guideline suggests clinical evaluation alone over adding procalcitonin when deciding whether to start antimicrobials for possible or probable sepsis or septic shock. A low marker should not delay treatment that the clinical assessment indicates is needed. The same guideline suggests that PCT combined with clinical assessment may help decide when to stop antibiotics once the source is adequately controlled and the best duration remains uncertain. Source control means addressing the underlying focus, such as draining an infected collection when appropriate. These recommendations concern different decisions at different points in an illness. Neither says that an individual should start, stop or change prescribed treatment by reading a laboratory result at home. They illustrate why a useful biomarker needs a defined population, purpose and place within a treatment pathway. [8]

## A large trial tested whether marker guidance changed care

The ADAPT Sepsis trial assigned 2,760 critically ill adults with suspected sepsis to daily PCT guided advice, daily CRP guided advice or standard care. PCT guidance reduced average antibiotic exposure from 10.7 to 9.8 days, with an adjusted difference of 0.88 days. It met the trial's predefined noninferiority criterion for death by 28 days. Noninferiority means that a confidence interval excluded the prespecified unacceptable increase in risk; it does not prove identical mortality or absence of all harm. CRP guidance did not demonstrate the same reduction in antibiotic duration, and its mortality comparison was inconclusive. Thus the two markers were not interchangeable treatment tools. The results apply to structured protocols in critical care, where clinicians could consider the whole patient. They are not a rule that every falling inflammatory marker permits antibiotics to be stopped. [9]

## A primary care trial asked a different question

Cals and colleagues studied 431 patients with lower respiratory tract infection across 20 Dutch general practices. Clinicians using a point of care CRP test prescribed antibiotics at the first consultation to 31 percent of patients, compared with 53 percent without the test. Training in communication also reduced prescribing, and recovery and satisfaction were similar across study groups. The study demonstrates a practical outcome: a test used with assessment and explanation can change prescribing without an observed loss of recovery in that population. It does not show that CRP alone identifies every bacterial pneumonia, nor was it a study of unstable patients in intensive care. The communication result also matters. An explanation of expected recovery and reasons for reassessment can be part of good diagnostic care rather than an optional addition after a number has been obtained. [10]

## Normal markers can occur in a disease threatening vision

Giant cell arteritis is inflammation of large arteries that can interrupt blood supply to the eye. Kermani and colleagues studied 764 people who underwent temporal artery biopsy; 177 had a biopsy supporting the diagnosis. A small proportion of biopsy positive patients had both ESR and CRP within the normal range without prior glucocorticoid treatment. This is a concrete reason to avoid using normal markers as an absolute exclusion rule. It is also important to keep the denominator clear: the study concerned people already selected for biopsy, not an unselected population with headaches. A normal result changes probability within an assessment; it does not erase sufficiently concerning symptoms. The clinician may need imaging, biopsy or urgent treatment decisions based on the whole presentation, while still considering alternative explanations for the symptoms. [11]

## A published case ended with persistent visual disability

A 2026 report described a 75 year old man with sequential eye involvement despite ESR and CRP remaining within their reference ranges. Vascular imaging did not show a critical atherosclerotic narrowing, while PET imaging showed increased activity along the vertebral arteries that supported the clinical diagnosis of giant cell arteritis. He received corticosteroid treatment with specialist involvement. At follow up he described complete loss of sight in one eye and very restricted remaining vision in the other, with no further worsening after treatment began. That was stabilization with major disability, not restoration of sight. The diagnosis was supported by clinical and imaging assessment; this account does not relabel it as biopsy proven. A single report cannot establish how often this presentation occurs or prove what an earlier intervention would have achieved. It does show why a reassuring blood result must not close an investigation when vision is changing. [12]

## Treatment can suppress the expected signal

Bari and colleagues reported a 65 year old man with rheumatoid arthritis receiving several medicines, including tocilizumab, which blocks a key inflammatory signaling pathway. His painful swollen knee eventually prevented weight bearing, but he was otherwise systemically well and CRP was normal. Fluid from the joint and blood cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus. He completed six weeks of antibiotics with complete symptom resolution, while CRP stayed normal throughout. The authors considered prior tocilizumab treatment the likely explanation for suppression of the expected response. This is an observed case, not a frequency estimate or a reason to change the medicine independently. It demonstrates why a medication history belongs beside the marker result. A low CRP may reflect an altered ability to produce that signal rather than evidence that no serious infection exists. [13]

## A very high procalcitonin did not settle the cause of shock

Altermanini and colleagues described a 23 year old man undergoing investigation of severe anemia. During colonoscopy, worms were seen and he developed fever, a rapid heartbeat and very low blood pressure. PCT reached 95.2 ng/mL, initially raising concern for sepsis. The authors ultimately favored anaphylactic shock because of the abrupt clinical sequence and rapid improvement after emergency allergy treatment; blood cultures were negative. They proposed that disturbance of the worms was the trigger, but did not obtain tryptase testing, so that mechanism remained an inference. He was discharged five days later, and follow up showed normalization of inflammatory markers and subsequent blood counts. This case does not prove that infection was impossible. It demonstrates that a striking PCT concentration can occur in a different life threatening inflammatory state, and that the diagnosis depends on the full clinical sequence rather than the biomarker alone. [14]

## High sensitivity CRP answers another clinical question

A high sensitivity CRP assay measures the same protein with performance suited to lower concentrations. The adjective does not mean that the patient has a more sensitive immune system or that the result is a general test for every hidden illness. Mayo distinguishes its high sensitivity assay, used in cardiovascular risk assessment, from routine CRP requested for other inflammatory disorders. This is an example of purpose shaping interpretation. A concentration used in a discussion of future vascular risk should not be read through a sepsis decision rule, and an acute inflammatory illness can make that risk assessment difficult to interpret. The reader should preserve the exact test name and the reason it was ordered. A result becomes useful when its analytical range and intended clinical task agree with the question being asked. [15]

## A forensic case revealed inflammation after death

Hashida and colleagues reported a 74 year old man evaluated for lower back pain and found dead three days after treatment for presumed lumbar disc disease. Autopsy found an unusually positioned perforated appendix, a large abscess behind the abdominal lining and associated soft tissue infection. Microbiological testing of pus identified several bacteria, and CRP in cardiac blood was 43.5 mg/dL. This was a postmortem measurement, not a documented missed living blood result. The authors attributed death to sepsis arising from the infection. Their conclusion depended on the combined anatomical, microscopic, microbiological and biochemical findings. The case illustrates how an inflammatory marker can support a forensic explanation without locating the source by itself. It does not establish that every backache requires CRP, that the same value existed at the earlier consultation, or that a particular test would certainly have prevented death. [16]

## Postmortem marker research needs its own evidence

Palmiere and colleagues investigated PCT, CRP and another inflammatory marker in a forensic series including sixteen sepsis related fatalities and control individuals. Their work examined whether biochemical measurements could contribute to postmortem diagnosis. This is a different setting from deciding how to treat a living patient. Death, specimen source, time before sampling and the circumstances of the investigation must be considered, and a study in selected autopsy cases cannot establish a universal cutoff for every body or laboratory. The relevant forensic conclusion connects biochemical findings with the clinical history, examination of organs, tissue microscopy and microbiology. An elevated marker alone cannot identify the responsible organism, determine whether care was negligent or establish the precise time at which an infection became irreversible. Those are further questions requiring their own evidence. [17]

## An acutely unwell person needs assessment before a marker returns

NICE's current adult suspected sepsis guidance bases assessment on the history, examination, physiological observations and risk factors. People may be seriously ill without a high temperature, and a change noticed by the patient or family can be important. The process is not reduced to whether CRP or PCT crosses a single threshold. Severe deterioration, confusion, difficulty breathing or other alarming changes require urgent medical assessment, rather than waiting at home for a repeat inflammatory panel. This distinction keeps the time scales clear: recognition of possible organ dysfunction, collection of appropriate tests and interpretation of a later trend are separate tasks. Once the immediate problem is addressed, a series of measurements may help monitor progress. A favorable laboratory trend should be understood alongside the person's actual recovery and any evidence that the source of illness remains uncontrolled. [18]

## New visual symptoms have a separate urgent pathway

NHS guidance on temporal arteritis explains why suspected disease is treated urgently: loss of blood supply can cause permanent blindness or stroke. New visual symptoms, especially with a new headache, scalp tenderness or pain while chewing, need prompt clinical assessment. The clinician may arrange a same day eye assessment when vision is affected and begin treatment before all confirmatory results are available. This is a clinical response to the possible consequence of the disease, not a universal emergency category assigned to every mildly high ESR or CRP. For routine monitoring, retain the test name, units, date, symptoms and medicines, and ask what change would alter the treatment plan. For an important new symptom, report the symptom rather than relying on the reassurance of an old or newly normal marker. [19]

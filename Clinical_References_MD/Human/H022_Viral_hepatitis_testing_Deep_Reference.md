## The word hepatitis does not identify the cause

Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver. Viral hepatitis testing investigates specific infections, while other blood tests assess liver injury and function. The familiar hepatitis A, B and C viruses are different organisms with different testing pathways. Hepatitis A does not become chronic; hepatitis B and C can persist and lead to cirrhosis, meaning substantial liver scarring, or liver cancer. Many infected people initially have no symptoms. A and B are vaccine-preventable, while C can usually be cured with antiviral treatment. A positive result therefore needs its full name and context before its meaning can be explained. This chapter explains human testing, mainly United States pathways, including the often-omitted hepatitis D and E viruses. [1]

## What is actually included in the sample request

Most routine investigations use a blood sample. Serology measures antibodies or viral proteins through selective binding reactions; molecular assays detect viral genetic material. Mayo's acute hepatitis profile illustrates why the order name matters: it includes hepatitis A IgM, hepatitis B surface antigen and core IgM, and hepatitis C antibody with RNA reflex testing. The serologic components use electrochemiluminescence, converting binding reactions into measurable light. This acute profile is not the same as screening for hepatitis B immunity, and it does not automatically test for D or E. A negative panel answers only the questions included in that panel. The clinician should review the component results, exposure timing and whether indicated follow-up tests were completed. [25]

## Hepatitis A: recent illness versus immunity

For suspected hepatitis A, the relevant antibody is immunoglobulin M against hepatitis A virus, abbreviated IgM anti-HAV. IgG is a different antibody class associated with past infection or vaccination. Total anti-HAV combines classes, so a positive total result alone cannot establish acute hepatitis A. CDC recommends IgM testing when symptoms and clinical assessment suggest this infection, rather than routine screening of well people. IgM commonly persists for months and can occasionally react falsely, so positive does not supply an exact acquisition date. An unexpected result may require reassessment. Confirmed cases prompt public health review because exposed contacts may benefit from time-sensitive prevention; readers should contact a clinician promptly after a recognized exposure. [2]

A 2021 report described a 56-year-old woman initially diagnosed with hepatitis A after a positive IgM result despite lacking typical symptoms. Abnormal liver tests fluctuated over the following eighteen months. Further investigation, including biopsy, supported autoimmune hepatitis. HAV RNA was negative in current and stored specimens, and the authors concluded that the IgM result had been misleading. After two months of corticosteroid treatment, her liver tests normalized and HAV IgM became negative. The earlier diagnosis had resulted in isolation, distress and delayed recognition of the immune disease. The report does not quantify how often this occurs. It shows why persistent or clinically inconsistent IgM reactivity warrants investigation, rather than repeated acceptance of the same explanation. [3]

## Hepatitis B starts with three complementary markers

CDC recommends at least one hepatitis B screen for all adults aged 18 or older using the triple panel. Hepatitis B surface antigen, HBsAg, is a viral protein; anti-HBs is antibody against that surface protein; total anti-HBc is antibody against the viral core. Together they help distinguish current infection, previous infection, vaccine response and susceptibility. Vaccination alone does not produce core antibody. Periodic testing remains appropriate for susceptible people with ongoing exposures. Screening is useful even without symptoms or disclosed risk. Additional hepatitis B e antigen and its antibody, HBeAg and anti-HBe, help characterize viral activity after infection is identified. They do not replace the triple panel or measure liver scarring. Persistence of HBsAg for at least six months supports chronic infection. [4]

The common patterns are understandable when read as a set. HBsAg and core antibody together support current infection; core IgM helps investigate recent infection, although it can also appear during a flare of longstanding disease. Negative HBsAg with positive core and surface antibodies usually indicates resolved infection. Surface antibody alone can reflect vaccine immunity when a completed series is documented. All three negative results commonly indicate susceptibility in someone never vaccinated. These patterns require clinical interpretation: recent vaccination can transiently produce HBsAg, and antibodies can wane. An anti-HBs concentration of at least 10 mIU/mL is interpreted in relation to completion of the vaccine series and the reason for testing. [5]

## An isolated core antibody needs explanation

Isolated core antibody means total anti-HBc is positive while HBsAg and anti-HBs are negative. CDC's dedicated interpretation guidance lists several possibilities, including previous infection with waning surface antibody, a false-positive core result, occult infection and a viral surface variant missed by an assay. Occult infection means viral genetic material is present despite negative surface antigen testing. The person's immune condition, prior results and exposure background help determine which further tests are needed; HBV DNA can be important. A person about to receive immune-suppressing treatment needs particular attention because previously controlled infection may reactivate. This pattern should not automatically be translated into either active contagious hepatitis or complete reassurance. A clear follow-up plan is more useful than an unexplained positive label. [6]

## Viral DNA measures replication, not the whole liver

HBV DNA testing amplifies selected viral DNA sequences using polymerase chain reaction, or PCR. A quantitative result estimates the concentration, commonly reported in international units per milliliter, whereas a qualitative result describes detection. Mayo's serum assay explains that a concentration below its quantification range can be detected without being measured accurately as a number. That differs from an undetected result. Both depend on the assay and specimen. Viral DNA helps assess activity and treatment response, but it is not a direct percentage of liver damage. Interpretation combines viral markers with liver chemistry, examination and assessment of fibrosis, the build-up of scar tissue. A low treated viral load should not lead someone to stop prescribed therapy independently. [7]

## Pregnancy and infant follow-up are connected but different

CDC recommends HBsAg screening during each pregnancy. When a pregnant person has hepatitis B, the care team coordinates maternal evaluation and the infant's prevention plan. The infant later needs post-vaccination serologic testing that contains HBsAg and anti-HBs only, rather than the adult triple panel. Recommended timing is age nine to twelve months, or one to two months after completion when the vaccine series is delayed. These tests assess both infection status and the immune response to vaccination. Testing too early can mislead, and a record of vaccine administration does not itself establish the outcome. Families should receive a scheduled pediatric follow-up and an explanation of who will review the results. [8]

## Vaccine antigen can be detected without infection

Khoo and colleagues described five people with advanced kidney disease whose HBsAg became positive after hepatitis B vaccination. Three were receiving hemodialysis and two had not started dialysis. All had negative HBV DNA testing, and repeat surface antigen results became negative. The median first positive test was four days after vaccination; the median documented negative repeat was thirteen days, with variable testing intervals. HBsAg remained negative during reported follow-up extending up to two years. Dialysis equipment was temporarily isolated while results were clarified. This selected series is not a population frequency estimate or a universal clearance timetable. It illustrates the importance of telling the testing team about recent vaccination while still investigating a possible genuine infection. [9]

## Transferred antibodies can resemble a past infection

Parker and colleagues reported a 50-year-old man whose hepatitis B core and surface antibodies became positive after intravenous immunoglobulin, a treatment prepared from pooled donor antibodies. HBsAg and HBV DNA were negative. The apparent past infection caused substantial marital distress and nearly led to unnecessary antiviral treatment before further immune treatment. A remaining vial from the administered batch contained the same antibodies, and the patient's antibody level fell on repeat testing, supporting passive transfer rather than his own response to HBV infection. Clarification occurred before the antiviral was started; the authors reported that the brief delay in arthritis treatment had no consequential effect because first-line treatment was working well. Antibodies were genuinely present, but their origin changed the interpretation. [10]

## Reactivation can have serious outcomes

Controlled or resolved hepatitis B can become active again in particular treatment settings. In a primary FDA adverse-event investigation, Bersoff-Matcha and colleagues examined twenty-nine reports of HBV reactivation during direct-acting antiviral treatment for hepatitis C. Two patients died and one required liver transplantation. Diagnosis and treatment of reactivation were delayed in seven reports and possibly seven others. These voluntarily submitted reports had incomplete information and no denominator of everyone treated, so their counts cannot provide an individual risk percentage. The indexed abstract was available for this chapter, rather than the complete report. The clinical lesson is to establish HBV status and an appropriate monitoring plan before HCV treatment, while recognizing that effective hepatitis C treatment remains beneficial. [11]

## Hepatitis C: antibody exposure and RNA infection

A reactive hepatitis C antibody test, anti-HCV, can represent current infection, a previous infection that cleared, or a false antibody reaction. CDC's standard laboratory pathway automatically follows a reactive antibody result with HCV RNA testing. Detectable RNA establishes current infection; reactive antibody with undetectable RNA usually means no current infection. Recent exposure, immune suppression or concern about specimen integrity can justify further investigation. RNA usually becomes detectable within one to two weeks after exposure, whereas antibodies often take eight to eleven weeks. For possible exposure within six months, clinicians should consider RNA even if antibody is negative. These are biological detection intervals, not instructions to delay seeking care until a particular week. [12]

Completing the second step is a measurable patient-care outcome. CDC's 2023 operational guidance describes Veterans Health Administration facilities where only 64 percent of people completed the testing sequence when RNA required a separate visit, compared with 98 percent in facilities using single-visit collection strategies. These are facility observations, not a randomized comparison proving that one change alone caused the difference. They nevertheless show the practical cost of an unfinished antibody result. Reflex testing means that the laboratory performs RNA testing automatically when the antibody reaction calls for it. Collecting all required samples at the initial visit reduces the chance that transportation, missed contact or a second appointment prevents someone from learning whether current infection is present. [13]

## Reading a molecular result below the measuring range

HCV RNA assays commonly use reverse transcription PCR: RNA is copied into DNA, selected sequences are amplified, and a fluorescent signal is compared with standards. Mayo's quantitative assay distinguishes an undetected result from RNA detected below the range that can be accurately quantified. For that assay, a result reported below 15 IU/mL can mean detectable RNA, not an interchangeable negative. An inconclusive reaction may require another specimen. The laboratory manages collection and transport requirements because RNA integrity affects the answer. Keep the actual wording, units and assay with the report. A screening antibody index, an RNA concentration and a liver-enzyme value measure different things; their numbers should not be compared as if they were one scale. [14]

## Recent infection can fluctuate

The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) emphasize that HCV RNA alone cannot distinguish acute infection from longstanding infection. A documented negative baseline followed by detectable RNA, or antibody conversion, can help establish a recent acquisition. During acute infection, RNA may fluctuate or briefly become undetectable, so one negative RNA result does not always establish spontaneous clearance. Alanine aminotransferase, abbreviated ALT, can be normal or fluctuate as well, and other liver insults can raise it. Clinicians combine the timeline, repeated tests when indicated and competing diagnoses. Current guidance favors treating diagnosed acute infection without waiting for a hoped-for spontaneous clearance. [15]

## Point-of-care testing can shorten the path to care

In June 2024, FDA authorized the Xpert HCV test for adults with symptoms or exposure risk in appropriately certified point-of-care services. It detects HCV RNA from fingerstick blood and can provide results in about an hour, potentially allowing diagnosis and linkage to treatment during one encounter. This is a molecular test, so calling every rapid hepatitis test an antibody test would be incorrect. It is also different from a home self-test. The benefit depends on having a service ready to explain the result, evaluate the patient and arrange treatment. A fast instrument cannot by itself complete care when referral or follow-up is missing. The service should confirm availability and suitability. [16]

## Cure is checked with RNA after treatment

For chronic hepatitis C, undetectable or nonquantifiable HCV RNA twelve weeks or longer after treatment completion defines sustained virologic response, often abbreviated SVR12, and is consistent with cure. An end-of-treatment result alone is a different time point. Antibody usually remains positive after cure, so repeating antibody does not measure treatment success or diagnose reinfection. When ongoing exposure warrants reassessment, clinicians use RNA. Cure does not erase established cirrhosis or every future liver risk: people with cirrhosis still need appropriate surveillance for liver cancer. The follow-up therefore separates several outcomes—viral clearance, residual scar-related risk and new exposure—rather than treating one successful result as the end of every form of care. [17]

## An exposed infant needs an age-specific test

Maternal hepatitis C antibodies can persist in an infant without proving infant infection. CDC's 2023 recommendations use NAT for HCV RNA at age two to six months for perinatally exposed infants. An undetectable result at or after two months does not require further follow-up unless clinically warranted; detectable RNA leads to pediatric specialist consultation. Previously untested children aged seven to seventeen months should receive RNA testing, while those aged eighteen months or older begin with antibody and RNA reflex testing if reactive. The purpose of earlier testing is also practical: more exposed children can be identified and linked to care before they disappear from follow-up. [18]

## An occupational exposure starts a separate timeline

After a healthcare worker's blood exposure, CDC recommends prompt baseline assessment of both the source patient and worker, with HCV RNA preferred for source testing in relevant circumstances. When follow-up is indicated, RNA is checked at three to six weeks and a final antibody assessment at four to six months, with appropriate RNA follow-up or substitution when baseline antibody was already positive. These dates are specific to this occupational pathway. A baseline result cannot identify infection acquired moments earlier. CDC does not recommend routine hepatitis C post-exposure prophylaxis; the strategy is to detect and treat infection if it occurs. Prompt exposure assessment still matters because hepatitis B and HIV require their own prevention decisions. [19]

## Hepatitis D requires the hepatitis B context

Hepatitis D virus depends on hepatitis B surface proteins, so it is investigated within the care of someone with HBV. WHO's 2024 guideline conditionally favors offering total anti-HDV testing to all HBsAg-positive people; where resources prevent that, specified higher-risk groups are prioritized. A positive antibody test is followed by NAT for HDV RNA to establish active infection. Antibody exposure and active viral replication are separate findings here too. The recommendations have limited-certainty evidence, and RNA assay access and standardization remain challenges. An unexpectedly severe course or substantial liver inflammation despite low HBV DNA should prompt consideration of another cause, including HDV. Low HBV DNA does not exclude clinically important disease driven by a second virus. [20]

## Hepatitis E is not confined to overseas travel

Hepatitis E can follow contaminated water or, for certain genotypes, animal-to-human exposure through undercooked meat. WHO describes diagnosis using specific IgM antibodies and, where needed, HEV RNA in blood or stool. RNA testing is particularly important in uncommon or chronic presentations. Immune-suppressed people, especially organ transplant recipients, can develop persistent infection. The geographic and exposure pattern matters, but absence of travel does not exclude locally acquired disease. Symptomatic hepatitis during pregnancy deserves prompt assessment because HEV can cause severe maternal and fetal complications in relevant settings. The different genotypes and populations prevent one mortality estimate or testing timetable from being applied indiscriminately to every person with a positive result. [21]

Davern and colleagues tested stored samples from 318 United States patients thought to have drug-induced liver injury. Nine had HEV IgM and four also had detectable HEV RNA. Reassessment favored hepatitis E over medication injury in seven of the nine, while two remained more likely to have drug-related injury. Six-month specimens were available for only four people: all four had undetectable RNA, including two whose initial RNA was positive, with falling IgM and rising IgG supporting recent infection. These findings do not show that every suspected medication reaction is hepatitis E or that all nine had equivalent certainty. Testing changed causal attribution, with follow-up limited to the specimens actually available. [22]

## Forensic attribution needs clinical and independent evidence

The New Hampshire healthcare outbreak investigation identified the outbreak HCV strain in thirty-two of 1,074 cardiac catheterization laboratory patients tested. Three carried more than one strain. Molecular comparison was combined with interviews, medication discrepancies and records linking a technician to exposure days. Limiting testing to patients with documented direct care by that worker would have missed fifteen of the thirty-two confirmed cases. The report later recorded the technician's guilty plea and thirty-nine-year prison sentence; individual patients' treatment outcomes were not its focus. This is a relevant forensic lesson: antibody or RNA establishes diagnostic evidence, while reconstructing transmission requires additional records and molecular interpretation. A newly discovered positive test, by itself, cannot prove where, when or from whom infection was acquired. [23]

## Clinical deterioration can be urgent before the viral label is settled

Severe acute liver illness is assessed while the cause is still being investigated. AASLD stresses prompt viral and autoimmune testing when acute liver failure is suspected, with particular attention to encephalopathy, meaning altered brain function associated with liver failure. New confusion, unusual drowsiness or rapid deterioration in someone with jaundice warrants urgent medical assessment rather than waiting for a routine hepatitis panel. Clinicians also examine clotting function, other laboratory findings, medicines and possible nonviral causes. The urgency comes from the person's condition, not a universal antigen value or antibody deadline. Conversely, an asymptomatic screening result still deserves reliable follow-up even when it does not call for emergency care. The goal is both an accurate cause and an appropriate response to current illness. [24]

# V004: Canine heartworm antigen and microfilaria testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog
- **Clinical Category**: Parasitology and infectious disease
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft on 2026-11-05; no independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: Heartworm test, Dirofilaria immitis testing, Modified Knott test

## Plain-Language Overview

The two tests look for different evidence of the same infection. Using them together reduces the chance that one test's blind spot will be mistaken for a clean bill of health.

## Clinical Purpose

Uses complementary blood tests to assess heartworm infection. Antigen testing detects worm-associated proteins; microfilaria testing looks for circulating offspring in blood.

## How the Test Works

Antigen assays use antibodies to recognize worm proteins, associated predominantly with mature female worms. A microfilaria concentration test separates and preserves small circulating worms for microscopic examination and identification. The dog's infection stage and preventive history affect the evidence available to each method.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

A veterinary professional collects blood. An antigen assay is performed in clinic or a laboratory, and blood is examined for microfilariae, often using a concentration technique such as a modified Knott test.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Provide the dog's age, preventive medication history, missed doses, travel or relocation, and previous results. Timing after possible infection influences whether testing can detect it.

## Interpreting Results

Antigen detection and microfilaria detection are reported separately. Unexpected or positive findings may require verification and further assessment by the veterinarian; antigen results do not count adult worms.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

A positive antigen test should be verified by an appropriate additional method before treatment decisions. Discordant antigen and microfilaria findings require investigation rather than averaging the results. Routine heat treatment of samples is not recommended for canine screening in the AHS summary; reference-laboratory evaluation may be considered for selected discrepancies. Further clinical assessment and imaging help assess the effects of infection.

## Limitations & Confounders

Early infections, low worm burdens, or male-only infections can yield no detectable antigen. Some infected dogs have no circulating microfilariae. A negative result from either test alone does not establish freedom from infection.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Blood collection can cause brief discomfort or bruising.

## Species & Population Notes

This is a canine entry. Feline heartworm diagnosis requires a separate approach; canine test interpretation must not be transferred directly to cats.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Record assay platform and both results. Microfilariae need identification because other filarial species can circulate in canine blood. Preserve discordant results and document the follow-up method rather than replacing the original report.

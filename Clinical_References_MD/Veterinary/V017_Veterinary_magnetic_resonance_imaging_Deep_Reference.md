> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How do complementary T1, T2, FLAIR, T2*, and diffusion-weighted MRI sequences distinguish brain and spinal cord diseases in dogs and cats, and why do microchip susceptibility artifacts, general anesthesia, and cerebrospinal fluid correlation shape every veterinary MRI report?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Manage status epilepticus, intracranial hypertension, and progressive myelomalacia risk before and during MRI anesthesia

Because veterinary MRI requires thirty to ninety minutes of general anesthesia inside a magnet bore where direct hands-on patient access is restricted, dogs and cats with status epilepticus, Cushing reflex (bradycardia with systemic hypertension from elevated intracranial pressure), or acute tetraparesis with hypoventilation require immediate anticonvulsant control, hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline), and normocapnic mechanical ventilation with MRI-conditional monitoring equipment before scanning.

## Why MRI is the reference standard for the canine and feline nervous system

Whereas computed tomography relies on X-ray attenuation by electron-dense calcium and iodine, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) uses a strong static magnetic field (commonly 1.5 Tesla or 3.0 Tesla in high-field veterinary referral centers, or 0.2 to 0.4 Tesla in low-field permanent-magnet units), radiofrequency pulses, and switched spatial gradient coils to measure the relaxation properties and proton density of hydrogen nuclei in water and lipid molecules. Because the brain and spinal cord are encased within thick cortical bone of the calvarium and vertebral canal, X-ray beam hardening degrades CT images of the caudal brainstem, cerebellum, and non-mineralized spinal cord parenchyma. MRI receives virtually zero signal from dense cortical bone, eliminating bone beam-hardening artifact completely and delivering unmatched soft-tissue contrast between cerebral grey matter, myelinated white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, intervertebral disc nucleus pulposus, and epidural fat. [1, 2]

Before ordering a brain or spinal MRI in a dog or cat, a thorough veterinary neurological examination must first localize the lesion to a specific neuroanatomical region: forebrain (cerebrum/thalamus), brainstem, cerebellum, vestibular system, or one of the four functional spinal cord segments (C1–C5, C6–T2, T3–L3, or L4–S3). Because high-resolution MRI acquires thin slices over a targeted field of view using surface or phased-array radiofrequency coils matched to the region of interest, scanning the entire body of a large dog is neither diagnostically optimal nor safe for anesthesia duration; an accurate neuroanatomical localization ensures the magnet coils and pulse sequences are centered over the exact anatomical compartment responsible for the animal's clinical deficits. [2, 3]

## What each core MRI pulse sequence reveals about tissue pathology

No single MRI sequence can identify a tissue type in isolation; veterinary MRI interpretation compares signal intensities across a standardized multi-sequence panel. On T1-weighted images (short repetition time TR and short echo time TE), fat, subacute methemoglobin, proteinaceous fluid, melanin, and gadolinium contrast agents appear bright (hyperintense), while cerebrospinal fluid and edema appear dark (hypointense), providing crisp anatomical detail. On T2-weighted images (long TR and long TE), both free cerebrospinal fluid and intracellular or interstitial tissue edema appear bright, making T2 the primary screening sequence for parenchymal inflammation, ischemia, neoplasia, syringomyelia, and loss of normal hydrated water signal within degenerated intervertebral discs. [1, 4]

Three specialized pulse sequences resolve ambiguities left by standard T1 and T2 images. T2-Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) uses an inversion recovery pulse timed to null the signal of free pure water (cerebrospinal fluid in the ventricles and subarachnoid space turns black) while keeping bound water in periventricular or cortical parenchymal edema bright. Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) or chemical fat-saturation suppresses bright bone-marrow and epidural fat signal on T2-weighted spine and musculoskeletal scans so vertebral osteomyelitis, discospondylitis, paraspinal myositis, or nerve-root tumors stand out brightly. Finally, T2*-weighted gradient recalled echo (GRE) or Susceptibility-Weighted Imaging (SWI) detects microscopic magnetic field distortions caused by paramagnetic iron in deoxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin, and hemosiderin, making hemorrhages, cavernous malformations, and hemorrhagic infarcts bloom as dark signal voids. [1, 4, 5]

## Diffusion-weighted imaging and gadolinium contrast enhancement

Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) and its quantitative Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) map measure the microscopic Brownian motion of water molecules within extracellular and intracellular spaces. When an acute ischemic stroke—such as a cerebellar artery infarct in a Cavalier King Charles Spaniel or Greyhound—starves neurons and glia of adenosine triphosphate, membrane sodium-potassium pumps fail within minutes and water shifts into swollen cells (cytotoxic edema). Restricted water diffusion appears bright on DWI and dark (hypointense) on the ADC map within hours of vascular occlusion, distinguishing an acute cerebrovascular accident from vasogenic edema or T2 'shine-through' artifact. [5, 6]

Intravenous administration of paramagnetic gadolinium chelates (typically 0.1 mmol/kg of macrocyclic or linear agents such as gadobutrol, gadoteridol, or gadopentetate dimeglumine) shortens the T1 relaxation time of adjacent water protons, producing bright enhancement on post-contrast T1-weighted images wherever vascularity is increased or the tight junctions of the blood-brain or blood-spinal-cord barrier are disrupted. Extra-axial tumors that arise outside the blood-brain barrier—most notably feline and canine meningiomas, pituitary macroadenomas, choroid plexus tumors, and peripheral nerve sheath tumors—typically exhibit intense, uniform contrast enhancement, often accompanied by a dural tail sign, whereas low-grade intra-axial gliomas or non-infectious meningoencephalitides may show ring enhancement, patchy leptomeningeal enhancement, or no enhancement at all. [1, 7]

## Intervertebral disc disease, spinal cord hyperintensity, and vascular myelopathies

In canine spinal disease, MRI is the definitive modality for distinguishing compressive intervertebral disc extrusions and protrusions from non-compressive vascular or inflammatory myelopathies. In Hansen Type I intervertebral disc extrusion, sagittal and transverse T2-weighted images demonstrate loss of the normal bright hyperintense signal of the nucleus pulposus within the parent disc space, narrowing of the intervertebral disc space, and a hypointense extradural mass compressing and displacing the hyperintense spinal cord and obliterating the dorsal and ventral subarachnoid cerebrospinal fluid columns and epidural fat. [3, 8]

Equally importantly, MRI uniquely visualizes non-compressive acute myelopathies that have completely normal survey radiographs and non-contrast CT scans: Fibrocartilaginous Embolic Myelopathy (FCEM, an ischemic spinal cord infarct caused by embolization of nucleus pulposus material into spinal vessels, classically presenting as peracute, non-painful, asymmetric paresis in large or miniature dogs during exercise), Acute Non-Compressive Nucleus Pulposus Extrusion (ANNPE, a high-velocity 'low-volume, high-impact' contusion from normal hydrated disc material hitting the cord without leaving residual extradural compression), and Hydrated Nucleus Pulposus Extrusion (HNPE, often forming a characteristic ventral extradural 'seagull-wing' T2-hyperintense lesion in the cervical spine). Furthermore, measuring the longitudinal extent of intramedullary T2 hyperintensity relative to the length of the L2 vertebral body provides critical prognostic information in paraplegic, deep-pain-negative dogs for risk of ascending-descending progressive myelomalacia. [3, 8, 9]

## Intracranial tumors, MUO, and feline infectious peritonitis

In dogs and cats presenting with new-onset seizures, circling, obtundation, or cranial nerve deficits, brain MRI differentiates structural neoplasia, inflammatory meningoencephalitis, congenital malformations (hydrocephalus, Chiari-like malformation and syringomyelia), and metabolic or toxic encephalopathies. Meningiomas—the most common primary brain tumor in cats and in dolichocephalic dogs—appear as broad-based, extra-axial masses along the calvarium, falx cerebri, or tentorium cerebelli that displace the brain parenchyma inward, show strong gadolinium enhancement, and in cats frequently induce adjacent calvarial hyperostosis and may occur as multiple synchronous meningiomas. Intra-axial gliomas (astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas), strongly predisposed in brachycephalic breeds (Boxers, French Bulldogs, Boston Terriers, English Bulldogs), arise within the piriform, frontal, or temporal lobes with mass effect, perilesional vasogenic edema, and variable or ring-like contrast enhancement. [1, 7, 10]

Inflammatory brain diseases share overlapping MRI features with gliomas and require cerebrospinal fluid analysis and infectious disease PCR/serology for synthesis. Meningoencephalitis of Unknown Origin (MUO—encompassing granulomatous meningoencephalomyelitis, necrotizing meningoencephalitis in Pugs and Maltese, and necrotizing leukoencephalitis in Yorkshire Terriers and Chihuahuas) typically produces multifocal, asymmetric T2 and FLAIR hyperintensities across the forebrain or brainstem; however, in roughly 7 to 25 percent of dogs with histologically or CSF-confirmed inflammatory meningoencephalitis, the brain MRI is unremarkable. In young cats with neurologic Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP), high-field post-contrast T1 and FLAIR MRI reveals a highly characteristic triad of marked ependymal and periventricular contrast enhancement, meningeal and choroid plexus enhancement, and obstructive secondary hydrocephalus with cervical syringomyelia caused by pyogranulomatous ventriculitis and aqueductal stenosis. [10, 11, 12]

## Why idiopathic epilepsy is a diagnosis of exclusion—and post-ictal MRI mimics

According to the International Veterinary Epilepsy Task Force (IVETF) consensus statements, Tier I confidence for canine Idiopathic Epilepsy rests on onset of recurrent generalized seizures between 6 months and 6 years of age, a normal inter-ictal neurological examination, and unremarkable hematology, serum biochemistry, bile acids, and urinalysis; Tier II confidence adds fasting/postprandial bile acids, a standardized epilepsy-specific brain MRI protocol (including thin-slice dorsal and transverse T2, FLAIR, T2*, DWI, and pre/post-contrast T1 images angled perpendicular and parallel to the hippocampus), and cerebrospinal fluid analysis confirming the absence of structural or inflammatory brain lesions. [13, 14]

A crucial diagnostic trap on brain MRI performed shortly after cluster seizures or status epilepticus is seizure-induced reversible post-ictal brain change. Prolonged seizure activity causes transient excitotoxic glutamate release, focal hyperperfusion, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and cytotoxic/vasogenic edema targeting the piriform lobes, amygdala, hippocampus, cingulate gyrus, and thalamus. On MRI, these post-ictal lesions appear as bilateral (or occasionally asymmetric) T2 and FLAIR hyperintensities with mild mass effect, sulcal effacement, variable restricted diffusion, and patchy contrast enhancement that can be mistaken for glioma, necrotizing encephalitis, or thiamine deficiency. Recognizing the characteristic anatomical distribution in the piriform/hippocampal cortex and documenting complete resolution on a follow-up MRI weeks after seizure control prevents misdiagnosing reversible post-ictal edema as fatal brain neoplasia. [13, 15]

## Microchip susceptibility artifact and MRI safety screening in dogs and cats

Every veterinary patient entering the MRI suite must undergo strict ferromagnetic safety screening—both for human staff entering Zone IV and for metallic foreign bodies or implants inside the animal. While modern titanium and implant-grade 316L stainless-steel orthopedic bone plates, tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) plates, and hemoclips are generally MRI-conditional (safe against physical dislodgement at 1.5T and 3.0T, though capable of localized radiofrequency heating), ferromagnetic steel pellets, BB gun projectiles, vascular coils, cardiac pacemakers, and even metallic dirt or barium/iron debris on the haircoat can experience magnetic torque, heating, or severe image distortion. [1, 16]

The single most common veterinary-specific MRI artifact is magnetic susceptibility distortion caused by the subcutaneous identification microchip implanted between the dorsal scapulae in dogs and cats. Although veterinary microchips do not migrate or injure the animal at 1.5T or 3.0T, the tiny ferrite coil inside the glass transponder severely distorts the local static magnetic field over a radius of three to six centimeters, creating a massive signal void and geometric warping that completely obliterates the cranial thoracic and caudal cervical vertebral canal (commonly C5 to T3) on spinal MRI, especially on gradient-echo T2* and fat-saturated sequences. When a dog presents with a C6–T2 or T3–L3 cranial thoracic myelopathy and the microchip sits directly dorsal to the lesion, taping traction on the loose dorsal cervical skin to pull the microchip laterally or cranially away from the spine—or in rare cases temporarily excising a superficial subcutaneous microchip under sterile prep—restores diagnostic visibility of the spinal cord. [16, 17]

## Anesthesia monitoring inside the bore and safe timing of CSF collection

Because high-field MRI scanning requires thirty to sixty minutes of complete immobility inside a long magnet bore, general anesthesia management requires specialized MRI-conditional non-ferromagnetic anesthesia machines, long breathing circuits, fiber-optic pulse oximetry, sidestream capnography, and invasive or oscillometric blood pressure monitors shielded against radiofrequency interference. Maintaining normocapnia (end-tidal CO2 between 35 and 40 mmHg) via mechanical ventilation is critical in patients with intracranial masses or edema, because hypercapnia from anesthetic hypoventilation causes cerebral vasodilation, raises intracranial pressure, and can precipitate tentorial or foramen magnum brain herniation during the scan. [1, 18]

In veterinary neurology workups, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) collection via cerebellomedullary cisternal or lumbar puncture is almost always performed under the same anesthetic episode immediately after the MRI scan rather than before it. Performing MRI first serves two vital purposes: first, it prevents iatrogenic needle-tract hemorrhage or meningeal contrast leakage from mimicking pathology on the MRI; second, and most importantly, it allows the neurologist and radiologist to inspect the sagittal and transverse T2 and FLAIR brain images for signs of elevated intracranial pressure—such as caudal transtentorial herniation, displacement of the cerebellar vermis through the foramen magnum, effacement of the cerebral sulci and mesencephalic aqueduct, or severe midline shift—in which case cisternal CSF tap is contraindicated or deferred to avoid fatal pressure-gradient brain herniation. [10, 18, 19]

## Documented clinical cohorts and outcome prediction evidence

Large veterinary clinical studies have quantified both the diagnostic sensitivity and the prognostic power of MRI in canine and feline neurological disease. In Ito and colleagues' study of 71 paraplegic dogs with absent deep pain perception (nociception) secondary to acute thoracolumbar intervertebral disc extrusion undergoing decompressive hemilaminectomy, the length of intramedullary spinal cord T2 hyperintensity on preoperative sagittal T2-weighted MRI strongly predicted functional recovery and risk of fatal progressive myelomalacia: 100 percent of dogs without intramedullary T2 hyperintensity recovered ambulation, compared with 55 percent of dogs with T2 hyperintensity less than 6 times the length of the L2 vertebral body, and only 10 percent of dogs whose T2 hyperintensity exceeded 6 times the length of L2 (with 45 percent of the >=6x L2 group developing progressive myelomalacia). Subsequent studies confirmed that combining T2 hyperintensity length with absence of cerebrospinal fluid signal on Single-Shot Fast Spin-Echo (SSFSE/HASTE) myelographic sequences further refines myelomalacia risk. [9, 20]

For intracranial disease, Wolff and colleagues evaluated the accuracy of high-field MRI in 115 dogs with histologically confirmed brain lesions (gliomas, meningiomas, pituitary tumors, choroid plexus tumors, and inflammatory/vascular lesions): MRI achieved 94.4 percent sensitivity and 95.5 percent specificity for detecting neoplastic versus non-neoplastic brain disease, yet distinguishing specific glioma grade or separating focal granulomatous meningoencephalomyelitis from glioma by MRI appearance alone carried a 10 to 24 percent overlap rate, proving why stereotactic brain biopsy, CSF analysis, and longitudinal therapeutic response remain essential companions to neuroimaging. Similarly, in an individually documented case of a 4-year-old Golden Retriever evaluated after 48 hours of severe cluster seizures, initial brain MRI showed bilateral symmetric T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity and swelling of the piriform lobes and hippocampi; rather than euthanizing for suspected bilateral glioma or necrotizing encephalitis, anticonvulsant control was instituted and a repeat MRI 10 weeks later confirmed 100 percent resolution of the piriform and hippocampal lesions, establishing reversible post-ictal edema associated with idiopathic epilepsy. [15, 21, 22]

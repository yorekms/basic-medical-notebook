# V027: Feline leukemia virus and immunodeficiency virus testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease testing
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 5)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a positive feline retrovirus test establish, and what still needs to be learned about the cat?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical state determines urgency

Breathing difficulty requires immediate veterinary assessment.

## Two viruses and two different screening targets

A common combination blood test checks for feline leukemia virus, abbreviated FeLV, and feline immunodeficiency virus, abbreviated FIV. The two result windows answer different questions. FeLV screening usually looks for p27 antigen, a protein belonging to the virus. FIV screening usually looks for antibodies, proteins the cat's immune system makes in response to an exposure. Antigen and antibody are therefore different kinds of evidence. Neither result, on its own, describes every illness the cat has or predicts its remaining life. The AAFP guidelines recommend interpreting testing alongside exposure history, examination and, when needed, additional methods or repeat samples. This chapter concerns domestic cats. Although some infection names resemble human diseases, a feline result must not be interpreted using a human test algorithm, human prognosis or a human treatment regimen. [1]

## How FeLV exposure connects to the testing question

FeLV is a retrovirus, meaning its genetic material can become incorporated into an infected cat's cells. Persistently infected cats can shed infectious virus in saliva and other secretions. Close contact, grooming, bites and transmission from a mother to her kittens can matter. The relevant history consequently includes other cats, outdoor access, previous households and the mother's status when known. Young kittens are particularly susceptible. An apparently healthy animal can still be infected, so appearance alone cannot replace testing when the question matters. Conversely, exposure does not guarantee the same outcome in every cat. The immune response can limit infection to different degrees. Knowing that a cat shared a household with an infected cat is a reason to assess risk; it is not the same observation as showing viral antigen or genetic material in that individual. [2]

## FIV exposure has a different usual pattern

FIV primarily spreads through deep bite wounds that introduce infected saliva and cells. This makes fighting history important, especially in cats with unsupervised outdoor access. It does not mean a particular fight can be reconstructed from a positive antibody result. Many infected cats remain outwardly healthy for years, while others develop problems associated with impaired immune defenses or secondary disease. FIV is highly species specific; the familiar comparison with HIV explains a broad biological relationship, not a risk of a person catching feline FIV. The cat's mouth, skin, weight, appetite and other health findings still deserve their own assessment. An infection label should make the clinician more attentive to treatable problems. It should not turn every later symptom into presumed terminal viral disease without considering other explanations. [3]

## A negative result soon after exposure can be premature

Tests need time for their targets to become detectable. The FelineVMA toolkit describes FeLV antigen detection after the early exposure period and FIV antibody detection commonly requiring sixty days or longer. It advises repeat FIV testing after sixty days when an initially negative result needs follow up. These are diagnostic planning intervals, not waiting periods for an ill cat. The last plausible exposure matters: continued contact can create a new opportunity for infection after the original sample. A record should therefore preserve the date, assay, result and relevant contacts rather than only a permanent label such as negative. When results conflict, the veterinarian may choose another method instead of repeating the identical test indefinitely. A single testing schedule cannot cover every newly acquired kitten, exposed adult, vaccinated cat and sick patient equally well. [4]

## Progressive and regressive FeLV infection are different states

The 2025 Australia and New Zealand FeLV guidelines distinguish persistent active infection from infection controlled by the immune response. In progressive infection, circulating antigen usually remains detectable and the risk of associated illness is greater. In regressive infection, viral genetic material can remain integrated while blood antigen becomes undetectable. An early antigen positive result may therefore change over time. The guideline recommends confirmation using blood proviral DNA testing and later reassessment when classifying the course. Abortive infection is another outcome, in which direct routine tests can remain negative after effective immune control. These categories explain why the same phrase FeLV positive can conceal different biological states. They are clinical interpretations assembled from method and timing, rather than diagnoses that can always be assigned from one test strip. FeLV vaccination and maternal antibodies do not explain a positive FeLV antigen result in the way FIV antibodies can complicate FIV screening. [5]

## PCR finds a genetic target rather than every property of infection

Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, amplifies a selected genetic sequence so that very small quantities can be detected. For FeLV, a proviral DNA assay looks for viral material integrated within cells. That differs from measuring circulating p27 protein or viral RNA, the genetic material associated with active virus production. A provirus positive cat is not automatically shedding the same amount of infectious virus as a strongly antigen positive cat. Sample type matters as well: whole blood contains cells, while plasma is the liquid portion. The ABCD diagnostic guidance also describes immunofluorescence testing for cell associated p27 in neutrophils and platelets. These approaches examine related stages and compartments of infection. Discordance can therefore reflect biology as well as analytical limitations. A useful report identifies the actual target and specimen instead of treating every method as a universally interchangeable confirmation stamp. [6]

## Assay performance belongs to the tested product and population

Levy and colleagues compared four combination screening products using well characterized surplus clinical samples. FeLV antigen and FIV antibody performance differed between products, and the investigators calculated how predictive values change with infection prevalence. Sensitivity describes detection among infected samples; specificity describes exclusion among uninfected samples. A positive predictive value instead asks how often a positive result represents infection in the population being tested. Those are different quantities. Even a relatively uncommon false positive can become important when screening many low risk cats. The study's brands, versions, reference methods and sample selection form part of its result; historical percentages should not be advertised as permanent properties of every current product. For an unexpected positive in a healthy low risk cat, the practical implication is to confirm and contextualize the result before making a major decision. [7]

## Kittens and a difficult negative FIV result need context

FIV antibodies can reach kittens from their mother without proving that the kitten itself is infected. The Australia and New Zealand FIV guidelines explain why age, maternal history and repeat testing are needed before interpreting an antibody positive young kitten. Persistent positivity after the maternal antibody period is more concerning than an isolated early result. Molecular testing may help, but a negative FIV PCR is not an infallible exclusion test. Viral genetic diversity, low circulating amounts and the assay's design can affect detection. A veterinarian may use a combination of methods when clinical suspicion remains high. This is a useful example of two apparently opposite cautions coexisting: an antibody positive kitten may not be infected, and a PCR negative result may fail to settle a difficult case. Neither caution justifies ignoring the cat's actual exposure and health findings. [8]

## Vaccination can leave an antibody signal

Westman and colleagues followed antibody responses after primary and annual FIV vaccination with an inactivated whole virus product. Some antibody tests produced positive results in vaccinated, uninfected cats, and the pattern depended on the test and timing. This does not mean the vaccine had infected those cats. It means the assay was detecting an immune response that could arise without natural infection. Certain test formats were better able to distinguish previously vaccinated cats after the early vaccination period, while recent primary vaccination remained more difficult to interpret. Preserve the product and dates when records exist; absence of a current local vaccine does not erase an animal's earlier history. These findings concern the studied FIV vaccine and assays. They must not be generalized to FeLV antigen testing or turned into a claim that every positive feline retrovirus test is vaccine related. [9]

## A new test still requires an adequate reference assessment

A later Australian study evaluated the RapidSTATUS FIV antibody test in vaccinated and unvaccinated pet cats. Infection status was established using an algorithm combining several antibody tests, PCR and, for rare discordant cases, virus isolation. The testers were blinded to vaccination and infection status, reducing the risk that expectations would influence reading. The design illustrates why a claim that one test agreed with another is weaker than a comparison supported by several complementary methods. It also shows the difficulty of establishing a reference status when no single measurement answers every biological question. The findings apply to the evaluated kit, specimens and groups. They do not validate saliva substituted for blood, an untested brand or an owner interpreting a faint line from a photograph. Laboratories and clinicians need the instructions and validation evidence for the method actually being used. [10]

## An experimental transfusion study exposed the meaning of latency

Nesina and colleagues transfused blood from FeLV provirus carriers without detectable circulating antigen into susceptible recipient cats. All ten recipients became infected, and some developed progressive infection and fatal FeLV associated disease during follow up. The study demonstrated that antigen negative blood was not necessarily safe donor blood: infected cells could carry biologically important proviral DNA. This was an experiment with defined donors and recipients, not evidence that casual cohabitation carries the same risk as transfusion. Its clinical consequence is to distinguish screening a pet's general infection status from screening a blood donor, where a different exposure route makes latent infection relevant. The outcome also prevents an oversimplified interpretation of the word regressive. Controlled infection can have a much better everyday prognosis while still requiring particular precautions in situations that transfer infected cells directly. [11]

## Quantitative results can add prognostic information

Beall and colleagues prospectively followed 127 cats originally identified as FeLV antigen positive at shelter screening. The researchers used laboratory p27 concentrations and proviral DNA loads to classify groups at enrollment, then compared survival. Cats classified as high positive had a median survival of about 1.37 years; most low positive cats were still alive during the initial follow up. Median means the time at which half the studied group had experienced the outcome, not an expiry date for an individual cat. The categories depended on the study's assays and derived cutoffs. This was observational evidence linking measured viral burden with prognosis, not a trial showing that changing the number by a particular treatment improved survival. It supports a more informative discussion than a bare positive label, while leaving substantial variation between individual cats visible. [12]

## Longer follow up changes what can be said

The 2025 lifetime report followed the same cohort for up to eight years, rather than providing an independent replication. High positive cats had a median survival of 1.4 years, but some survived five years or longer. Nineteen of the 29 low positive cats and seven of the eight cats classified as cryptic or negative at enrollment were still alive at the end of follow up. Six cats were lost to follow up across the high and low groups. The study also found that sample type influenced detection of low positive cats. This longer record makes two points concrete: infection measurements carry prognostic information, and substantial individual exceptions remain. It does not establish that every low positive cat is free of future risk. Manufacturer affiliated authors and assay specific classification are also relevant when deciding how broadly to apply the findings. [13]

## FIV outcome evidence does not support a single fixed lifespan

Carlton and colleagues compared naturally FIV infected and uninfected pet cats in Australian clinical cohorts. After duplicate records were removed, survival data from 184 infected and 550 uninfected cats were compared. The study did not find a statistically significant difference in survival time between these groups. That finding does not prove identical risk under every housing condition or in every region. It is a retrospective comparison influenced by which cats were tested, recorded illnesses and decisions about euthanasia. Nevertheless, it is important evidence against automatically treating an FIV antibody positive cat as having only a short life remaining. The relevant next questions concern that cat's current disease, preventive care and living conditions. A diagnosis of infection can improve care without being mistaken for a complete prognosis or a reason to withhold investigation of a treatable illness. [14]

## A household study reported exposure without detected transmission

Litster studied cats in two rescue settings. In one, initial testing identified eight FIV positive and 130 negative cohabiting cats. Later testing of smaller subsets found no change in status; in the second setting, all 19 tested kittens from five FIV positive mothers were negative after weaning. These were the reported outcomes, not proof that transmission is impossible. Follow up was incomplete and the study described particular housing conditions. It cannot guarantee safety in a home where cats inflict deep bites. The useful distinction is between identifying a plausible route and measuring what happened in an observed group. A veterinary household plan should consider behavior and exposure, rather than assume that FeLV and FIV spread identically or infer an exact source of infection from the fact that two cats have lived together. [15]

## A real case improved after a severe blood abnormality

A published case described a cat with profound neutropenia, meaning a very low number of neutrophils, important white blood cells involved in defense against infection. A clinic screening test detected FeLV antigen and the report classified the infection as progressive. Prednisolone treatment produced hematologic remission. The cat relapsed four weeks after treatment had been stopped prematurely, then achieved complete and prolonged remission when treatment was reintroduced. The original abstract does not supply an unlimited future survival outcome, so none should be invented. This case is useful because the infection label did not make investigation and treatment of the blood abnormality pointless. It also does not show that corticosteroids are suitable for every FeLV infected cat. The underlying process, competing infection risks and response require professional assessment; the reported improvement is a possibility documented in one patient. [16]

## A contrasting case preserves both discordance and a fatal outcome

Park and colleagues reported a four year old cat with severe anemia, fever and abnormal immature blood cells. FeLV antigen testing was positive, while a subsequent PCR for viral genetic material was negative. The authors diagnosed acute erythroleukaemia, a rare malignancy involving red cell precursors, and described it in association with FeLV. The contradictory virus results remained part of the evidence; this chapter does not resolve them into an independently proven viral cause. Transfusion support and chemotherapy initially improved anemia and clinical signs, but the cat later deteriorated and died of shock 67 days after diagnosis. Postmortem tissue and marrow examination supplied additional evidence about the malignancy. The case demonstrates why temporary improvement and a final successful outcome are different claims. It also shows why neither a positive screening result nor a negative molecular result should erase the rest of a complex diagnostic investigation. [17]

## Forensic interpretation needs more than an infection label

Veterinary forensic standards emphasize a complete examination, relevant history, diagnostic records and preservation of evidence when an animal's condition is being assessed in a legal investigation. Applied to a retrovirus result, the record should identify the animal, collection date, specimen, assay and any confirmatory or subsequent findings. A positive test by itself cannot establish when infection occurred, which other animal transmitted it, or whether a particular person caused or knowingly ignored disease. Those are additional inferences. The leukemia case above likewise shows why a cause of illness should not be assumed from one viral marker when pathology and discordant testing add complexity. Medical care and relief of suffering still require attention while evidence is being clarified. This is a boundary on interpretation, not a claim that either retrovirus result supplies a standalone test for neglect. [18]

## Urgency follows the cat in front of the clinician

A stable cat with an unexpected positive screening result needs a confirmation and care plan. A cat struggling to breathe needs immediate veterinary assessment, regardless of whether the virus status is known. Cornell describes respiratory distress as a sign with many possible causes, including airway disease, heart disease, fluid around the lungs and tumors. An owner should not wait for a scheduled repeat retrovirus test before seeking help for breathing difficulty. Equally, that sign does not prove FeLV associated lymphoma or FIV associated infection without further evidence. The next decision concerns stabilizing and assessing the current problem. The laboratory result then contributes to a broader explanation and future care. Keeping those tasks connected prevents both unnecessary despair after a screening result and dangerous delay when a previously healthy looking cat becomes acutely unwell. [19]

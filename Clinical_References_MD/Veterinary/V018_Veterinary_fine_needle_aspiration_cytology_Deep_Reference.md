# V018: Veterinary fine needle aspiration cytology

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Cytology and tissue examination
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 4)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can a few cells from a lump reveal and what information remains outside the sample?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Urgency depends on the animal and the lesion

A cat with difficulty breathing needs immediate veterinary assessment while the cause is investigated. A cytology appointment or pending report does not replace that assessment.

## A small sample can answer a large question

Fine needle aspiration cytology examines cells collected through a thin needle from a lump, enlarged lymph node or selected internal lesion. Cytology means the study of cells; histopathology examines a piece of tissue with its internal arrangement preserved. This chapter concentrates on dogs and cats, because that is where the cited clinical evidence applies. The 2026 AAHA oncology guidelines emphasize that feeling or imaging a mass cannot reliably establish what it is. A fine needle sample may identify a fatty lump, inflammation or a round cell tumor such as lymphoma or a mast cell tumor. Other lesions require tissue biopsy. Learning which question has actually been answered is as important as learning the diagnosis: identifying tumor cells does not automatically establish their aggressiveness, complete surgical removal or spread elsewhere in the body. [1]

## What the pathologist is looking for

After staining, a veterinary clinical pathologist examines the kinds of cells, their appearance and their relationship to the material around them. Inflammation, infection and neoplasia, meaning abnormal new tissue growth, may produce different patterns. Cells can also be damaged during collection or preparation, making normal structures misleading or obscuring useful detail. Some tumors release cells readily; connective tissue tumors can be difficult to sample because their cells remain firmly attached. Nondiagnostic means insufficient information for a diagnosis, rather than absence of disease. The word suspicious likewise carries uncertainty rather than supplying a complete cancer diagnosis. Merck's veterinary cytology guidance recommends histology when findings are doubtful or do not fit the clinical picture. A reassuring interpretation from an unrepresentative sample cannot resolve a growing or otherwise unexplained lesion. [3]

## The diagnostic journey starts before the microscope

The laboratory needs the animal's identity, age, breed and sex, the exact sample location, how long the lesion has been present, relevant medications and the clinical or imaging findings. These details help determine whether the cells could plausibly explain the problem. Cornell's eClinpath guidance also stresses slide quality: thick material, crushed cells, blood contamination and poorly cellular fluid can reduce what can be concluded. Examining a preparation before the animal leaves may reveal an inadequate collection while additional sampling is still feasible. Air dried cytology slides and formalin preserved tissue require different handling; formalin fumes can interfere with staining. These are professional collection and laboratory decisions. For the owner, an important question is whether the submitted material actually represented the intended lesion and arrived in interpretable condition. [2]

Fine needle sampling can be performed with or without suction, but the best choice is not identical for every site. Whitlock and colleagues compared both methods in 104 canine and feline lymph nodes, with each node sampled by both techniques and the interpreting pathologists unaware of the method. Samples obtained with aspiration were more often diagnostic and contained more cells; cell preservation and dilution by blood did not differ significantly. This was a study of ultrasound guided lymph node sampling, rather than proof that suction is always superior in every organ or tumor. It shows why apparently small technical choices can affect the chance of obtaining an answer. A repeat sample may be worthwhile when the first collection failed, provided the veterinarian judges the intended target and procedure appropriate. [5]

## Accuracy numbers need their missing samples

In Ghisleni and colleagues' study of 292 skin and subcutaneous masses from dogs and cats, 49 specimens were excluded because they contained too few cells. Among the remaining 243 specimens, cytology and histology agreed in 221, or 90.9 percent. For identifying neoplasia, reported sensitivity was 89.3 percent and specificity 97.9 percent. Sensitivity describes detection among lesions that truly had neoplasia; specificity describes correct exclusion among those that did not. Neither number means that every negative result is equally reassuring. In this selected series, the negative predictive value was 68.7 percent, and 21 lesions classified as nonneoplastic by cytology were neoplastic on histology. The study supports the value of the test while exposing two distinct limitations: some collections never yielded an interpretable result, and some interpretable samples still missed neoplasia. The percentages apply to this population and comparison, not every newly discovered lump. [4]

## A published case in which a mass was an infection

Boyd and colleagues described a six year old neutered male domestic longhair cat referred for rapid and difficult breathing. Echocardiography, an ultrasound examination of the heart, showed a mass in the cranial mediastinum, the front portion of the chest between the lungs, touching the heart. Fine needle aspirates contained round yeast structures consistent with Histoplasma, a fungal organism. The cat received oral fluconazole under veterinary care. The published abstract reports marked improvement in clinical findings, lesion size and antigen concentrations at subsequent checks. It does not establish a lifelong cure or provide enough follow-up detail to invent a final survival time. The lesson is specific: imaging located a real mass, while cell examination supplied evidence of an infectious cause that imaging alone could not establish. This unusual case cannot tell us how often other heart-adjacent masses are fungal. [11]

## A published series in which deeper lumps were tumors

Robinson and colleagues reported seven dogs with mast cell tumors located within muscle. All seven had a palpable mass diagnosed as a mast cell tumor by fine needle aspiration, followed by CT or ultrasound to assess location and disease extent. Six underwent surgery as their main treatment; three also received additional chemotherapy. All six surgical patients were alive at follow-up of at least seven months. One individual, a ten year eight month old Lhasa apso, was reported alive with a nineteen month disease free interval after surgery and additional chemotherapy. The remaining dog received chemotherapy without surgery and died twenty-three days later. These are observed outcomes in a small selected referral series, not a controlled comparison proving that surgery caused the survival difference. Cytology identified the tumor family; imaging, tissue examination and follow-up supplied other parts of the clinical story. [12]

## Lymphoma diagnosis and classification are different tasks

An aspirate of an enlarged lymph node can be highly useful when lymphoma, a cancer of lymphocytes, is suspected. Nevertheless, reactive lymph nodes can enlarge during infection or immune stimulation, and some lymphomas are composed of deceptively mature-looking cells. The Oncology Pathology Working Group's 2025 consensus considers cytopathology acceptable for diagnosing many canine nodal lymphomas when the clinical probability is high and exact subtype is not required. Complete classification requires histopathology with immunohistochemistry, which uses markers to identify cells within tissue. Flow cytometry measures markers on cells in suspension and helps determine their lineage. A molecular clonality result is not a replacement for this characterization. If an apparently negative aspirate conflicts with strong clinical suspicion, the consensus supports further sampling or histopathology rather than treating that result as the final word. These recommendations concern canine nodal lymphoma; feline intestinal disease is a different diagnostic setting. [14]

## Bone lesions show why classification can remain incomplete

Sabattini and colleagues studied 68 dogs with destructive bone lesions, comparing initial cytology or biopsy findings with a final diagnosis based on surgical or postmortem tissue, or follow-up for nonneoplastic lesions. Overall cytologic accuracy was 83 percent, but the tumor type was correctly specified in only half of the evaluated cases. Thus, recognizing a malignant process and naming its precise origin were different achievements. This matters when a painful bone lesion could represent a primary bone tumor, metastatic disease or a nonneoplastic condition requiring different treatment. The study does not justify choosing irreversible treatment from a vague cytology phrase alone. It supports integrating the sample with imaging, examination and any further tissue assessment needed for a defensible diagnosis, while keeping the limitations of this retrospective canine population visible. [6]

## The liver needs its tissue arrangement

The liver is an especially important limit to extrapolation. Merck's liver guidance describes useful applications of aspirates, including supporting hepatic lipidosis, or fat accumulation in liver cells, in cats, and recognizing certain infections or tumors. Many other liver disorders depend on how changes are distributed through the tissue. Individual cells on a slide cannot preserve that map. A sample therefore may fail to distinguish a reactive change from disease that requires architectural assessment, and absence of malignant cells does not exclude cancer. Liver biopsy remains the standard for diagnosing most hepatic disorders. A useful owner discussion separates the narrow question an aspirate could answer from the broader question the veterinarian still needs answered before recommending long term treatment. [7]

## A splenic result can miss a heterogeneous mass

A splenic mass may contain blood, dead tissue, reactive tissue and neoplasia in different regions. Holter and colleagues tested 25 canine spleens after surgical removal, using ultrasound guided aspiration and needle core sampling through an artificial skin covering. Final excisional histology was the comparison standard. FNA accuracy was 72 percent, with sensitivity 71 percent and specificity 75 percent for neoplasia; the tested core approaches did not provide a statistically demonstrated improvement. Crucially, these were ex vivo samples, collected outside the living animal. The experiment cannot establish the bleeding risk of sampling an unstable dog, and it was not a survival trial. It illustrates why collecting cells from a small part of a mass may leave substantial diagnostic uncertainty. A negative or nondiagnostic aspirate should not be translated into a guarantee that a concerning splenic mass is benign. [8]

## Chest sampling has a different risk setting

Vignoli and colleagues examined 62 animals, comprising 52 dogs and 10 cats, that underwent both CT guided aspiration and tissue core biopsy of thoracic lesions. Cytology yielded a diagnosis in 43 of the 62 samples. The detailed results describe CT detected mild to moderate pneumothorax, meaning air entering the space around the lung, and mild hemorrhage. No clinical manifestations or need for further therapeutic intervention were reported. Complication category counts differ within the publication, and both procedures were performed, so these findings should not be presented as a precise FNA-only complication rate. They also cannot estimate the risk of sampling a simple skin lump. The relevant decision balances diagnostic information against lesion location, surrounding structures and the animal's condition. A needle procedure inside the chest has a different clinical risk setting from sampling a superficial lump. [10]

## Diagnostic yield is not the same as a confirmed answer

Cordner and colleagues retrospectively reviewed pancreatic aspiration in 92 dogs. Diagnostic yield was 73.5 percent: this measures how often the samples allowed a cytologic diagnosis, rather than how often every diagnosis was proven correct. The main process identified on cytology agreed with confirmatory testing in ten of eleven evaluable cases, a much smaller comparison group. Seven adverse events were recorded during the following forty-eight hours, all in dogs with substantial additional illness or other invasive procedures. Those associations cannot show that aspiration caused every event, or that it caused none. The study offers useful evidence about feasibility and observed problems while leaving uncertainty about accuracy and causation. Reading only the favorable yield figure would conceal both the nondiagnostic samples and the limited independent confirmation. [13]

## A rare reported complication deserves proportion

Mizuno and colleagues described a fourteen year old spayed female miniature Dachshund with a mass at a previous incision site seven months after removal of a lung adenosquamous carcinoma, a malignant epithelial tumor. The authors presumed needle tract implantation following earlier transthoracic fine needle biopsy. The new mass was widely removed, and tissue examination supported adenosquamous carcinoma with a marker associated with lung origin. No local recurrence was observed, but the dog died. The abstract does not establish that implantation caused the death. This is an individual report of a presumed mechanism, with the incidence for this tumor stated to be unknown. It establishes a possible concern requiring professional judgment and follow-up, rather than a percentage risk that can be applied to all aspirations. Equally, calling an event uncommon is not a reason to erase it from the consent discussion when the particular lesion makes it relevant. [9]

## Follow-up cytology can inform rather than guarantee

Lee and colleagues studied 36 incompletely removed mast cell tumors in 33 dogs whose surgical scars were aspirated within sixty days. Local recurrence occurred in five tumors overall. Among the 31 tumors grouped as lacking cytologic evidence of residual tumor, two recurred; among the five with residual tumor on cytology, three recurred. The negative predictive value was therefore 93.5 percent in this study. This is useful prognostic information, but a negative scar aspirate did not eliminate recurrence. Sampling methods and additional therapies were not standardized, and the retrospective design limits causal conclusions. The practical question becomes whether the result changes the recommendation for further local treatment or monitoring in this particular dog. It does not certify that every microscopic tumor cell has been removed or determine the optimal follow-up schedule for every patient. [15]

## Digital images still need the right cells

A scanned slide can allow a pathologist elsewhere to review an aspirate, but digitization does not correct an unrepresentative collection. The ASVCP digital cytopathology quality guidelines address preparation, scanning, image quality, staff training and validation of the particular system. A scan can omit a relevant region or fail to resolve fine detail; some cases require review of the original glass slide. The reader should therefore distinguish a laboratory's validated diagnostic workflow from a casual photograph or an automated label. The relevant question is whether the system captured and displayed the information needed for this specimen and whether a qualified interpreter assessed its limitations. These guidelines do not establish that an unvalidated artificial intelligence application can independently diagnose a pet's mass. [17]

## Urgency follows the animal as well as the sample

For cats, difficulty breathing is a reason for immediate veterinary assessment, even before the cause is known. Cornell's Feline Health Center explains that respiratory distress is a clinical sign with many possible causes, including heart disease, airway disease, fluid around the lungs and tumors. An owner should not wait for a scheduled cytology result or attempt to solve the breathing problem at home. This does not mean every skin lump creates the same emergency. It means a test appointment cannot substitute for assessing a change in the animal's condition. Urgency comes from the animal's condition while the diagnostic team works to establish its cause. [16]

## Forensic use requires a record larger than a slide

A forensic investigation asks questions about injury, disease and care in a legal setting, and its conclusions require more than identifying cells. Touroo and colleagues' minimum standards for examining suspected abused animals emphasize complete records, diagnostic investigation, preservation of evidence and documentation of changes over time. Necessary treatment to protect health and relieve suffering takes precedence over evidence collection. Applied to an aspirate, this means preserving the identity and origin of the specimen and linking the laboratory result to the examination, photographs, history and other findings. Inflammation on a slide alone does not establish who caused a lesion, the person's intent or how long a condition was neglected; those would be additional inferences requiring separate evidence. A tumor diagnosis may explain a lesion medically, while the investigation still examines whether and how the animal's needs were addressed. This is an interpretive boundary, not a claim that cytology can reconstruct an entire event. [18]

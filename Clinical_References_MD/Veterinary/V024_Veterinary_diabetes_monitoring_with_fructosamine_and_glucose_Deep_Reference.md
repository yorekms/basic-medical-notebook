# V024: Veterinary diabetes monitoring with fructosamine and glucose

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Endocrine and metabolic testing
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 3)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft; independent clinical review pending. Some primary studies were accessible only through abstracts or indexed article text.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *If a diabetic animal has a reassuring average result but still has abnormal glucose episodes, what has the test measured and what has it missed?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency for severe illness or neurological signs

Seizures or marked neurological changes in a diabetic dog or cat need emergency assessment now. A severely unwell diabetic cat needs emergency evaluation for ketoacidosis even if glucose appears reassuring.

## Begin with the patient and the question

A glucose result and a fructosamine result answer different questions. Hyperglycemia means increased glucose in blood; glucosuria means glucose in urine. Neither word alone establishes why the change occurred. In cats, excitement or illness can produce substantial temporary hyperglycemia, so the 2026 AAHA feline guidelines require evidence of sustained hyperglycemia when diagnosing diabetes mellitus. Increased fructosamine can support persistence, while measurements in a less stressful environment may clarify a clinic result. Weight loss, increased thirst, increased urination, and appetite changes supply essential context. The initial investigation also evaluates blood cells, serum chemistry, urine sediment, and thyroid status rather than stopping at one glucose number. A monitoring visit after diagnosis has a different purpose: determining whether treatment and the animal's current physiology are producing acceptable control. Evidence used to establish diabetes should not be treated as a complete monitoring plan. [1]

## Dogs need their own monitoring assessment

For an insulin treated dog, the 2026 AAHA canine guidelines combine changes in clinical signs and body weight with glucose curves, continuous glucose monitoring, or fructosamine as needed. A blood glucose curve is a series of measurements showing how glucose changes over a defined period. Its nadir is the lowest measured point, not necessarily the lowest point that occurred between samples. Clinical improvement matters, but observation and laboratory assessment complement one another. Persistent thirst might reflect inadequate diabetic control or another disease; a comfortable looking animal can still have an unrecognized glucose abnormality. Routine urine glucose monitoring is not recommended as the principal assessment of canine control. Urine accumulates over time and cannot reconstruct the blood glucose pattern at each moment. Results that disagree should prompt review of the patient's history, sampling conditions, and measurement method rather than automatic selection of whichever result looks most reassuring. [2]

## Fructosamine measures glucose attached to proteins

Fructosamine is a collective laboratory measurement of glucose modified blood proteins, particularly albumin. Glucose attaches without an enzyme directing the reaction; this process is called glycation. The result therefore depends on glucose exposure and the amount and lifespan of the proteins carrying that exposure. It integrates recent glycemia, meaning glucose status, rather than recording a precise calendar of every day's concentrations. A brief stress response usually does not persist long enough to increase fructosamine, although prolonged stress hyperglycemia can occasionally do so. Cornell's laboratory uses a nitroblue tetrazolium method: under alkaline conditions, fructosamine related compounds produce a colored reaction product, and the analyzer measures the rate of color change. The instrument is measuring a chemical signal from glycated proteins, not counting glucose molecules directly. Serum, the liquid after blood clots, or plasma, the liquid portion of unclotted blood, can be tested, with the laboratory's accepted sample requirements and interference checks applying to the particular assay. [3]

## The glucose sample keeps changing after collection

Blood glucose is a snapshot whose accuracy depends on the sample journey. Insulin promotes glucose use and storage, while several opposing hormones can raise its concentration. After collection, blood cells continue consuming glucose. Delayed separation of serum or plasma from cells can therefore produce an artificially low result. Cooling and prompt processing reduce this problem; a tube label does not establish when separation occurred. Cornell uses a hexokinase assay, in which linked enzyme reactions generate a product measured by light absorption in proportion to glucose. Portable meters and sensors require separate validation. Severe genuine hypoglycemia, meaning low blood glucose, can cause disorientation, loss of coordination, seizures, or coma. Such signs in a diabetic dog or cat require emergency veterinary assessment now rather than waiting for a routine average measurement. [4]

## An average cannot exclude a dangerous low period

The Society for Comparative Endocrinology and partner societies emphasize that any fructosamine value can coexist with episodic hypoglycemia. A hypothetical worked example illustrates the limitation: imagine two insulin treated animals with similar fructosamine results. One has moderately increased glucose throughout much of the day; the other alternates between substantial highs and short low periods. Their protein based averages might resemble one another even though their immediate risks differ. These are explanatory possibilities, not published patients or invented treatment outcomes. A curve or sensor trace can reveal variability that an average hides. Conversely, a single low sensor point does not prove sustained hypoglycemia without assessing measurement reliability and the animal. The consensus also cautions that laboratories do not generate interchangeable fructosamine results. Trends are most interpretable using the same analyzer and reasonably stable serum protein concentrations. Published categories of good or poor control cannot simply be transferred between assays. [5]

## Thyroid disease can make the average misleading

Graham, Mooney, and Murray studied thirty hyperthyroid cats and thirty five healthy cats in 1999, measuring fructosamine before and after radioiodine treatment. Hyperthyroidism means excessive thyroid hormone activity. The hyperthyroid cats had lower fructosamine concentrations than healthy controls; values remained lower overall at the thirty day assessment. Changes after treatment were not directly explained by changes in measured total protein or globulins, another group of blood proteins, and the authors proposed altered protein turnover as a mechanism. This is an important distinction: measuring how much protein is present does not fully measure how rapidly protein is being replaced. The study was not a validation of a numerical correction formula for diabetic cats. In a cat with concurrent thyroid disease, a relatively low fructosamine should therefore be interpreted alongside glucose measurements and thyroid assessment rather than accepted as proof of excellent diabetic control. [6]

## Protein abnormalities have species dependent effects

Reusch and Haberer investigated fructosamine in normoglycemic dogs and cats with other laboratory abnormalities in 2001. Normoglycemic means that blood glucose was not increased or decreased. Low protein concentrations produced the most frequent and pronounced departures: fructosamine was below the reference interval in about two thirds of affected animals in each species. Associations differed between dogs and cats, and reduced fructosamine with azotemia or hyperlipidemia in dogs was not reproduced in the feline groups. Azotemia means increased nitrogenous waste products in blood; hyperlipidemia means increased circulating lipids. A second hypothetical worked example is a diabetic cat whose fructosamine falls while protein loss develops. The fall could partly reflect changing protein biology rather than improved glucose control. No patient outcome is implied. The appropriate question is whether paired glucose information, protein results, and the clinical examination support the same interpretation. [7]

## A sensor follows fluid between cells

Continuous and flash glucose monitoring systems measure interstitial glucose, meaning glucose in fluid between tissue cells. They provide repeated estimates through a small sensor rather than taking a fresh blood sample for every point. Shea and Hess evaluated FreeStyle Libre in eight outpatient diabetic cats in 2021, analyzing fifteen sensor placements. Agreement with an automated biochemical analyzer was encouraging overall, but only six paired low glucose samples were available, limiting conclusions in that critical range. The cats were established insulin treated patients, not newly diagnosed cats in ketoacidosis. Sensor loss was also a practical limitation. A trace contains information about timing and variability, but an interrupted trace cannot describe the missing hours. This study supports use in its tested setting, while leaving important limits visible: the number of individual animals, repeated placements within animals, sparse low range observations, and the distinction between outpatient monitoring and critical illness. [8]

## Feline low glucose performance needs separate testing

Berg and colleagues specifically tested FreeStyle Libre 2 during experimentally controlled glucose changes in nine healthy cats in 2023. They compared interstitial readings with a veterinary blood glucose meter, AlphaTrak2, rather than a laboratory reference analyzer. Overall correlation was stronger during stable glucose than during changing concentrations. Importantly, the direction of error varied: the sensor generally read below blood glucose in the normal range but could read above blood glucose during marked hypoglycemia. A reassuring sensor number could therefore understate the severity of a low blood glucose episode in that model. Correlation means that measurements tend to move together; it does not guarantee numerical agreement for every pair. The study does not establish identical performance in every sick diabetic cat or another sensor generation. A veterinarian must reconcile an unexpected low reading or neurological signs with clinical assessment and an appropriate confirmatory blood measurement. [9]

## Rapid change is also a challenge in dogs

Howard and colleagues examined an earlier FreeStyle Libre system in twenty four apparently healthy dogs during insulin induced, subsequently corrected hypoglycemia in 2021. Interstitial results and portable meter results were compared with a clinical chemistry analyzer. Under these rapidly changing experimental conditions, the flash system had limited agreement with the reference method and failed to reliably detect hypoglycemia. The lowest median sensor value occurred later than the lowest median blood value. This temporal mismatch helps explain interstitial lag: blood and tissue fluid are related compartments, but they need not change simultaneously. It does not justify applying a fixed time correction to every trace. The animals were not a representative cohort of unstable diabetic dogs, and these findings should not be assigned automatically to newer instruments. The practical lesson is narrower: a sensor's overall usefulness does not establish reliable recognition of every rapidly developing low glucose event. [10]

## Wear time and skin tolerance affect the record

Corradini and colleagues' 2016 prospective study followed ten client owned insulin treated diabetic dogs wearing the original FreeStyle Libre. Interstitial readings correlated well overall with plasma glucose measured by a hexokinase method, but that did not mean every reading met the study's analytical accuracy criteria. Seven sensors lasted the intended fourteen days; three detached earlier. Mild redness at the application site occurred in five dogs. These observations make the specimen journey broader than a blood tube: attachment, skin response, sensor integrity, and missing data influence what reaches the report. Placement and interpretation belong within veterinary care, with the device and its version documented. Repeated readings from ten dogs increase the number of paired comparisons without increasing the independent animal population to hundreds. The study supports feasibility and useful patterns, while its small population and limited low glucose observations constrain claims of universal reliability. [11]

## Disagreement can be informative rather than an error to erase

Briggs and colleagues reviewed fifty three insulin treated dogs in 2000, classifying control using history, examination, and body weight before comparing several glycemic measurements. Among twenty five dogs judged clinically well controlled, all blood glucose and glycated protein assessments agreed with that classification in only fifteen. Existing cataracts and an enlarged liver were also poor indicators of current control: a consequence of earlier disease need not describe today's glucose pattern. This retrospective cohort is not a modern sensor trial, and its laboratory categories should not become universal thresholds. It nevertheless demonstrates why disagreement deserves investigation. A clinician can ask whether an observation reflects current glucose, prior exposure, a sampling interval, another illness, or an assay limitation. Keeping the original measurements and their timing is more informative than silently discarding inconvenient results or treating a single average as the final judge. [12]

## Remission requires follow up rather than a declaration of cure

In Gottlieb and colleagues' 2015 study, twenty one cats in diabetic remission were compared with twenty eight healthy controls using glucose assessment and a supervised glucose tolerance test. The latter examines the response to an administered glucose challenge; it is a research or clinical procedure, not an owner experiment. Most remission cats still had impaired glucose tolerance. Six of twenty cats followed for at least nine months relapsed and again required insulin. All remission cats had fructosamine within the testing laboratory's reference interval at the initial assessment, and fructosamine did not significantly predict relapse in this small cohort. Remission therefore means a period without the previous treatment requirement, not proof that glucose regulation is permanently normal. These data support continued veterinary monitoring. They cannot provide a certain relapse forecast for an individual cat, establish a home glucose challenge protocol, or be generalized to remission in dogs. [13]

## A published feline remission case shows the sequence

Lodarski, Benchekroun, and Cervone reported one thirteen year old castrated male domestic shorthair cat in 2025, DOI 10.1177/20551169251399435. Lethargy, weight loss, thirst, and urination changes followed a long acting glucocorticoid injection. Early fructosamine was within its laboratory interval, but later hyperglycemia, glucosuria, and increased fructosamine supported diabetes. Insulin was prescribed but was not administered because injections proved difficult. After oral velagliflozin and dietary management, glucose improved and weight increased. Persistent diarrhea led to clinician directed discontinuation after sixty four treatment days. The cat remained off diabetic medication through the reported 175 day follow-up, with reassuring glucose and fructosamine assessments. A pulmonary adenocarcinoma was also removed during follow-up. This is an actual observed remission, not evidence that this drug reliably produces remission. The contribution of the earlier glucocorticoid, its waning effect, dietary change, or spontaneous recovery cannot be separated in one uncontrolled case. Longer term relapse was not reported, and the authors noted limitations in ketone monitoring. The sequence illustrates why an earlier normal fructosamine does not settle a subsequently evolving illness. [14]

## A published canine case reveals what intermittent samples missed

Kim and colleagues reported one twelve year old neutered male Miniature Schnauzer in 2017, DOI 10.17221/25/2017-VETMED. Anorexia, lethargy, oral bleeding, and abdominal pain prompted blood chemistry, blood gas and electrolyte testing, urinalysis, and pancreatic assessment. Hyperglycemia, increased fructosamine, ketonuria, and metabolic acidosis supported diabetic ketoacidosis with concurrent pancreatic disease. Initial hospital treatment included fluids, insulin, and supportive care. After an initial discharge, the dog returned two days later with hyperglycemia and recurrent illness. Comparing a continuous interstitial record with intermittent portable blood measurements revealed short low glucose periods between samples. The clinicians revised insulin management; the dog's condition and acid-base disturbance improved and the dog was discharged. Durable long term survival or remission was not established by the reported follow-up. The authors interpreted rebound patterns as a Somogyi phenomenon, but a single case cannot establish that mechanism or its frequency. The directly useful observation is that sampling intervals missed excursions. Human critical care outcomes discussed in the paper do not establish outcomes or treatment targets for this dog or other animals. [15]

## A favorable glucose result does not exclude ketoacidosis

The 2024 SENSATION study provides broader feline outcome evidence. This prospective open label field trial followed 252 cats receiving velagliflozin, including 214 previously untreated and thirty eight previously insulin treated cats. Glycemic measurements and clinical signs generally improved. At day 180, eighty one percent of the 158 cats remaining had blood glucose or fructosamine within reference ranges. That denominator matters: it is not eighty one percent of all enrolled cats. Eighteen cats developed ketoacidosis; fourteen of those were classified as euglycemic, meaning their glucose was not markedly increased at diagnosis under the study's definition. Most ketosis or ketoacidosis events occurred early. The study was baseline controlled rather than randomized against another therapy, and previously treated cats had different risks. Its findings support testing for metabolic complications when a treated cat becomes ill despite an apparently favorable glucose result. They do not authorize switching an individual cat's treatment or infer remission from glucose control while medication continues. [16]

## Urgency comes from the animal and the metabolic disturbance

The 2025 iCatCare guidelines explain that inadequate insulin action can drive ketone production and metabolic acidosis, an excess acid burden with disturbed blood chemistry. A diabetic cat with vomiting, loss of appetite, marked lethargy, weakness, or dehydration needs urgent assessment for ketoacidosis; a severely unwell cat needs emergency veterinary care now. Normal appearing glucose does not exclude the euglycemic form, especially during sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor treatment. This drug class lowers glucose through renal glucose loss, so glucosuria itself is an expected effect. Blood beta hydroxybutyrate measures a major ketone; standard urine strips detect a different ketone and can miss early disease. Ketone findings require interpretation with the examination and acid-base assessment, rather than equating every trace result with ketoacidosis. Glucose, electrolytes, blood gas testing, urine assessment, and investigation of concurrent disease are complementary. Fructosamine cannot replace this acute evaluation. [17]

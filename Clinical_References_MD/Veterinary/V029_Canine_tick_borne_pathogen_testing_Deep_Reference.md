## Start with the dog, then identify the test target

This chapter concerns dogs; canine test interpretations and treatment evidence should not be transferred directly to cats. A tick panel usually combines several separate questions. Serology detects antibodies, the immune system's response to exposure. Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, detects selected pathogen DNA, or genetic material, in the submitted specimen. A blood count measures effects on the animal, including anemia (too few red cells) and reduced platelets, which help blood clot. These observations are related but not interchangeable. The Companion Animal Parasite Council (CAPC) recommends combining history, examination, serology, PCR and hematology for ehrlichiosis and anaplasmosis. A dog with unexplained bleeding or marked illness needs prompt assessment while the cause is investigated. A screening result alone cannot establish the severity, date or source of an infection, and antibodies can persist after clinical recovery. [1]

## Geography and travel decide which organisms belong on the list

The European Scientific Counsel Companion Animal Parasites (ESCCAP) guidance distinguishes infections by pathogen, vector and distribution. Ehrlichia and Anaplasma are bacteria; Babesia are single-celled parasites; Borrelia are spiral-shaped bacteria. They are not one interchangeable tick fever. Different tick species carry different agents, and the important species of Babesia vary between regions. A clinician therefore needs the dog's lifetime travel, importation, previous homes, tick prevention and transfusion history, not just its current address. Imported infection can appear where local transmission is uncommon. Conversely, seeing a tick species in an area does not prove that every tick carries a particular pathogen. European, North American and Australian recommendations answer different local questions. A panel suitable for one region may leave out an important organism in another. [2]

## Ehrlichia and Anaplasma occupy different blood cells

Ehrlichia canis primarily involves monocytes, a type of white blood cell. Anaplasma phagocytophilum involves neutrophils, another white cell, whereas Anaplasma platys infects platelets and can produce cyclic changes in platelet counts. The European practitioner guideline explains why a stained blood smear may reveal bacterial clusters called morulae, yet a negative smear is weak evidence against infection: clusters can be scarce or transient. Antibodies also take time to appear, and related species can cross-react on serology. A genus-level positive may therefore need species-specific PCR to clarify the organism. Paired quantitative antibody measurements, collected during illness and later convalescence, can show a rising response; a single positive titer, or antibody level, may reflect older exposure. PCR before antibiotics can provide complementary evidence. The laboratory needs the actual specimen and treatment history because suppressed or intermittent circulation can produce a negative blood PCR in an infected dog. [3]

## A controlled study shows why the first antibody test can miss illness

Moroff and colleagues studied eight dogs experimentally infected with Ehrlichia canis and compared PCR with three antibody methods. On the day a reduced platelet count was first detected, PCR was positive in all eight dogs; the antibody methods detected seven, four and one, respectively. After antibiotic treatment began, the blood PCR became negative while antibodies remained detectable. This experiment connects disagreement to the changing biological target, rather than assuming that one result must be a clerical mistake. It also demonstrates product-specific performance: assays designed around different antigens need not become positive together. The small experimental study and its 2014 test versions cannot supply a permanent ranking of every present-day brand. The useful clinical question is which method fits the suspected stage of infection. [4]

## A real anaplasmosis case had negative antibodies and a positive PCR

A published Vancouver Island case illustrates the early diagnostic gap. The dog's clinical history and a blood smear showing morulae in a neutrophil suggested anaplasmosis. A combination screening test was negative, including its Anaplasma antibody component. After consultation with the laboratory, PCR on the submitted serum detected Anaplasma DNA. The dog improved substantially after a few days of veterinary therapy and had an uneventful recovery. It shows why a negative exposure screen should not end investigation when the examination and cell findings point elsewhere. It does not establish serum as the preferred specimen for every laboratory: the submitted material must match the laboratory's validated assay and the veterinarian's diagnostic question. [5]

## Another dog appeared to have lymphoma until the findings were integrated

In a 2025 report, a four-year-old Golden Retriever developed fever, poor appetite and lameness after numerous ticks had been removed. Screening antibodies were negative. Joint fluid contained unusual T lymphocytes, a type of immune cell. Flow cytometry, which classifies cells by their features, plus a clonality test supported concern for lymphoma. Blood PCR instead identified Anaplasma phagocytophilum. The dog improved with doxycycline alone, so cancer treatment was deferred. At the follow-up examination 28 days after hospitalization, the clinical signs and platelet abnormality had resolved; repeat joint testing no longer showed the aberrant cell population. Clonality means that many cells share an origin; in this unusual patient it did not establish cancer independently of context. The report supports a comprehensive investigation, not a claim that every clonal result or swollen joint is infection. [6]

## Modern assay comparisons still measure a specific reference standard

A 2026 comparison evaluated two commercial antibody systems against indirect fluorescent antibody testing. Anaplasma analysis included 310 retained canine serum samples and Ehrlichia analysis 309. Detection did not differ significantly for Anaplasma, whereas the laboratory multiplex system was more sensitive for Ehrlichia relative to that reference. Sensitivity describes detection among reference-positive samples; specificity describes exclusion among reference-negative samples. Neither quantity is the chance that a particular positive dog is sick. The investigators also examined a separate regional sample set and found positivity rates that differed from CAPC reporting. Selection of submitted samples, antigen design and the reference test all matter. These data help choose an exposure screen; they do not turn an antibody result into proof of active clinical disease. [7]

## A panel can miss a disease because it never included it

Wong and colleagues compared a historical rapid screening assay with molecular testing in 100 pet dogs and 100 stray dogs in Hong Kong without obvious clinical signs. The methods disagreed for some Ehrlichia and Anaplasma results, and the rapid test did not include Babesia, the most frequently detected arthropod-borne infection in that cohort. Another important finding came from sequencing: several initially Ehrlichia-positive PCR products were actually Wolbachia DNA, from bacteria associated with heartworm. This demonstrates why target specificity and confirmatory identification matter even for molecular tests. A negative panel only addresses its listed targets; a positive amplification signal depends on assay design and quality control. These selected dogs and older products do not describe the prevalence or accuracy of all current panels. [8]

## Lyme antibodies usually establish exposure before they establish an explanation

The American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM) Lyme consensus emphasizes that most Borrelia-seropositive dogs remain clinically well. Compatible fever and painful swollen joints can support suspected Lyme arthritis, but other causes and coinfections still need consideration. The consensus also recommends assessment for protein loss in urine in seropositive dogs. Proteinuria means protein is escaping into urine; it requires interpretation with urine sediment, kidney measurements and other possible causes. The serious syndrome called Lyme nephritis remains a presumed association rather than something established by one antibody result. The panel describes important gaps in proving that kidney immune complexes are Borrelia-specific in a living dog. Human Lyme symptoms and testing rules should not be copied directly into canine care; feline clinical disease is even less well established. [9]

## For Lyme testing, the antigen named on the report matters

A Lyme antibody test is named partly for the bacterial material it uses to capture the dog's antibodies. CAPC describes C6-based testing as a way to distinguish natural exposure from responses detected by some whole-cell assays after vaccination. Vaccination therefore belongs on the request form, together with the product when known; it should not be used to dismiss every positive result automatically. Borrelia burgdorferi also differs from the blood-cell organisms discussed earlier. It resides mainly in tissues, so whole-blood PCR is generally a poor way to exclude canine Lyme infection, and a routine blood smear will not reveal it. A negative broad blood PCR and positive Lyme antibodies can consequently be biologically compatible rather than contradictory. Clinical interpretation must stay linked to the organism and specimen. [10]

## Antibody timing can help without providing an exact infection date

Wagner and colleagues followed dogs after experimental Borrelia infection and compared several antibody targets. Antibodies against OspC and C6 appeared as early as three weeks, while OspF responses appeared later in that experiment. OspC responses subsequently peaked and declined, whereas other responses increased across the observation period. The researchers also examined sera from 125 client-owned dogs. These findings explain why a laboratory may report different antibody patterns rather than a single Lyme label. They do not create a calendar precise enough to identify the particular walk, tick or household responsible. Experimental challenge, repeat environmental exposure and vaccine composition can alter interpretation. A quantitative antibody value is an immune measurement, not a direct bacterial count, severity score or independent prediction of kidney failure. [11]

## Related Borrelia can sometimes cross-react with Lyme assays

A controlled study infected seven Beagles with Borrelia turicatae, a relapsing-fever organism distinct from the usual Lyme agent. Six developed a measurable antibody response to that organism. Five of those six reacted on at least one of the evaluated Lyme-associated assays, especially a whole-cell fluorescent antibody method. The two C6 assays remained negative at their diagnostic thresholds. None of the dogs developed the monitored clinical signs during daily observation. This is a bounded finding about particular assays and an experimental exposure, not evidence that every Lyme result is unreliable. It shows why an unexpected pattern in a dog with unusual travel or exposure deserves a laboratory discussion. Cross-reaction means recognition of related material; it does not establish two simultaneous infections. [12]

## Babesia testing asks about a red-cell parasite and its species

Babesia multiply within red blood cells. Destruction of infected cells, together with immune removal of cells, can produce anemia; some dogs also develop low platelets and other complications. A stained blood smear can show the parasites, but low parasite numbers can make a carrier's smear negative. Whole-blood PCR can identify parasite DNA and help distinguish species that look similar microscopically. Antibodies may cross-react and may still be absent early. CAPC distinguishes larger and smaller canine Babesia because their behavior and treatment response differ. A generic positive result therefore warrants attention to the identified species and method. Improvement after therapy does not automatically demonstrate eradication, and relapse can occur when a persistent infection is unmasked by illness or immune suppression. [13]

## A Canadian Babesia case improved, but follow-up did not prove clearance

A twelve-year-old American Staffordshire Terrier on Prince Edward Island was referred for suspected immune-mediated destruction of red cells. Blood-film findings suggested Babesia, and PCR with sequencing identified Babesia vulpes. Transfusion support improved the dog's condition, and species-directed treatment followed. Five days after completing that course, the owner reported that the dog was doing well; anemia had improved but remained mild, and no parasites were seen on the reviewed smear. Financial constraints prevented a follow-up PCR. The reported outcome was clinical improvement with a defined laboratory trend, not molecular proof of permanent clearance. The case also illustrates why apparently unfamiliar local diagnoses deserve consideration when the blood findings fit. A historical impression that a region is low risk cannot exclude an individual infection. [14]

## A fatal coinfection case shows why urgency follows the patient's condition

An Indian case report described a two-month-old puppy with a week of vomiting and poor appetite, pale mucous membranes, severe anemia and low platelets. A blood smear showed organisms consistent with Babesia and Ehrlichia, and molecular testing confirmed the reported coinfection with Babesia canis vogeli and Ehrlichia canis. Despite treatment, the puppy died that same day. This single report does not provide a mortality percentage for all coinfected dogs, and it cannot separate precisely how much each infection contributed. Its practical lesson is that laboratory identification can arrive in a patient already requiring urgent supportive care. Marked weakness, pallor, bleeding or systemic deterioration should not be reduced to an argument about which screening spot is positive before the animal is assessed. [15]

## Healthy positive dogs and regional surveys answer a different question

A 2025 Townsville study sampled 301 domestic dogs at least six months old through a convenience sample involving clinics and university contacts. None tested positive for Ehrlichia canis, while nine, or three percent, were PCR positive for Babesia vogeli. The population consisted of healthy dogs, so these findings directly illustrate infection detection without a corresponding clinical illness at sampling. They cannot show that Ehrlichia was absent from all dogs in the region, particularly sick or unsampled animals. Nor does a three-percent estimate transfer to puppies, shelters or another country. A regional prevalence figure describes who was sampled, when and how. Those details influence the meaning of a screening result just as much as the printed name of the assay. [16]

## Blood donation requires a separate screening decision

ACVIM's North American donor-screening consensus treats healthy blood donors differently from dogs being evaluated for illness. A donor can carry a blood-borne agent without obvious signs, and transfusion places material directly into a susceptible recipient. For selected organisms, combining antibody and PCR testing helps address their different detection windows; neither method alone excludes every carrier. The consensus considers regional exposure and specific agents rather than assuming that a general wellness panel makes blood safe. It does not recommend routine Borrelia screening of healthy canine donors in the same way as agents with established blood-borne concerns. Donor eligibility is consequently a veterinary blood-bank assessment. An owner should disclose travel, previous positive tests and treatment history even when the prospective donor currently appears healthy. [17]

## Forensic investigation cohorts show risk without diagnosing an animal's history

In a multi-state dogfighting investigation, researchers tested 269 seized dogs. Babesia gibsoni was detected in 39 percent using the study's combined testing definition, and infection was associated with anemia. The study distinguished antibody-positive and PCR-positive counts and considered blood exposure through bites and transmission from dam to offspring as possible routes. These are findings in a documented investigation cohort; a positive dog elsewhere has not thereby been proved to have fought. Breed, scars or a pathogen result alone cannot identify a perpetrator or reconstruct a transmission event. In an individual welfare investigation, retain identification, collection dates, laboratory methods, body-condition findings, injuries and the broader scene evidence. The study supports medical screening and anemia monitoring during rescue, rather than replacing the investigation with a test result. [18]

## Clinical recovery, negative blood PCR and eradication are distinct outcomes

In an experimental Ehrlichia canis study, ten dogs were treated during different phases of infection. Dogs treated in acute or subclinical phases became blood-PCR negative as clinical measurements improved; chronically infected dogs remained intermittently positive. Ticks subsequently fed on treated dogs could still become PCR positive. This research outcome explains why one negative blood sample cannot prove that every relevant tissue is free of organisms. It also differs from demonstrating that each PCR-positive tick would transmit disease to another dog; the measured result was DNA detection in the acquisition-fed ticks. Follow-up must therefore combine the clinical course, blood-cell recovery, specimen timing and an appropriate laboratory plan. Repeated antibiotics simply to make an old antibody result disappear are not an interpretation strategy. [19]

## Australian guidance adds a specific long-term and biosecurity context

Australia first detected locally acquired canine ehrlichiosis in 2020. Its national veterinary guidance describes the challenge of managing apparently recovered and subclinical dogs in affected regions: antibodies can persist, and a negative blood PCR does not assure that tissue-associated infection has cleared. The guidance advises considering previously diagnosed dogs potentially subclinically infected for life and maintaining appropriate owner education and vector protection. That is a region-specific management stance, not a prediction that every positive dog will develop fatal chronic disease. Illness can emerge long after travel, so previous residence remains relevant. Local veterinarians also handle the applicable reporting requirements and movement advice. Preserve the full timeline when a dog is rehomed; replacing the record with a single recovered label can conceal information needed for future care. [20]

## There is no single safe tick attachment clock for every pathogen

Fourie and colleagues studied Anaplasma transmission using infected ticks on dogs and an artificial feeding system. All three dogs whose ticks remained until engorgement developed detectable DNA and antibodies; none of the groups with removal at the earlier tested times did, and all dogs remained healthy. Yet pathogen DNA appeared much earlier in some artificial feeding chambers. These findings distinguish transfer of detectable material, establishment of infection and development of clinical illness. They should not be converted into an owner rule that every tick removed before a particular hour is harmless. Tick species, pathogen, inoculum and experimental conditions matter, and other canine agents can transmit faster. The appropriate response is prompt tick management and suitable prevention, with testing planned around the dog's actual exposure and clinical condition. [21]

## A useful follow-up plan includes prevention and a durable record

CAPC recommends year-round tick control because exposure varies geographically and seasonally, and brown dog ticks can maintain infestations in homes or kennels. Finding a tick despite preventive treatment does not by itself prove that the product was never used; environmental pressure and reinfestation also matter. The veterinary team should match prevention to species, region and the household rather than borrowing another animal's product. Dogs can also signal shared environmental tick exposure for people, who need their own medical assessment if ill. Keep the pathogen name, antibody or DNA target, specimen, collection date, treatment dates and follow-up findings together. That record helps the next clinician distinguish a new illness from an old exposure signal and decide which unresolved question actually needs another test. [22]

## Choose and preserve samples for the question being asked

The American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) 2026 One Health guidance explicitly separates exposure tests from tests directed at current tick-borne infections. Its diagnostic table lists antibodies, blood smears and PCR as complementary approaches with different limits. It also stresses proper collection and preservation, communication about financial constraints and clinical interpretation of discordant results. For an owner, the practical preparation is a concise timeline and a complete list of drugs, previous results and travel. The veterinary team selects blood or tissue specimens and the laboratory handles validated methods and controls. When several infections are possible, a positive first result need not explain every abnormality. Decide what a further test could change: immediate care, organism identification, donor eligibility, follow-up or regional disease reporting. That purpose makes repeat testing interpretable. [23]

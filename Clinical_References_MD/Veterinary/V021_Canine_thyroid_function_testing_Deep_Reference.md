# V021: Canine thyroid function testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog
- **Clinical Category**: Endocrinology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 2)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft not independently clinically reviewed

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When a tired dog has a low thyroid hormone result, is the thyroid failing, or is the result describing illness, medication, breed, or the assay itself?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Urgent only for critical clinical deterioration

A dog with profound altered awareness or collapse and cardiac or breathing deterioration needs emergency veterinary assessment now. A low T4 result by itself does not establish a crisis.

## The question is about a working system

Hypothyroidism means inadequate thyroid hormone supply from a malfunctioning thyroid control system. Primary disease starts in the gland; central disease starts in the pituitary or hypothalamus, brain structures that direct it. Inflammation and loss of functioning tissue are important primary processes. Thyroiditis, meaning thyroid inflammation, can precede hormone deficiency. An immune marker therefore answers a different question from a measurement of hormone output. A positive antibody result does not automatically establish a need for replacement, and absent antibodies do not prove normal gland structure. [1]

## What usually prompts testing

Testing is most useful when a coherent pattern raises suspicion: gradual lethargy, exercise intolerance, otherwise unexplained weight gain, heat seeking behavior, and characteristic skin or coat changes. Alopecia means hair loss; nonpruritic means it is not primarily itchy. High cholesterol or a mild nonregenerative anemia, in which the marrow is not increasing red cell production appropriately, can reinforce suspicion but are nonspecific. The 2023 AAHA guidance cautions against diagnosing from an isolated low total thyroxine result. A result in the upper part of the applicable interval generally argues strongly against ordinary primary hypothyroidism, while a low or low normal result with convincing signs prompts free thyroxine and thyroid stimulating hormone testing. Severe illness can depress both total and free hormone concentrations, making stabilization and later reassessment valuable when clinically appropriate. The purpose of the extra tests is to resolve a particular uncertainty, rather than to convert any flagged result into a disease label. [5]

## How the sample becomes a set of measurements

The usual specimen is serum, the liquid remaining after blood has clotted. The clinic records medications, concurrent disease, and whether the sample is for diagnosis or for monitoring an already treated dog; the requested profiles are not identical. Michigan State University distinguishes direct serum free T4 testing from a premium profile using equilibrium dialysis, which separates hormone from interfering proteins before measurement. Its diagnostic profiles also investigate thyroid antibodies, whereas its therapeutic profiles omit antibody testing. Fasting may help reduce lipemia, the cloudy appearance caused by fat, but the laboratory explicitly makes this conditional on medical suitability. A specimen that remains lipemic can still be submitted with that history. The useful preparation is a coordinated clinic and laboratory plan using the laboratory's current handling requirements. Owners should provide a complete medication history and follow the veterinarian's instructions rather than independently changing food or medicines to produce a more favorable number. [3]

## Total hormone free hormone and the assay

Total T4 includes hormone attached to blood proteins and the small unbound fraction called free T4. Most veterinary total T4 methods are competitive immunoassays: sample hormone competes in an antibody based measurement system, and the instrument converts its signal into a concentration. Equilibrium dialysis separates free from protein bound hormone before the measurement step. An assay is a specific analytical method, not just a test name. Different antibodies, calibration systems, measurement ranges, and detection technologies can give different results on the same specimen. The European Society of Veterinary Endocrinology therefore emphasizes validation, continuing quality control, and method specific reference intervals. A human thyroid assay is not automatically fit for the much lower concentrations important in canine hypothyroidism. Moving a borderline result between instruments can create an apparent change without establishing a biological change. The report should identify the method, units, and applicable interval before comparisons are made. [2]

## What antibodies and TSH add

Thyroid stimulating hormone, abbreviated TSH, is the pituitary signal encouraging thyroid hormone production. High canine TSH together with low thyroid hormones supports primary gland failure, but normal TSH cannot exclude it. Thyroglobulin is a protein involved in making thyroid hormone; an autoantibody is an antibody directed against the animal's own material. Thyroglobulin autoantibodies support autoimmune thyroiditis, whereas antibodies against T4 or T3 can also interfere with hormone measurements. Unexpectedly high hormone values can therefore require investigation rather than reassurance. Cornell recommends combining antibody findings with functional tests, and notes that total T3 is less helpful as a routine hypothyroidism test. The distinction matters in breeding assessment as well as in a sick dog: identifying a marker of thyroid inflammation is different from showing inadequate hormone production today. Laboratories can advise which free T4 method is appropriate when antibody interference is suspected, because assay design changes the vulnerability to interference. [4]

## What the original diagnostic studies actually showed

Peterson and colleagues' 1997 prospective series compared 54 hypothyroid dogs, 54 dogs with other illness initially suspected of hypothyroidism, and 150 clinically normal dogs. Diagnosis in the hypothyroid group incorporated clinical findings, stimulation testing, exclusion of other illness, and response to replacement. Free T4 was low in 53 of the 54 hypothyroid dogs, but TSH remained within the reference limits in 13. Three hypothyroid dogs had high total T4 because of autoantibodies. Combining a hormone result with TSH improved specificity, the ability to avoid incorrectly labeling unaffected dogs. These are valuable demonstrations of why a single result can mislead, not a promise that the published percentages describe every contemporary assay or clinic population. The study's diagnostic classification also incorporated information beyond the blood tests under evaluation, including subsequent response; readers should not treat its performance estimates as independent proof from one isolated tube. [6]

A separate 1999 investigation by Dixon and Mooney examined 107 dogs with suggestive signs, with hypothyroidism confirmed in 30 and excluded in 77 using exogenous TSH response testing. Low total hormone, low free hormone, and high endogenous TSH overlapped between groups. The performance of each depended on the cut point chosen. Endogenous means produced within the dog; exogenous means administered from outside. The authors considered TSH useful alongside T4 and found that free T4 added only limited information beyond total T4 in that particular population. This does not contradict the need for carefully selected additional testing. It shows that the value of another assay depends on the clinical population, the reference standard used to classify disease, and what is already known. An optimal research cut point is not a universal boundary separating every healthy dog from every hypothyroid dog, so those study thresholds should not be transplanted onto an unrelated laboratory report. [7]

## Breed and medication can change the meaning

In a 2001 primary study, 98 clinically normal Greyhounds were compared with 19 healthy dogs of other breeds. Basal total and free T4 were lower in Greyhounds, while mean canine TSH was not different. Some Greyhounds were receiving testosterone for estrus suppression, a further population detail relevant to interpreting the study. The central lesson is that a general canine reference interval can make a healthy Greyhound appear abnormal. Hypothetical worked example: an energetic Greyhound with stable weight has low total T4 on a screening panel but TSH within its applicable interval. That pattern does not establish hypothyroidism. A veterinarian checks breed appropriate interpretation, the examination, and competing explanations before pursuing further testing. This example has no actual animal or reported outcome. It illustrates why the reference population belongs in the interpretation, and why a breed association should neither force a diagnosis nor make genuine thyroid disease impossible. [8]

Medication effects are biological as well as analytical. In a prospective study of 12 normal adult dogs treated with phenobarbital for 29 weeks, total T4 and free T4 measured by equilibrium dialysis decreased, while TSH showed a delayed compensatory increase and cholesterol rose. The study also evaluated the adrenal axis. Its canine findings should be used directly rather than assuming that a drug effect described in people must occur identically in dogs. For thyroid testing, the important point is that low free hormone is not an absolute safeguard against drug related misclassification. The prescribing veterinarian weighs the reason for treatment, the clinical signs, and the whole profile. A laboratory finding does not justify an owner stopping an anticonvulsant. A repeat test or a different investigation may be appropriate, but its timing and safety depend on the patient's treatment needs and cannot be specified from the hormone value alone. [9]

## Illness and follow up can overturn an early impression

A longitudinal university study of 66 dogs with hypoadrenocorticism, published online in 2024 and in a 2025 journal issue, demonstrates another confounder. This adrenal disorder can coexist with thyroid disease, but its initial hormonal disturbance can also imitate it. TSH was high at diagnosis in 10 of 38 dogs assessed before adrenal treatment; it returned to the reference range in all seven of those dogs with available follow-up. Five dogs in the overall cohort were eventually confirmed hypothyroid, four after years of adrenal treatment. Thus, improvement of an initially abnormal thyroid profile does not eliminate the need for later clinical reassessment, and an abnormal initial TSH does not prove concurrent lifelong thyroid failure. Follow-up was incomplete and the population was selected for adrenal disease. Its numbers describe those dogs rather than the prevalence of thyroid disease in all dogs, and they support reassessment rather than a universal testing timetable. [10]

Repeated measurements can also alter a screening classification. A 2025 retrospective study retrieved repeated thyroid evaluations for 8,309 dogs from the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals database; 8,255 had usable initial classifications. More than nine in ten initially classified normal remained normal at follow-up, while equivocal categories changed more often. Equivocal means the available results did not support a firm classification. The database mainly represents pedigree dogs submitted for phenotypic screening, which assesses observable test results rather than directly identifying every causal gene. It is not a random sample of dogs presenting with lethargy. Changes can reflect evolving disease, transient influences, or classification uncertainty. The practical lesson is to preserve prior results and the circumstances of collection. A new abnormality is more interpretable when the clinician can compare the methods and clinical histories instead of reading each panel as an unrelated verdict. [11]

## Specialist testing can clarify selected disagreements

A thyroid stimulating hormone stimulation test asks whether the gland can increase T4 output after a controlled stimulus. Corsini and colleagues studied 114 dogs with suspected hypothyroidism, classifying 40 as hypothyroid and 74 as euthyroid using the test and follow-up information. Euthyroid means having adequate thyroid function. Their recombinant human TSH protocol discriminated the groups well, including some dogs with concurrent illness or medication exposure. Its published concentration cut points belong to that protocol and population; they are not basal T4 thresholds and are not reproduced here as general rules. The retrospective design and incorporation of follow-up response in classification matter when judging accuracy. Availability, cost, and the unresolved question determine whether such a test is useful. It is a clinic supervised dynamic test, meaning that a change after stimulation is measured, rather than a more elaborate name for an ordinary baseline blood sample. [13]

New analytical methods also need critical reading. A 2025 study compared free T4 by chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay with ultrafiltration followed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry in 59 submitted canine samples. Ultrafiltration separates the free fraction; mass spectrometry identifies and measures molecules through their physical behavior rather than solely through antibody binding. The methods correlated, but correlation means that values tend to move together, not that they are interchangeable at a clinical decision boundary. The authors described the work as preliminary, and it was not a large outcome trial independently confirming thyroid disease in every dog. The study supports attention to method differences in complex results. It does not establish that every laboratory must replace a validated immunoassay or that a technically sophisticated measurement automatically resolves a difficult clinical diagnosis without considering illness, medications, and the patient. [12]

## Published cases show stakes and limits

Published case one involved a nine year old intact male mixed breed dog with weakness, poor appetite, severe liver abnormalities, obesity, coat loss, and altered awareness. Giralico and colleagues reported it in 2021, DOI 10.1155/2021/6622767. Routine blood tests and imaging identified major concurrent disease; the combination of neurological and skin findings with marked lipid elevation prompted a thyroid panel. Total T4 was undetectable and TSH high, although free T4 lay within that laboratory's interval. Supportive treatment and antibiotics were given for the acute illness. Intravenous thyroid replacement was planned, but the dog developed respiratory arrest before it could begin; the report records unsuccessful resuscitation and an owner decision for euthanasia, without clarifying circulation between those events. Postmortem examination found severe thyroid atrophy, atherosclerosis, and necrotizing liver and biliary inflammation, supporting suspected myxedema crisis. This is one animal with substantial competing disease. It supplies no successful replacement trial, cannot isolate which disorder caused arrest, and cannot establish a survival probability or a time limit for another dog. [14]

Published case two offers a different outcome. Saunders reported one six year old intact male English Springer Spaniel in 2025, DOI 10.1155/crve/9937245, after recurrent collapse, lethargy, and declining appetite. Low total T4 led to a fuller thyroid assessment; free T4 was low and TSH high. Echocardiography showed severe dilated cardiomyopathy, a disorder with enlarged, weakly contracting heart chambers. During a documented respiratory arrest, a short Holter electrocardiographic recording showed continuing sinus rhythm rather than an explanatory ventricular arrhythmia. The dog recovered with resuscitation and received thyroid replacement together with cardiac medicines; a later atrial fibrillation episode also required care. Cardiac measurements improved substantially at ten weeks and six months, and the owner reported no further respiratory arrests at eleven months. Earlier collapses lacked continuous monitoring, so their mechanism remains uncertain. Concurrent cardiac treatment prevents attributing improvement solely to thyroid replacement, and residual cardiac abnormalities prevent calling the heart completely normal. One survivor does not negate the fatal case or predict another dog's outcome. [15]

## Monitoring and urgency answer different questions

Once replacement has been prescribed, the submission needs the product, administered amount and frequency, and time elapsed since the tablet. Michigan State's laboratory specifically requests these details when interpreting a monitoring profile. A post tablet concentration answers a treatment exposure question that differs from the pretreatment diagnostic question. Omitting timing can make two follow-up reports look contradictory when they represent different points after administration. Owners can help by keeping an accurate treatment and sampling record and describing changes in activity, weight, coat, and any new signs to the veterinarian. The report should remain linked to that history. A monitoring value alone should not be used to reconstruct the untreated gland's capacity or to independently alter the prescribed regimen. If the original diagnosis is uncertain, the clinician and laboratory need to plan a separate reassessment that accounts for ongoing supplementation. [16]

Gradual coat changes usually prompt scheduled investigation. Suspected hypothyroid crisis is different: profound dullness or collapse with hypothermia, meaning abnormally low body temperature, and cardiac or respiratory deterioration requires emergency veterinary assessment now. Urgency follows the failing dog rather than an isolated low T4. Waiting at home for a routine thyroid panel should not delay that assessment. [1]

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Why can a dog with a serious infection have a negative early test, and what changes when the test detects antibodies rather than bacterial DNA?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical deterioration needs prompt assessment

Do not wait for paired antibody testing when an ill dog worsens.

## The disease and the diagnostic question

Leptospirosis is an infection caused by bacteria in the genus Leptospira. In dogs, it can range from an unnoticed infection to serious kidney injury, liver dysfunction, lung disease or death. A serovar is a strain grouping distinguished by features recognized by antibodies; many can infect dogs, and the locally important types vary. The testing question is therefore more specific than whether a dog has ever encountered the organism. Clinicians ask whether current illness is attributable to leptospirosis, whether the organism or its DNA is present, and what precautions are needed. These questions require different evidence. Dogs of any breed or lifestyle can be affected, including small urban pets.  [1]

## MAT measures the immune response

The microscopic agglutination test, abbreviated MAT, detects antibodies in serum, the liquid portion of clotted blood. The laboratory mixes serial dilutions of serum with live leptospiral antigens and looks for clumping. A titer describes the greatest dilution still meeting the test's reaction endpoint. Thus a titer of 1:800 is a dilution result, not a count of 800 bacteria. Laboratories test panels of serovars, and a panel may not represent every possible infecting strain. Cornell also warns that damaged or fatty specimens can hinder interpretation. The test is not a direct measurement of protective immunity after vaccination. A negative reaction cannot settle every suspected case, particularly if the relevant organism is poorly represented by the panel.  [2]

## A time sequence is stronger than one isolated titer

The updated ACVIM consensus recommends combining evidence rather than diagnosing from a single positive or excluding infection from a single negative MAT. Early in disease, a dog may not yet have produced a detectable antibody response. Paired acute and convalescent samples can show seroconversion, meaning a change from undetectable to detectable antibodies, or a substantial rise in titer. The consensus describes repeating serum after roughly one to two weeks when appropriate. The ordering veterinarian coordinates timing with the testing laboratory. PCR, which detects DNA, can provide evidence before antibodies appear. Vaccination dates, onset of illness and antibiotic exposure all affect interpretation. Whenever feasible, diagnostic specimens are collected before treatment, but an ill dog should not wait for convalescent antibodies before receiving necessary care. A plan for repeat testing is part of managing uncertainty, rather than an instruction to leave a worsening patient untreated. [3]

Miller and colleagues showed that the same serum need not yield identical MAT results at every laboratory. They submitted samples from 18 research colony dogs, nine vaccinated, and 17 dogs with clinical leptospirosis to as many as five diagnostic laboratories. Results differed between laboratories and over time, and the identity of the most reactive serogroup was not consistently reproducible. The study therefore cautioned against using the largest titer as a reliable identification of the infecting strain. This matters when someone tries to infer whether a vaccine failed or which animal supplied the infection. A high response to a named antigen is an antibody observation. Determining the actual infecting organism can require culture and molecular characterization. Keeping paired samples within a coordinated laboratory process also makes comparisons more interpretable. [4]

## Vaccination can change antibody results

A 2025 field study followed 97 healthy dogs after revaccination against four leptospiral serovars. Antibodies to vaccine serovars were detected in all dogs during follow up, and 71 still had detectable antibodies at 52 weeks. Reactions to nonvaccine serovars also occurred. The investigators could not completely exclude natural exposure, but the timing of many increases supported a vaccine response. This observation prevents a common mistake: assuming that an antibody reaction to a serovar outside the vaccine necessarily proves infection with that serovar. It also shows why the date and product name of a recent vaccine are useful diagnostic information. Its outcomes should not be turned into a clinical disease rate. [5]

A companion publication examined five commercial antibody tests using the same 97 dog cohort, so it is not an independent second population. Test responses after revaccination differed substantially. At two weeks, 18 dogs were positive on WITNESS Lepto, compared with 43 on SNAP Lepto and 81 on ImmunoComb in that study. These tests recognize different antibody signals, including immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G, often abbreviated IgM and IgG. Results therefore cannot be treated as interchangeable simply because the devices all carry the word Lepto. This was an investigation of vaccination effects in healthy dogs, not a full comparison of diagnostic accuracy in critically ill patients. A clinic result needs the exact test name, vaccination history and supporting investigations.  [6]

## PCR looks for DNA in a particular specimen

Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, amplifies selected DNA sequences until they can be detected. Unlike MAT, it does not wait for the dog to make antibodies. However, it can only detect target material present in the submitted sample. Blood and urine can yield different results as infection evolves. Washington State's diagnostic laboratory accepts particular specimen types, specifies storage and transport, and warns that antibiotic treatment can reduce detection. Insufficient sample volume can also lead to a limitation being recorded on the report. These are laboratory requirements, not instructions for owners to collect invasive samples. The practical owner contribution is an accurate timeline: when signs began, when each specimen was collected, and which medicines had already been given. A negative urine PCR is an observation about that specimen and assay, rather than proof that every organ was free of infection. [7]

Martin, Heseltine and Creevy evaluated a PCR assay against a MAT based clinical classification in 43 dogs, using 49 submitted specimens. Seventeen dogs were classified as having leptospirosis and 26 as not having it. Urine PCR sensitivity was approximately 69 percent, while blood PCR sensitivity was 25 percent in the much smaller blood sample group. Specificity was 100 percent in this dataset. Sensitivity describes detection among classified cases; specificity describes exclusion among classified noncases. The reference classification itself had limitations, and the result does not establish perfect specificity everywhere. Nor does it mean blood PCR is intrinsically unhelpful: illness timing and the small numbers mattered. The study supports using complementary testing when suspicion remains, rather than assuming one negative assay always closes the investigation. [8]

## A persistent DNA signal needs its own question

Hetrick and colleagues followed 32 dogs initially diagnosed through urine PCR. Twenty achieved a negative urine PCR within 14 days or less with the expected antimicrobial approaches, while 12 remained positive after a course of doxycycline lasting as long as 18 days. The published study investigated subsequent testing and treatment, not a simple universal clearance deadline. PCR is a DNA observation, whereas growing an organism in culture is a different demonstration. Persistent positivity therefore needs interpretation alongside illness, treatment history and the laboratory method; it is not an owner instruction to lengthen or switch antibiotics. Likewise, clinical improvement and disappearance of a molecular signal are different outcomes. Follow up may need to establish both the dog's organ recovery and the meaning of continued detection, while retaining appropriate handling precautions. [9]

An asymptomatic carrier is an animal harboring an organism without recognized illness. Carmona Gasca and colleagues described viable Leptospira isolates from asymptomatic dogs as well as fatal cases in Mexico. Four isolates came from kidneys or urine among 58 dogs in a selected canine control setting, with additional isolates from other investigated dogs. Culture supplied evidence beyond an antibody reaction alone. It demonstrates that appearance alone cannot determine whether an animal might shed organisms. It also explains why a positive urine result and the cause of current illness are related questions rather than identical ones. The veterinarian must decide how the test fits the dog's clinical condition and exposure setting. [10]

## A fatal suspected case remained unconfirmed

Howard reported a six year old female American bulldog with vomiting, jaundice, severe kidney injury and progressively reduced urine production. Leptospirosis was the leading clinical suspicion. One rapid test was interpreted as faintly positive, but blood and urine PCR and a separate antibody ELISA were negative. Despite hospital treatment, the dog worsened and the owner elected euthanasia on the third hospital day. Kidney and liver PCR and immunohistochemistry, staining designed to identify the organism in tissue, were also negative after death. A MAT was not performed. The report retained leptospirosis as a possibility while discussing alternative causes, including toxic injury. This must remain a suspected case, rather than a confirmed example of a test missing proven leptospirosis. Its evidential lesson is valuable precisely because the final cause was unresolved: a persuasive pattern, treatment failure and a fatal outcome do not manufacture a missing microbiological confirmation. [11]

## A confirmed case improved but developed a new complication

Cho and colleagues reported a nine year old female mixed breed dog with severe kidney injury, absent urine production and lung involvement. Both blood and urine PCR confirmed Leptospira infection. Continuous renal replacement therapy, which supports blood cleaning when kidneys cannot, helped bridge the period before urine production recovered. On day 13, while blood kidney markers were improving, the dog developed tremors, excessive salivation and altered awareness associated with severe magnesium depletion. Veterinary magnesium treatment rapidly resolved the neurological signs. The dog was discharged on day 20 with improved appetite, activity and urine output. Follow up still showed abnormal renal magnesium handling despite improvement in creatinine. This reported outcome contains both recovery and an important complication. It explains why a confirmed infectious diagnosis or a better creatinine result does not eliminate the need to investigate new signs during recovery. [12]

## A postmortem result can clarify a negative living sample

Grippi and colleagues investigated a two month old puppy born in a Sicilian municipal kennel. It developed loss of appetite, fever and then jaundice. Urine PCR was negative, while antibody testing showed reactions to leptospiral antigens. The puppy deteriorated and died on the third day. PCR on kidney tissue after death detected pathogenic Leptospira DNA, supporting the diagnosis that the urine sample had not established. Examination also identified intestinal intussusception, where bowel folds into another segment. Testing of the other 66 kennel dogs found antibody positive animals, but no further clinical leptospirosis was observed during monitoring. The kennel introduced movement restrictions, disinfection and rodent control. This is a relevant forensic lesson about combining the clinical record, postmortem tissue findings and microbiology. A tissue PCR result does not by itself establish the exact exposure, prove neglect, or demonstrate that every other finding was caused by the same infection. [13]

## Survival figures need the population and the time point

In a retrospective referral study, Da Fonseca Ioannou and colleagues described 22 dogs with leptospirosis severe enough to require renal replacement therapy. Sixteen survived to hospital discharge, approximately 73 percent of the original group. Three of those discharged dogs died within 30 days, and 12 were alive at least six months after discharge. Thus survival to discharge and longer survival had different denominators and different meanings. Some surviving dogs continued to have chronic kidney abnormalities. The maximum creatinine concentration did not distinguish survivors from nonsurvivors in this small cohort; broader organ involvement and bilirubin were more informative associations. These observations should not become an individual survival calculator or proof of a treatment effect. They describe selected dogs that reached a center able to provide intensive support, with no untreated comparison group. [14]

A Sydney case series illustrates how different a reported outcome can look in another setting. Seventeen dogs with confirmed leptospirosis were identified between 2017 and 2020. Six died, nine were euthanized and two survived, an overall case fatality of about 88 percent. The two survivors recovered their biochemical abnormalities over subsequent follow up. Several dogs died before a strong antibody response developed; three dogs tested by MAT were negative to all tested serovars. Dialysis was not available locally in that series. The high fatality therefore belongs to that particular outbreak, severity pattern and treatment setting. It cannot be directly compared with the referral dialysis cohort as though the difference measured the benefit of dialysis alone. Case selection, available treatment and euthanasia decisions all influence the number, while negative early serology can complicate recognition. [15]

## An outbreak investigation links human and animal evidence

A CDC investigation in Wyoming described 13 reported canine cases during a 2023 outbreak. Three met confirmed criteria and ten probable criteria; four dogs died or were euthanized. A human patient with occupational dog exposure also met a probable case definition. The report connected the events epidemiologically, meaning through histories, timing and exposure patterns, rather than presenting a genetically proven transmission chain from a named dog. This distinction matters in occupational health and forensic reasoning. A shared setting may support an exposure hypothesis without identifying the precise source. The investigation also found increased recommendations for canine vaccination after public health outreach. It provides a real example of veterinary recognition contributing to human health protection while keeping the certainty attached to each case definition visible. A report of several cases is not proof that every contact in that workplace became infected. [16]

## Genetic similarity has greater resolution than an antibody label

Randolph and colleagues investigated 59 dogs associated with the 2021 Los Angeles outbreak. Fifty four survived to discharge, although some retained kidney abnormalities. Culture and genome analysis identified serovar Canicola in the characterized organisms, but the genomes were distinct. Exposure to indoor congregate facilities was associated with peak period cases; the limited postpeak genetic samples did not establish direct dog to dog transmission. This combination is instructive: the same serovar name does not mean two infections came from one identical source. A forensic or public health conclusion needs the clinical and sampling timeline, the resolution of the typing method and the alternatives supported by the investigation. The report identified no associated human cases. That observation does not establish zero zoonotic risk; it records the outcome of this investigation within its available surveillance and samples. [19]

## Protecting people while diagnosis is still being resolved

Leptospirosis is zoonotic, meaning it can pass between animals and people. CDC advises avoiding contact with urine or blood from an infected animal before appropriate treatment, using protective clothing when contact is unavoidable, covering skin wounds and washing hands after handling animals or potentially contaminated material. Owners should obtain the veterinarian's instructions for cleaning and for protecting other household animals. People concerned about an exposure should contact a healthcare professional and explain the animal contact, rather than assume a canine test supplies a human diagnosis. These precautions are relevant while the investigation is incomplete because recognition can lag behind illness. They should be proportionate and practical: the aim is to reduce contact with potentially infectious body fluids while the dog receives necessary care, not to infer that ordinary proximity proves transmission. [17]

## Prevention and diagnostic interpretation remain separate tasks

The American Veterinary Medical Association recommends vaccination against leptospirosis for dogs and reducing exposure to contaminated water, rodents and other possible sources. Vaccination also reduces the chance that an infected dog spreads the organism. The preventive plan is discussed with the veterinarian using the dog's record and local circumstances. Antibody testing after vaccination asks a different question from whether the dog currently needs treatment for leptospirosis. A positive antibody response can be an expected consequence of immunization; it should not be used to label the vaccine itself as an infection. Conversely, a vaccination record should remain available if illness develops, rather than being treated as a reason to skip examination. Preserve dates, product names, specimen results and follow up so that prevention and later diagnostic decisions can be interpreted together. [18]

## The clinical condition determines urgency

Cornell's canine guidance emphasizes that diagnosis needs both routine assessment and tests directed at Leptospira. A dog with repeated vomiting, jaundice, marked lethargy, reduced urine or breathing difficulty needs prompt veterinary attention; severe deterioration warrants emergency assessment. Owners should not wait for the next scheduled antibody sample before reporting a change. Routine blood work and urinalysis help establish organ damage and guide support, while PCR and antibody tests investigate the cause. A useful discussion asks which question each result answers, which specimen was tested, what treatment preceded collection and what remains uncertain. The immediate goal is to assess and support the animal, while the diagnostic record develops. A negative early test and a serious clinical problem can coexist, and both observations need attention. [20]

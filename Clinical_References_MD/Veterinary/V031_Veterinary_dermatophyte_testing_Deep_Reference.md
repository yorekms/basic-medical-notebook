## Ringworm is a fungal diagnosis with several different kinds of evidence

Dermatophytosis, commonly called ringworm, is caused by fungi that use keratin, the structural material in hair, superficial skin and claws. It is not a worm infection. This chapter covers dogs and cats; studies in shelter cats do not automatically validate the same performance in every dog. The World Association for Veterinary Dermatology (WAVD) consensus found no single diagnostic gold standard. Examination and direct microscopy can demonstrate infected hair; culture grows recoverable fungus; polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detects selected fungal DNA, or genetic material. Each answers a different question. Disease can be contagious to other animals and people, so investigation and containment matter even when the pet seems otherwise well. A positive coat sample alone, however, does not establish tissue invasion, the source of infection or the appropriateness of prolonged isolation. [1]

## The organism, animal and exposure setting shape the suspicion

ESCCAP's superficial-mycosis guidance describes Microsporum canis and other dermatophytes affecting dogs and cats. Despite its name, M. canis is particularly important in cats. Some other dermatophytes are associated with soil or contact with other animal hosts. Young animals, damaged skin and crowded housing can influence risk, but no breed or housing label establishes the diagnosis. Hair loss, scaling, crusts and broken hairs can have several causes, including parasites, bacterial infection and other inflammatory disease. Ask about new animals, grooming equipment, previous treatment and people with skin lesions. These details guide sampling and household precautions; they do not prove who infected whom. The date a lesion was noticed is also not necessarily the date infection began. [2]

## A Wood lamp helps find hairs worth testing

A proper Wood lamp uses ultraviolet light to reveal characteristic fluorescence along some infected hair shafts. It is a clinical search tool: the veterinarian identifies suspect hairs, then examines or submits them. Merck's veterinary guidance emphasizes that no single test is definitive in every case. The relevant apple-green signal is in hair, not simply any glowing scale, lint or material on the surface. Equipment, examiner technique, the organism and previous therapy influence what is seen. A negative examination cannot exclude every dermatophyte infection, particularly infections with organisms that do not show the same fluorescence. Conversely, a glowing spot photographed with a household ultraviolet device is not enough to diagnose ringworm. The result needs the skin examination and confirmation appropriate to the presentation. [3]

## A shelter study measures the limits of lamp screening

A study of seventy shelter cats compared Wood lamp examination with fungal culture. Culture detected Microsporum canis in about sixty-four percent, while about forty-nine percent showed fluorescence. Relative to culture, the lamp examination had a reported sensitivity of seventy-one percent and specificity of ninety-two percent. Sensitivity is the proportion detected among reference-positive cats; specificity is correct exclusion among reference-negative cats. The figures support trained use at intake while showing that some cats will be missed. They are not universal properties of every lamp, examiner or animal population. A shelter with frequent infection also differs from a household where the starting probability is low. Screening can sort the next diagnostic step, but a negative light examination cannot by itself settle an exposed animal's status. [4]

## Direct microscopy connects the fungus to a particular hair

The clinician can examine selected hairs and skin material microscopically for fungal filaments and arthroconidia, the small fungal units that form around infected hairs. Recognizing the structures takes training; normal debris can be mistaken for infection. In an observer study using samples from ten infected and ten uninfected cats, veterinarians classified stained and unstained hairs before and after seeing illustrated guidance. Reference images improved accuracy, whereas staining alone did not provide the same benefit. The study's overall comparison with culture also showed imperfect sensitivity and specificity. Thus, microscopy is valuable evidence when recognizable fungal structures involve a compatible hair, but the apparent simplicity of looking down a microscope does not make interpretation automatic. Discordant results should prompt review of the specimen and the clinical picture. [5]

## Dermoscopy can locate abnormal hairs without identifying every infection

Dermoscopy magnifies the skin and hair surface. In a study of sixty-seven cats, investigators compared this examination with fungal culture. Thirty-six cats had culture-confirmed dermatophytes, including several species; dermoscopy was positive in twenty-one of those thirty-six. Characteristic bent or comma-like hairs can therefore help a clinician choose hairs for further testing, but the method missed a substantial portion of culture-positive cats. This is a diagnostic aid, not a substitute for species identification or a universal negative screen. Its value is practical: an examination directed toward abnormal hairs may improve the material sent for microscopy and culture. The appearance of a damaged hair alone cannot distinguish every fungus from other processes that break or distort hair shafts. [6]

## A red culture medium is not a species identification

Dermatophyte test medium, abbreviated DTM, changes color under conditions that can accompany fungal growth. The colony must still be assessed, often microscopically, to identify the organism. In a blinded multicenter study, hair from fifty-four dogs and forty-seven cats with suspected disease, plus nine healthy controls, was compared between clinic testing and a mycology laboratory. Referral clinicians who examined colony appearance and microscopy achieved much closer agreement with the laboratory than general-practice readers relying only on color change. The groups differed in who read the plates and how, so the result should not be reduced to a judgment about all practices. It demonstrates that a culture is an interpreted laboratory process, not simply a container that turns red when ringworm is present. [7]

## Culture timing depends on growth, treatment and laboratory conditions

Stuntebeck and colleagues reviewed 13,772 feline fungal cultures, of which 2,876 grew Microsporum canis. Only seventy-six positive cultures required more than fourteen days for recovery or confirmation. Late positives were more frequent among samples from treated cats, where growth and microscopic features could be altered. These were culture specimens, not 13,772 independent cats. The study supports a laboratory-specific approach to finalizing cultures and avoiding unnecessary waiting, while acknowledging that a highly experienced research laboratory may differ from a clinic. A preliminary no-growth report is not the same as the laboratory's final negative result. Treatment history matters because antifungal exposure can suppress or distort growth; collection and interpretation should follow the veterinarian's and laboratory's plan rather than a fixed household countdown. [8]

## Positive coat cultures can include mechanical carriers

A Northwestern US shelter study distinguished infected cats from cats carrying small quantities of fungus on the coat. In the study population of 11,214 admitted cats, 202 were diagnosed with Microsporum canis infection and nineteen were classified as fomite carriers. A fomite is contaminated material; on an animal, carriage can mean that fungal particles are present without established hair infection. Lamp performance and culture timing varied within this setting, and kittens and transported cats had different risks. These findings show why the examination, colony burden, repeat samples and exposure context belong together. A colony count is useful information under a defined protocol, not an independent universal threshold that can classify every pet. The same positive culture label can conceal biologically different situations. [9]

## PCR can stay positive after cultures have become negative

Jacobson and colleagues compared a commercial PCR panel with culture in 132 shelter cats. All twenty-eight initially culture-positive cats were PCR positive, but PCR was also positive in twelve initially culture-negative cats; repeat cultures subsequently identified infection in two of the rechecked cats. Monitoring data from seventeen treated cats were especially instructive: fourteen remained PCR positive at the first negative culture and eleven at the second. PCR detects DNA and can detect nonviable fungal material or coat contamination, so positivity after treatment does not automatically mean continuing active disease. The observed results are specific to this assay and population. A negative PCR was reassuring in this study, but interpreting cure still requires clinical findings and the agreed monitoring method rather than indefinite treatment for residual DNA. [10]

## Another PCR study found missed infections as well as false positives

A Western Canadian retrospective study selected 246 animals with sufficient paired records: 198 cats and forty-eight dogs. Against a culture-based reference incorporating colony scoring, combined PCR sensitivity was about eighty-six percent and specificity about ninety-five percent. Seventy-two animals were culture positive. Those results differ from the smaller study reporting no PCR false negatives. Differences in specimens, pathogen targets, reference definitions and population can explain such variation; accuracy is not a permanent property of the word PCR. The 246 selected animals also should not be confused with the 125,939 total shelter admissions during the study period. A clinician facing a compatible lesion and a negative PCR can therefore consider culture or another assessment instead of assuming that molecular testing has excluded all dermatophytes. [11]

## Excellent results in a small study need their population attached

A separate 2024 investigation compared dermoscopy, direct hair examination, several culture media and PCR using forty-eight feline samples. PCR performed best within that study, whereas direct examination and dermoscopy were less satisfactory. That finding is useful evidence for the evaluated workflow, but it does not erase the imperfect PCR performance observed in larger shelter datasets. Small selected samples provide limited information about uncommon errors, and comparison methods can themselves miss infection or detect carriage. When a laboratory advertises high accuracy, ask which animals and organisms were tested, how infection was defined and whether treatment had begun. A sensible diagnostic plan can use a rapid result while retaining another method for a mismatch between the laboratory report and the animal's lesions. [12]

## Unusual nodules may require investigation below the hair coat

Most routine ringworm involves superficial structures, but deep, nodular or draining lesions require a different diagnostic approach. The European Advisory Board on Cat Diseases (ABCD) guidance recommends cytology or biopsy with appropriate fungal stains for atypical feline lesions. Cytology examines individual cells and material; histopathology examines tissue architecture and can show whether fungal structures occur within an inflammatory lesion. The laboratory needs to know that dermatophytosis is being considered so that suitable stains and cultures are requested. A coat culture alone cannot establish what caused a deep mass. Conversely, a negative superficial sample cannot reliably explain away an abnormal nodule. These are veterinary procedures chosen for the patient, with attention to other infections and tumors. Worsening, painful, ulcerated or extensive disease deserves assessment rather than repeated home surface checks. [13]

## Two real canine kerion cases reached documented cure

A 2026 report described a five-year-old Silky Terrier and an eight-year-old Shetland Sheepdog with inflammatory lesions called kerions. These raised lesions can resemble bacterial infection or a tumor rather than a familiar ring of hair loss. Hair microscopy and culture demonstrated Microsporum canis, and DNA sequencing supported species identification. After veterinary systemic and topical treatment, the Terrier had clinical cure by day eighty-one, with mycological cure, meaning no further recoverable fungus on follow-up testing, confirmed fifteen days later. The Sheepdog's lesion had resolved with hair regrowth by day sixty, and the report describes subsequent mycological cure. These outcomes distinguish improved appearance from follow-up evidence about fungus. Similar lesions in household members were reported, but that history alone cannot prove the precise direction or source of transmission. [14]

## A deep feline infection improved temporarily and then had a fatal outcome

A 2024 report described a three-year-old Korean domestic shorthair cat with chronic crusts and ulcerated nodules. Cytology showed fungal filaments; tissue examination demonstrated a deep inflammatory process containing fungal plaques, and culture identified Microsporum canis alongside Staphylococcus aureus. The diagnosis was dermatophytic pseudomycetoma, a deep mass of fungal and inflammatory material, with secondary bacterial skin infection. Several veterinary treatments produced partial improvement, but substantial nodules remained, lesions later recurred and the cat died ten months after presentation. The report does not establish a necropsy-proven mechanism of death or a fatal prognosis for ordinary superficial ringworm. Its lesson is that depth, recurrence and concurrent infection change the investigation and prognosis, and that early improvement is not the same outcome as sustained resolution. [15]

## Other deep infections have resolved, with follow-up qualifications

An earlier report described a Persian cat and a Maine Coon with generalized dermatophytosis and dermatophyte mycetomas. Culture identified Microsporum canis. Initial itraconazole was ineffective in one cat and poorly tolerated in the other. Following a revised veterinary plan, both achieved clinical and mycological cure, with sequential negative cultures reached after fourteen and twelve weeks, respectively. The Persian also underwent removal of the residual nodule. Its generalized skin disease later recurred after treatment stopped and was managed with intermittent treatment; the Maine Coon's nodule had resolved medically. These are different individual courses within one report, not two uncomplicated permanent cures. They demonstrate why adverse effects, lesion depth, culture results and recurrence must all be preserved when discussing an apparent treatment success. [16]

## A real shelter controlled disease while continuing adoptions

An open-admission shelter field study combined trained intake examinations, culture, separation of risk groups, treatment and changes in animal flow. Dermatophytosis was eliminated from the public adoption area within five months while admissions and adoptions continued. Of 273 culture-positive new admissions during one eight-month period, only sixty had the combined lesion, lamp and microscopy findings of true infection; the other 213 were judged mechanical carriers after follow-up. Disease later reappeared with cats returning from foster care, but the investigators could not determine where those cats originally became infected. The response illustrates the welfare value of distinguishing carriage from disease and protecting unexposed animals. It does not establish that every positive culture requires the same treatment or that a returning foster household caused transmission. [17]

## Hoarding investigations require examination alongside cultures

Researchers reviewing four large cat seizures screened for dermatophytosis in one investigation. Among seventy-six cats with suspicious skin lesions, sixty-nine grew Microsporum canis; approximately half were considered truly infected and half mechanical carriers. The selected lesional group is not a prevalence estimate for every seized cat or every multi-cat household. It is a particularly useful forensic example because even in a documented welfare investigation the culture result did not establish the same biological state in every animal. A defensible individual record retains lesion descriptions, photographs, identification, sampling dates, microscopy and repeat findings alongside environmental and care evidence. A positive ringworm result alone cannot establish neglect, the duration of suffering, who introduced an infection or whether an owner knew about it. [18]

## Skin lesions are common enough that appearance alone can mislead

An Ontario surveillance study sampled four hundred cats admitted to three shelters during a defined winter-to-spring period. None yielded dermatophytes, although seventy-seven had skin lesions and many samples grew other fungi. Positive laboratory control cultures grew as expected. This does not prove that those shelters or that region can never have ringworm: only one period and a few facilities were sampled, and no method has perfect sensitivity. It does show that visible skin disease is not synonymous with dermatophytosis and that background frequency varies greatly between settings. An outbreak response should be proportional to confirmed findings and exposure evidence. Proper testing protects animals both from missed contagious disease and from unnecessary treatment, confinement or disruption based only on nonspecific hair loss. [19]

## Treatment studies need a clear definition of cure

A 2025 randomized shelter-cat trial compared three topical approaches while all cats received oral itraconazole. The analysis included seventy-six cats and defined success as mycological cure by seven weeks. All lime-sulfur-group cats reached that endpoint; some cats in the alternative shampoo groups did not. Average cure times were shorter with lime sulfur, although the prespecified statistical test of the alternatives' inferiority remained inconclusive because confidence intervals, the estimated uncertainty ranges, crossed its margin. It compares combined treatment programs in cats, not topical products used alone or owner-selected substitutes. Veterinary monitoring also considers tolerance and temperature during bathing. The clinically relevant question is how the animal and viable fungal burden are changing under a supervised plan. [20]

## Environmental contamination is manageable and does not make a home hopeless

A laundering study tested fabrics contaminated experimentally and through contact with infected kittens. Naturally exposed towels and denim frequently retained small amounts of recoverable fungus after one wash, but none was detected after two washes under the tested conditions. The study demonstrates the importance of mechanical removal and adequate washing rather than assuming that every contaminated washable item must be discarded. Its exact findings belong to those fabrics, machines and protocols; they are not proof that any quick wash sterilizes every household material. The veterinary team can adapt cleaning to the setting and vulnerable household members. Keep contaminated hair and laundry contained during transport, preserve normal animal care and enrichment, and avoid turning environmental test results into a requirement for impossible whole-house sterility. [21]

## Cleaning and repeat testing should answer a defined clinical question

An experimental disinfectant study compared eight commercial products against spores from cat hair. All inhibited growth in a simple suspension test, but performance on contaminated textile surfaces depended on how thoroughly the product was applied. A laboratory fungicidal claim therefore does not mean that a light application over hair and debris will achieve the same result. Cleaning removes material; a suitable disinfectant acts on what remains, under its labeled conditions and veterinary advice. Do not improvise mixtures or apply environmental products to the animal. In practice, the useful endpoint is a recovering pet with a coherent examination and testing record, together with manageable transmission precautions. Repeating a coat PCR without deciding how its result will change care can prolong uncertainty instead of resolving it. [22]

# V022: Feline thyroid function testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Endocrinology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 2)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *If an older cat keeps losing weight while eating well, how can testing distinguish excess thyroid hormone from another disease, and how can follow up reveal too little hormone after treatment?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency for respiratory distress

A cat with labored or open mouth breathing needs emergency veterinary assessment now regardless of thyroid test results.

## Why the familiar cat becomes a different patient

The thyroid glands in the neck produce thyroxine, called T4, and triiodothyronine, called T3. Hyperthyroidism is excessive thyroid hormone production; the resulting increased metabolic activity can affect several organs. The familiar presentation is an older cat losing weight despite a good or increased appetite, sometimes with restlessness, increased drinking, vomiting, diarrhea, or an unkempt coat. A veterinarian may find enlarged thyroid tissue, a rapid heart rate, or other abnormalities. These findings raise a question rather than identify its answer: kidney disease, diabetes, and other illnesses can produce overlapping signs. Most hyperthyroid cats have increased blood T4, but some have values within the reference interval. Blood pressure and cardiac assessment may also matter because excess thyroid activity can accompany hypertension, meaning high blood pressure, and heart disease. A useful workup therefore describes the whole cat, including weight and muscle loss, instead of reducing the investigation to a number on a thyroid report. [1]

## The specimen journey can change the result

The clinic usually submits serum, the liquid left after clotting. Cornell's laboratory guidance describes allowing adequate clot formation, separating serum from cells, transferring it to a suitable storage vial, and keeping it chilled for shipment. These are professional specimen requirements, not instructions for owners to collect blood. The distinction between total and free hormone is especially important during transport. Total T4 includes hormone attached to proteins; free T4 is the unbound fraction. Warming, severe hemolysis, which is rupture of red blood cells, and certain low protein states can produce misleading free T4 results. Cornell uses equilibrium dialysis for feline free T4 which separates unbound from protein bound hormone before measurement, and explicitly states that its two step method used for canine samples is not valid for cats. That is a concrete example of why a test validated for dogs cannot simply be carried across to cats. A discordant result should prompt a check of specimen history and assay identity before an increasingly elaborate disease explanation. [2]

## A high free hormone result needs context

Peterson and colleagues' 2001 prospective series compared 917 untreated hyperthyroid cats, 221 cats with nonthyroidal disease, and 172 clinically normal cats. Total hormones were measured by radioimmunoassay, an antibody method using a radioactive signal, and free T4 by direct equilibrium dialysis. Free T4 detected more affected cats than total T4, but 14 of the 221 cats with other illness also had high free T4. Greater sensitivity, the ability to detect disease, came with lower specificity, the ability to avoid false positive labeling. Hypothetical worked example one: a cat with substantial unrelated illness has total T4 in the lower part of its interval and a modestly high free T4. Calling this hyperthyroidism from free T4 alone would ignore the study's central warning. The clinician reviews competing disease, specimen quality, repeat testing, and additional evidence. This invented interpretation exercise has no actual animal, treatment, or outcome; it illustrates why a more sensitive assay is not automatically a more decisive standalone test. [4]

## The pituitary signal adds a different view

Thyroid stimulating hormone, or TSH, is the pituitary signal encouraging thyroid activity. Excess thyroid hormone normally suppresses that signal. A 2015 prospective study used thyroid scintigraphy as its reference standard while evaluating T4, T3, free T4, and canine assay TSH in 917 hyperthyroid cats, 32 euthyroid suspect cats, and 131 healthy cats. Euthyroid means adequate thyroid function. Forty four hyperthyroid cats had total T4 within the study's reference interval, highlighting the problem of mild disease. Suppressed TSH was common in hyperthyroid cats, but the canine assay also returned undetectable values in some euthyroid cats. Undetectable means below the method's measurement limit, not necessarily absent. Combining TSH with thyroid hormone results improved specificity. The study supports a panel when results are ambiguous; it does not make an undetectable canine assay TSH sufficient proof of feline hyperthyroidism or make a single normal T4 an unconditional exclusion in a strongly suspicious cat. [5]

## Assay sensitivity is not the same as disease severity

A newer assay can change what undetectable means. Peterson, Dougherty, and Rishniw evaluated a bulk acoustic wave TSH assay in 2024, DOI 10.2460/ajvr.23.12.0278. Their population included 169 untreated hyperthyroid cats, 53 radioiodine treated cats, 12 cats with chronic kidney disease, and 78 healthy cats. The newer method could measure lower TSH concentrations than the canine chemiluminescent assay, helping distinguish genuinely suppressed TSH from low normal values. It performed better for identifying hyperthyroidism in that study and also helped assess treatment associated hypothyroidism. This is primary feline validation evidence, but it remains evidence about named assays in a defined population. The study does not authorize applying its decision limits to every laboratory's TSH method. When the method changes, the clinician needs its measurement limit and interpretive guidance. A laboratory's ability to resolve a lower concentration is an analytical property; it is not by itself a measure of how urgently the cat needs care. [6]

## A practical sequence when the first panel is unclear

Michigan State's feline guidance emphasizes clinical suspicion, total hormone, free hormone, and TSH together. Nonthyroidal illness can suppress total T4 into the interval in a genuinely hyperthyroid cat, while some euthyroid sick cats can have falsely high free T4. When signs are vague or results conflict, repeat testing after an appropriate interval and evaluation of other illness may be more informative than immediate labeling. Specialized imaging can be considered when uncertainty persists. Hypothetical worked example two: an older cat has progressive weight loss, a palpable thyroid nodule, and repeatedly high normal total T4. That combination keeps early hyperthyroidism in consideration without confirming it. A veterinarian may seek corroboration with free hormone, TSH, repeat measurements, or scintigraphy while investigating competing causes. This is an invented decision path, not a published case or prediction that any one next test will be positive. [3]

## Imaging maps function as well as anatomy

Thyroid scintigraphy uses an administered radioactive tracer and a gamma camera to show tissue that takes up the tracer. It can locate functioning thyroid tissue that a blood measurement cannot locate. Peterson and Broome described scintigraphic findings in 2,096 consecutive hyperthyroid cats in a specialist referral population. Disease distribution varied, and ectopic tissue, meaning thyroid tissue outside the expected location, and findings consistent with carcinoma were relatively uncommon. This is useful for treatment planning and for understanding why palpating only one nodule does not fully describe the thyroid system. Referral selection and the techniques used limit direct extrapolation to every older cat. The imaging pattern is also not a tissue diagnosis: a scan identifies distribution and activity, while malignancy may require additional evidence. A blood T4 value cannot reliably tell the clinician whether all active tissue is in one neck lobe, both lobes, or an unusual location. [7]

Quantification adds another layer. A 2016 prospective cross sectional study evaluated 524 cats referred for suspected hyperthyroidism, with disease confirmed in 504 and excluded in 20 using the thyroid panel. It compared thyroid to salivary and thyroid to background ratios with the percentage of injected tracer taken up by the thyroid. These indices ask related but different questions, and the study found that tracer uptake related particularly well to hormone concentrations and estimated thyroid volume. A ratio compares two measured regions; percent uptake compares thyroid activity with the administered tracer. Such measurements can support staging and planning, but the reference standard and the very unequal numbers of affected and unaffected cats should remain visible when judging performance. Quantitative imaging does not establish that a large active area is malignant, and its published values should not be transferred to a different camera, protocol, or clinical population without appropriate validation. [8]

## Follow up tests protect the kidneys as well as the thyroid

Hyperthyroidism and kidney assessment interact. A prospective before and after study of 262 cats treated successfully with radioiodine evaluated symmetric dimethylarginine, abbreviated SDMA, alongside creatinine, urine concentration, T4, and TSH. SDMA is a blood marker used to help assess kidney filtration. Peterson and colleagues' 2018 report found that pretreatment SDMA could help identify some cats that later became azotemic, but it was not sensitive enough to identify all of them. Azotemia means increased nitrogenous waste measurements in blood; it is not, by itself, a full explanation of the cause. The study required successful resolution of hyperthyroidism and reassessed cats months after treatment, so its findings do not describe every treated or untreated cat. A reassuring pretreatment kidney marker cannot guarantee normal kidney function after thyroid control. Monitoring needs the thyroid state, muscle condition, urine findings, and the trajectory of renal measurements together. [13]

A 2010 study by Williams, Elliott, and Syme examined treatment associated hypothyroidism and renal outcomes in 80 client owned cats using two overlapping retrospective study groups. Among 75 cats assessed six months after starting treatment, azotemia was more frequent in those classified hypothyroid than in those euthyroid. Hypothyroid cats that developed azotemia had shorter observed survival than hypothyroid cats remaining nonazotemic. This is an association in defined treated populations, not an individual lifespan forecast or proof that thyroid treatment inevitably damages kidneys. The key testing lesson is that low T4 after treatment can represent more than successful elimination of excess hormone. Concurrently increased TSH helps identify inadequate thyroid function, while other illness can also lower T4. The outcome analysis makes follow-up clinically meaningful but cannot remove confounding or establish which treatment is best for a different cat. Treatment choice and monitoring must account for its own benefits and limitations. [11]

A subsequent prospective interventional study followed 19 cats receiving antithyroid medication that had low T4 and high TSH, consistent with iatrogenic hypothyroidism. Iatrogenic means caused by medical treatment. Under veterinary supervision, the medication regimen was adjusted until thyroid hormone was again in the study's target interval. Plasma creatinine decreased significantly, and azotemia resolved in seven of the fourteen initially azotemic cats. Plasma is the liquid portion of anticoagulated blood. The investigators cautioned that concurrent body weight change could influence interpretation of creatinine; direct improvement in every aspect of kidney function cannot be assumed from that marker alone. This actual reported outcome supports identifying excessive thyroid suppression and reassessing it, rather than regarding a very low treated T4 as automatically desirable. It provides no safe owner directed adjustment rule. The observations concern a small selected group with documented treatment associated hypothyroidism and do not show that all kidney disease after treatment is reversible. [12]

## Low thyroid function is a separate feline question

Adult cats can occasionally develop spontaneous primary hypothyroidism. A 2018 prospective series described seven adults aged three and a half to eleven years. Six had low total and free T4, all seven had high TSH, and six had enlarged thyroids with intense tracer uptake. One had no visible thyroid tissue on imaging. After levothyroxine replacement, hormone results improved, goiters shrank, and increased creatinine normalized in the four initially azotemic cats. A goiter is an enlarged thyroid, not a synonym for hyperthyroidism. The intense uptake in these hypothyroid cats is a reminder that trapping a tracer and producing an adequate hormone supply are distinct processes. This small referral series establishes that adult primary hypothyroidism exists; it cannot estimate population prevalence. It also cannot justify diagnosing every low T4 in a sick adult cat as gland failure. The clinical pattern, TSH, imaging, and follow-up were used together, rather than relying on one low hormone concentration. [14]

## A published kitten case follows the entire diagnostic chain

Carvalho Abend and colleagues reported one two month old male Brazilian shorthair kitten in 2023, DOI 10.29374/2527-2179.bjvm003423. Constipation, profound inactivity, a broad flat face, short neck, delayed dental development, and abnormal skeletal maturation prompted endocrine investigation. Routine blood results were not remarkable, but free T4 by equilibrium dialysis and total T4 were low while TSH was increased. Thyroid enlargement supported a hormone synthesis problem. The kitten received levothyroxine replacement with serial clinical and hormone monitoring. Activity, defecation, coat, body size, and growth improved; clinical stabilization was reported after 120 days. Scintigraphy roughly a year into treatment showed enlarged lobes with intense uptake, and hormone values were within the reported intervals at seventeen months of age while receiving replacement. The report does not establish a genetic mutation or prove that every congenital form behaves similarly. Its treatment included repeated adjustments, and there was no untreated control. Published units are inconsistent in parts of the report, so numerical hormone thresholds are deliberately not transferred from this case. [9]

## A published neck mass case separates function from cancer

Oramas, Boston, and Wavreille's 2020 report described one fourteen year old neutered male Tonkinese cat with an incidental neck mass and no hyperthyroid signs. Total and free T4 were initially within that laboratory's intervals. Ultrasound, radiographs, needle sampling, and computed tomography investigated the lesion; initial sampling did not definitively establish malignancy. Surgical removal of the left thyroid lesion was followed by histopathology, microscopic examination of tissue, which diagnosed thyroid carcinoma. At last follow-up, 831 days after surgery, the cat had a right thyroid mass, hyperthyroidism, and suspected pulmonary metastases. Metastasis means spread of cancer to another site; the lung findings remained suspected rather than tissue confirmed. No DOI is listed in the indexed article. This one animal shows why normal thyroid hormones cannot exclude a thyroid tumor: hormone production and malignant behavior are different properties. The report cannot show that surgery alone is equivalent to radioiodine or another treatment, and 831 days is a follow-up observation rather than a demonstrated cure or a universal survival estimate. [10]

## Monitoring remains a clinical assessment

The authored AAFP guidelines describe antithyroid medication, radioiodine, surgery, and dietary iodine restriction as approaches with different requirements and follow-up needs. Monitoring combines thyroid hormone results with the examination, body weight, kidney measures, and surveillance for adverse effects appropriate to the treatment. A biochemical target is therefore part of a continuing clinical assessment. Repeated total T4 from a reliable laboratory helps follow trends, while TSH can clarify suspected treatment associated hypothyroidism. The guidelines also emphasize that concurrent kidney or heart disease needs attention rather than abandonment of thyroid assessment. Treatment response can answer whether a management plan is working, but a cat becoming quieter or gaining weight does not independently prove that every original sign arose from its thyroid. Changes in appetite, activity, urination, gastrointestinal signs, and medication administration are useful history for the clinician. Owners should report those changes and follow a planned recheck schedule rather than modify medication on the basis of an isolated result. [15]

## Urgency follows breathing and overall stability

A slowly changing weight or a borderline thyroid value warrants veterinary evaluation but does not itself identify an immediate endocrine emergency. A cat struggling to breathe, including open mouth breathing, needs emergency veterinary assessment now. Cornell's feline emergency guidance makes clear that respiratory distress can arise from heart failure, asthma, fluid around the lungs, and other causes. The thyroid may be relevant, but it must not become an assumed explanation that delays stabilizing the animal or investigating another cause. There is no hormone concentration in this document that supplies an owner with a countdown to deterioration. The clinical sign demands action even if a recent thyroid panel appeared reassuring. A test of thyroid function cannot replace assessment of oxygenation, the heart and lungs, or the cat's ability to remain stable during further investigation. [16]

# V019: Veterinary tissue biopsy and histopathology

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *A tissue diagnosis depends on what was sampled, what survived processing and how the microscopic findings fit the dog or cat in front of the veterinarian.*

## The diagnostic question begins before the tissue reaches the laboratory

A biopsy is tissue collected from a living animal for examination. Histopathology is the study of that tissue's microscopic structure: which cells are present, how they are arranged, and whether they invade neighboring structures. Cytology examines individual cells or small groups, often from a needle sample. The American Animal Hospital Association's 2026 oncology guidelines describe why cytology can establish many round-cell tumor diagnoses, while histology often adds architecture, invasiveness and grade. Neither method automatically makes the other unnecessary. A fatty-looking lump, an inflamed lesion and a malignant tumor can resemble one another clinically; the investigation should identify what uncertainty the proposed sample will resolve. [1]

Patient history is part of histopathologic interpretation. Species, breed, age, lesion location, duration, distribution, previous treatment and imaging findings can change the meaning of the same microscopic pattern. The University of Prince Edward Island requests this history, relevant laboratory results and a description of the lesion's size and appearance; clinical photographs can help. Signalment means the animal's basic identifying clinical characteristics. A report saying 'ulceration and inflammation' may be accurate yet incomplete if the submitted fragment came from an ulcer covering a deeper mass. Explaining exactly where the specimen came from helps the veterinarian and pathologist decide whether the original question has actually been answered. [2]

## Select the lesion and the depth that can answer the question

For skin disease, the newest visible abnormality is not always the best specimen, and the oldest damaged patch is often misleading. Texas A&M's diagnostic laboratory favors well-developed primary lesions: changes caused directly by the disease, rather than scratching, scarring or healing. Crusts and scales can contain important evidence. Sampling several appropriately selected sites can show a pattern that one fragment misses. Previous corticosteroids and secondary infections may alter that pattern, so preparation belongs in an individualized veterinary plan. Owners should provide a complete medication history; this does not translate into stopping treatment themselves or applying a universal waiting period to a deteriorating animal. [3]

Depth matters because skin includes several different diagnostic compartments. Disease centered in the epidermis, the outer surface, differs from disease of hair follicles or subcutaneous fat. The University of Guelph explains that a superficial sample can miss panniculitis, inflammation of that deeper fat, and that fragile lesions require special consideration. The appropriate edge or center also depends on the suspected process: active pigment loss, a pustule, an ulcer and hair loss do not share one ideal sampling location. When a feature expected clinically is absent from the slides, the team should consider whether the lesion was represented before treating a negative-looking section as proof that the disease is absent. [4]

## Preserve both tissue structure and the tests that may still be needed

Fixation stabilizes tissue so that its cells and architecture survive processing. Neutral buffered formalin is a standard laboratory fixative, but penetration into a large or dense specimen is limited; an intact-looking surface can surround a poorly preserved center. The University of Missouri's submission guidance therefore addresses specimen thickness, prompt fixation and recorded fixation time. Receiving laboratories differ in some handling requirements, so the submitting veterinary team should follow the laboratory's instructions for that specimen. A large excision, an eye and tiny endoscopic fragments need different planning. Fixation is a controlled diagnostic step, not simply placing every possible specimen into the same container. [5]

Some questions need material that has not been fixed. Bacterial or fungal culture seeks organisms that can grow, while toxicology and other ancillary tests may require separately handled tissue. Ancillary means additional to the routine microscopic examination. Calgary's Diagnostic Services Unit explicitly notes that fresh samples may be necessary to confirm a suspected diagnosis. This allocation is best planned before collection because formalin-preserved tissue cannot later be converted back into a fresh culture specimen. The clinician and laboratory decide which portions serve histology and which serve other tests, maintaining their individual identities. Finding inflammation without having reserved appropriate material can leave the cause unresolved despite technically good slides. [6]

## Orientation connects a microscopic finding to a place in the animal

A surgical margin is the cut boundary of an excised specimen. If tumor reaches one boundary, the surgeon needs to know whether that boundary was deep, toward the head, or on another side. Tennessee's biopsy service recommends orientation markers and surgical marking dye when margin assessment is needed. These markers require a clear key; an unexplained suture does not identify an anatomic direction. The submitted specimen's orientation should be preserved during handling and laboratory trimming. Cutting through an important boundary without documenting it can make a later measurement difficult to interpret. A useful margin result is a mapped observation, not merely the word 'clear' printed without context. [7]

The direction in which tissue is sectioned can also determine whether a lesion is visible. Hair follicles are three-dimensional structures, and a folded or poorly oriented skin fragment may not show them in an interpretable plane. Georgia's dermatology guidance describes both orientation concerns and crush artifact, distortion caused by squeezing delicate tissue. Damage may erase subtle inflammatory features or make cell types unrecognizable. The reader should distinguish a genuinely normal sampled structure from a structure the preparation did not preserve. If the clinical pattern strongly conflicts with the report, discussion with the pathologist may identify a need for differently oriented sections or a better representative specimen. [8]

## From the specimen to the slide

In the laboratory, gross examination records visible features and selects tissue for microscopic processing. Histotechnicians dehydrate the selected pieces, embed them in supporting paraffin wax, and cut very thin sections with a microtome, a precision cutting instrument. The University of Delaware describes how orientation is maintained through this sequence. Sections are placed on glass and routinely stained with hematoxylin and eosin, abbreviated H&E, so tissue relationships become interpretable under the microscope. The finished slide is a thin plane through selected material. It is not a transparent view of every cell in the entire mass; selection and sectioning remain part of what the result can establish. [9]

Hard tissues require additional processing. Michigan State University's guidance explains that bone specimens may need decalcification, removal of mineral so thin sections can be cut, and that superficial bone samples can miss the underlying disease. Radiographs help the pathologist relate a sampled area to lysis, destruction of bone, or new bone formation. An inflammatory reaction around a bone tumor and an infection can overlap in a small fragment. The location of the biopsy therefore belongs with the imaging. Extra processing or consultation can extend reporting time without implying that the specimen has been forgotten. Clinical support for an unstable or painful animal proceeds while that diagnostic work continues. [10]

## Recognize damage and sampling limits before interpreting disease

Artifact is a change introduced by collection, storage or processing rather than by the living disease. Texas A&M illustrates how heat from cautery can distort cells at surgical edges, freezing can leave crystal-related gaps, and crushing can destroy detail. After death, autolysis, breakdown by the tissue's own enzymes, and bacterial overgrowth can further obscure lesions. Severe artifact may prevent a confident diagnosis or margin assessment. It should be described as a limitation rather than silently interpreted as normality. The appearance of damaged tissue also cannot by itself establish when an injury occurred, whether it happened before death, or who was responsible for an animal's condition. [11]

Sampling limitations were demonstrated directly in Kemp and colleagues' study of livers from seventy dogs undergoing necropsy. Diagnoses from needle, cup and punch samples agreed with larger comparison samples in 66%, 60% and 69%, respectively; the differences among those proportions were not statistically significant. Even the larger punch specimens did not solve every disagreement. Portal triads are small regions containing a branch of the bile duct and blood vessels, used to judge how much liver architecture is represented. This was a postmortem sampling experiment, not evidence that one technique improved survival. Its practical lesson is that a technically acceptable fragment may still incompletely represent disease within an organ. [12]

## Intestinal biopsies require species and compartment context

The 2026 American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine guidance for canine chronic inflammatory enteropathy emphasizes adequate specimens from the relevant intestinal segments and proper orientation. Enteropathy means disease of the intestine. A well-oriented mucosal sample shows the villi, the fingerlike absorptive projections, and underlying structures; a collection of villous tips cannot answer all the same questions. The guideline recommends systematic assessment using World Small Animal Veterinary Association criteria or their modified form. Additional stains and molecular tests are selected according to the clinical suspicion and initial histology. These are canine recommendations, and endoscopic mucosal samples do not automatically represent deeper wall layers or every intestinal segment. [13]

Cats present a particularly difficult distinction between lymphoplasmacytic inflammation and low-grade intestinal T-cell lymphoma. Lymphoplasmacytic means the infiltrate contains lymphocytes and plasma cells; T cells are one family of lymphocyte. The 2023 American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine consensus found that no single known marker reliably separates these conditions in every cat. Histopathology remains central, but the diagnosis integrates symptoms, imaging, tissue distribution and additional testing. A dense lymphocyte population is not a complete explanation by itself. The distinction matters because prognosis and management can differ, while the evidence supporting some diagnostic recommendations remains moderate or low rather than definitive. [14]

## Additional stains refine an interpretation rather than replace it

Immunohistochemistry, or IHC, uses antibodies to detect selected proteins, called antigens, within tissue sections. A pattern of markers can help identify a tumor's cell lineage or support an infectious diagnosis. Veterinary laboratory guidelines emphasize that antigen detection alone does not necessarily diagnose the disease: staining must be interpreted with tissue appearance and other findings. Validated methods and appropriate controls help distinguish the intended signal from background staining or technical failure. Likewise, a negative stain can reflect absent antigen, poor preservation or limitations of the antibody in that species and specimen. A panel chosen for the actual diagnostic question is more meaningful than one isolated positive label. [15]

Polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangements, shortened to PARR, examines rearranged lymphocyte receptor genes. A dominant repeated pattern supports expansion of a related cell population, called clonality. Clonality is not synonymous with cancer, and a negative result cannot exclude every lymphoma. Colorado State University's assay guidance shows why specimen type and species matter: canine formalin-fixed samples use a different primer set from fresh specimens, and performance estimates do not transfer freely between them. Primers are short DNA sequences that direct what the reaction can amplify. Low target-cell numbers, preservation and the tested gene regions can all affect the answer, so morphology and clinical context remain essential. [16]

## Documented examples show why an abnormal slide needs clinical follow-up

Marsilio and colleagues investigated intestinal specimens from twenty clinically healthy, client-owned cats aged at least three years. Integrated histology, immunostains and clonality were interpreted as small-cell lymphoma in twelve and emerging lymphoma in one. Yet seventeen cats remained without signs of chronic enteropathy after a median follow-up of 709 days. Three developed gastrointestinal signs; two were euthanized 295 and 654 days after endoscopy. These observations do not prove that every suspicious result was false or that future disease was impossible. They show that diagnostic patterns developed in selected populations may overstate clinically consequential disease when applied to outwardly healthy older cats, and that follow-up remains part of interpretation. [17]

An individually reported eight-year-old Mastiff illustrates the opposite danger: assuming that a later change must be the same disease becoming worse. Initial lymph-node cytology, histology, IHC and flow cytometry supported T-zone lymphoma. Thirteen months later, progressive node enlargement was associated with a large B-cell lymphoma. Sequencing performed after euthanasia supported two distinct concurrent clones rather than simple transformation of the first clone. The dog was euthanized two months after the B-cell diagnosis, fifteen months after the original lymphoma diagnosis; no necropsy was performed. The case supports reassessing a changed clinical and cellular pattern. It does not establish how often two separate lymphomas occur together. [18]

## Grade describes biological behavior within a defined tumor type

Tumor grade summarizes microscopic features associated with behavior, such as differentiation and cell division. It differs from stage, which describes the extent of disease in the animal. Kiupel and colleagues studied ninety-five canine cutaneous mast-cell tumors from ninety-five dogs, with blinded grading by twenty-eight pathologists. All dogs received surgical excision alone; surviving dogs were followed for up to four years. Their proposed two-tier system separated high-grade tumors associated with a median survival under four months from low-grade tumors associated with survival beyond two years. These are historical group outcomes, not a countdown for an individual dog. The result belongs to canine tumors arising in skin; it should not be transplanted unchanged to feline mast-cell disease or to tumors in a different tissue compartment. [19]

A 2026 prospective study enrolled thirty-two dogs to examine how soft-tissue sarcoma grade varied within one tumor. Although motivated by preoperative planning, investigators obtained one central and two superficial peripheral punch samples after removal, comparing their grades with the resection specimen. Reported single-biopsy grade agreement was 71% centrally and 59% peripherally. These percentages describe biopsy-to-resection comparisons; peripheral tissue was sampled twice per dog, so they are not independent dog-level outcomes. Undergrading occurred at both locations, and multiple samples improved agreement. The study did not test a survival benefit. A small low-grade specimen therefore may incompletely represent a heterogeneous mass. [20]

## Margins are measured evidence with a sampling method

International veterinary surgical-pathology recommendations distinguish the submitted specimen, the way it is trimmed, and the information eventually reported. A perpendicular section can show tumor-to-edge distance; a tangential section examines tissue along a boundary and answers a different geometric question. Neither approach inspects every possible point of the entire surgical surface. Reports should identify relevant margins, distance when measurable, and the tissue composing the intervening boundary. The Kamstock consensus itself acknowledges that many technical recommendations arose from collective experience rather than definitive trials. Thus, 'complete in the sections examined' is an evidential statement whose meaning depends on orientation, processing and the tumor's known behavior. [21]

A study of ninety-four dogs with ninety-eight cutaneous soft-tissue sarcomas compared three margin categories. At three years, estimated cumulative local recurrence was 41.2% with infiltrated margins, 22.5% with tumor-free but close margins, and 7.1% with tumor-free margins. These are follow-up estimates, not simple fractions of all enrolled dogs, and four dogs had two tumors. The findings show why collapsing every tumor-free boundary into one category loses information. Conversely, an infiltrated margin did not mean that every tumor recurred. The veterinarian combines the microscopic measurement with grade, location, other treatment and the feasibility and burden of further intervention for that animal. [22]

## Record the outcome as well as the diagnosis

In a separate selected series, thirty-three dogs underwent scar revision after a narrowly or incompletely excised soft-tissue sarcoma, despite having no visible residual tumor. One dog developed local recurrence; median follow-up was 1,127 days, with a wide range of one to 3,192 days. That recurrence occurred 201 days after revision, although no residual tumor had been identified in the revision specimen. The result supports discussing further assessment after an unplanned excision while retaining uncertainty: the study was not a randomized comparison with observation alone. Neither an apparently empty scar specimen nor a favorable group recurrence rate guarantees freedom from later disease in an individual dog. [23]

A nasal-biopsy series included sixteen dogs and fourteen cats. Computed tomography, or CT, helped guide sampling; twenty-eight of thirty samples were diagnostically adequate, and twenty-six yielded clinically relevant diagnoses. Eleven animals had minor immediate nasal bleeding. One dog required readmission for increased bleeding the next day; bleeding settled during observation but recurred intermittently after discharge. That dog had a malignant neoplasm, and additional immunostaining was not pursued. This individual outcome matters alongside the diagnostic-yield figures. A useful tissue diagnosis does not make the procedure risk-free or settle subsequent treatment, and continuing bleeding or clinical deterioration requires reassessment rather than waiting passively for a final label. [24]

## Forensic meaning and the next clinical decision

In a welfare or forensic investigation, histology sits within a broader record. Liverpool's forensic pathology service describes examination of the major organs, photographs, continuity of specimen custody and additional testing when indicated. Continuity of custody records who handled the evidence and helps establish that the examined material belongs to the identified animal. Microscopy may support disease, tissue injury or a biological explanation for wasting, but an isolated lesion does not establish neglect, the precise time of an event or a responsible person. Those conclusions require the clinical history, scene and husbandry information, competing explanations, and a clearly stated account of what the specimen could not preserve or reveal. [25]

The final report should be read with the question that prompted sampling. Does it identify a specific disease, describe a pattern with several possible causes, or explain why the specimen was nondiagnostic? Michigan State University's biopsy guidance encourages direct discussion with the pathologist and accepts existing slides or paraffin blocks for consultation and additional testing. A mismatch between the animal and the report can justify review of deeper sections, another stain or a more representative sample; it does not automatically make either observer wrong. The useful endpoint is a documented next decision tied to the animal's condition, with uncertainty and follow-up recorded alongside the microscopic findings. [26]

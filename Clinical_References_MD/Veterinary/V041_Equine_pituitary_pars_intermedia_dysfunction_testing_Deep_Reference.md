# V041: Equine pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary

- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-09

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Why does every healthy horse on earth double or triple its plasma ACTH concentration between August and October—and how does a 10-minute Thyrotropin-Releasing Hormone (TRH) stimulation test catch early Equine Cushing's when resting ACTH is borderline?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Acute PPID-associated laminitis / sole abscess vs elective endocrine screening and pergolide titration

Aged horses (> 15 years) presenting with acute autumn laminitis, recurrent subsolar abscesses, or corneal ulcers require immediate foot stabilization alongside chilled EDTA plasma ACTH testing; stable horses with delayed spring coat shedding (hypertrichosis), epaxial muscle atrophy, pendulous abdomen, or polyuria/polydipsia undergo scheduled basal ACTH or TRH stimulation testing.

## Why Equine PPID is a dopaminergic neurodegenerative disease of the pars intermedia—and why the Dexamethasone Suppression Test is obsolete

In humans and dogs, Cushing's disease is caused by a tumor of the **pars distalis (anterior lobe)** of the pituitary gland (corticotropes) or an adrenal cortical tumor. In horses (*Equus caballus*), **Pituitary Pars Intermedia Dysfunction (PPID)**—affecting 20 to 25 percent of horses over 15 years of age—has a completely different neuroanatomical origin: age-related oxidative neurodegeneration of the **hypothalamic periventricular dopaminergic neurons** that project down the infundibular stalk to tonically inhibit the **melanotropes of the pars intermedia (intermediate lobe)** via **Dopamine D2 receptors**! Deprived of hypothalamic dopamine inhibition, pars intermedia melanotropes undergo hyperplasia and hypertrophy into pituitary macroadenomas, overproducing the pro-hormone **Pro-Opiomelanocortin (POMC)** and cleaving it into **alpha-Melanocyte-Stimulating Hormone (alpha-MSH)**, **beta-endorphin** (causing lethargy and decreased pain sensitivity), **Corticotropin-Like Intermediate Lobe Peptide (CLIP)**, and **Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH)** (a large fraction of which is biologically attenuated, explaining why PPID horses rarely develop adrenal cortical hypertrophy despite high immunoreactive ACTH!). [1, 4, 5]

Understanding that PPID originates in **dopamine-regulated pars intermedia melanotropes** rather than glucocorticoid-regulated pars distalis corticotropes explains why the historical **Overnight Dexamethasone Suppression Test (DST)** is now **obsolete and no longer recommended** by the Equine Endocrinology Group (EEG): pars intermedia melanotropes lack normal glucocorticoid negative-feedback receptors until late in the disease, making the DST insensitive in early PPID, requiring two farm visits 19 hours apart, and carrying a theoretical risk of precipitating acute laminitis by injecting exogenous dexamethasone into an insulin-dysregulated horse! [1, 4]

## Basal Endogenous Plasma ACTH: Chilled plastic EDTA rules and the Autumnal Seasonal Surge

Measuring **Basal Endogenous Plasma ACTH** is the frontline screening test for equine PPID, but accurate results depend on two strict analytical and biological rules:
1. **Pre-Analytical Sample Handling (Chilled Plastic EDTA)**: Equine ACTH is a fragile 39-amino-acid polypeptide that is cleaved by endogenous plasma proteases at room temperature and adsorbs onto glass surfaces. Blood must be collected into a **plastic purple-top EDTA tube** (using a chilled tube or placing the tube immediately onto ice packs), **centrifuged within 4 hours** (ideally within 1 to 2 hours; never leave whole blood unseparated overnight!), and the separated plasma shipped overnight on frozen ice packs to the reference laboratory (noting that severe hemolysis degrades ACTH and falsely lowers results!). [1, 4, 6]

2. **The Autumnal Seasonal Surge (Mid-July to Mid-November in the Northern Hemisphere; Mid-January to Mid-May in the Southern Hemisphere)**: Because horses are seasonal photoperiodic breeders and metabolic adapters, declining daylength after the summer solstice triggers a massive physiological surge in pars intermedia POMC, alpha-MSH, and ACTH output in *all* healthy horses and ponies to promote winter lipogenesis and haircoat growth (Donaldson et al., J Vet Intern Med 2005)! In September and October, a healthy horse's plasma ACTH routinely rises 2- to 3-fold above its spring baseline, while a horse with early PPID exhibits an exaggerated, prolonged seasonal surge (> 100 to 500+ pg/mL). Therefore, plasma ACTH **MUST be interpreted against week/month-specific and analyzer-specific reference intervals** (Immulite 2000 XPi reads ~15 to 25 percent lower than Immulite 1000!). Per updated EEG guidelines (using Immulite 2000 XPi):
- **Non-Autumn Baseline (December through June)**: Negative **< 30 pg/mL**; Equivocal **30 to 50 pg/mL**; Positive for PPID **> 50 pg/mL**.
- **Early/Late Seasonal Transition (July & November)**: Negative **< 40 pg/mL**; Equivocal **40 to 75 pg/mL**; Positive **> 75 pg/mL**.
- **Peak Autumn Surge (August through October)**: Negative **< 50 to 65 pg/mL**; Equivocal **50 to 100 pg/mL**; Positive **> 100 pg/mL**.
Notice a fascinating clinical paradox: rather than avoiding testing in the autumn, **September and October are actually the MOST sensitive months of the year to catch early PPID using a single basal ACTH blood draw**, because the seasonal photoperiod challenge naturally amplifies the gap between normal horses and hyperplastic PPID melanotropes (Copas & Durham, Equine Vet J 2012)! [1, 2, 7]

## The 10-minute Thyrotropin-Releasing Hormone (TRH) Stimulation Test for early or equivocal PPID

During the non-autumn months (December through June), resting basal plasma ACTH has high specificity (> 90 percent) in moderate-to-advanced PPID (such as a 24-year-old pony with a curly wooly coat that never sheds), but **only 50 to 65 percent sensitivity in early PPID** (horses presenting with subtle topline epaxial muscle loss, poor athletic performance, or unexplained subsolar hoof abscesses whose resting ACTH falls in the normal [< 30 pg/mL] or equivocal [30–50 pg/mL] zone). To unmask early pars intermedia hyperplasia without waiting for autumn, the **Thyrotropin-Releasing Hormone (TRH) Stimulation Test** is the gold-standard dynamic test. [1, 3, 4]

The physiological mechanism is elegant: while healthy pars distalis corticotropes respond minimally to TRH, **hyperplastic pars intermedia melanotropes strongly express TRH receptors** and release a massive bolus of stored ACTH into the bloodstream within minutes of intravenous TRH injection (Beech et al., JAVMA 2007)!
- **TRH Stimulation Protocol (Recommended December through June)**:
  1. Collect a baseline (**T0**) EDTA plasma sample for ACTH (horse may have normal access to hay, no grain within 4–6 hours).
  2. Inject **1.0 mg of synthetic TRH (protirelin)** intravenously for horses > 250 kg (or **0.5 mg IV** for ponies/miniature horses <= 250 kg; transient yawning, lip-smacking, flehmen, trembling, or coughing for 1–3 minutes after injection is normal and benign!).
  3. Collect the stimulated EDTA plasma sample **exactly 10 minutes (T10)** after TRH injection (**timing is critical**: ACTH peaks sharply at 5 to 10 minutes and drops rapidly by 20 to 30 minutes!).
- **TRH 10-Minute Cutoffs (Non-Autumn, Immulite 2000 XPi)**:
  - **Negative for PPID**: T10 ACTH **< 110 pg/mL**.
  - **Equivocal**: T10 ACTH **110 to 200 pg/mL**.
  - **Positive for PPID**: T10 ACTH **> 200 pg/mL** (or > 110 pg/mL with strong clinical signs). [1, 3, 4]

## Documented clinical cases: Spring topline loss caught by TRH stim and ALWAYS pairing PPID with Insulin!

A classic equine internal medicine case involves a 16-year-old Warmblood dressage gelding evaluated in April for progressive loss of topline epaxial musculature over the thoracolumbar spine, lethargy under saddle, and delayed shedding of long guard hairs under the chin and jugular groove. Basal EDTA plasma ACTH in April was **34 pg/mL** (equivocal non-autumn range 30–50 pg/mL). A 1.0-mg intravenous TRH stimulation test was performed: exactly 10 minutes post-TRH, his plasma ACTH surged from 34 pg/mL to **485 pg/mL** (positive cutoff > 200 pg/mL), confirming early Pituitary Pars Intermedia Dysfunction. Initiating oral **pergolide mesylate** (a Dopamine D2 receptor agonist that replaces the lost hypothalamic dopamine inhibition at 2 mcg/kg/day; 1 mg/day for a 500-kg horse) restored his topline musculature, energy, and normal coat shedding within 12 weeks. [1, 3, 8]

A second documented clinical mandate involves a 21-year-old Quarter Horse mare diagnosed with classic PPID (generalized hypertrichosis, polydipsia, October ACTH 410 pg/mL) who had never shown overt lameness. Because **~30 to 60 percent of horses with PPID have concurrent Insulin Dysregulation**—and because it is the *insulin concentration*, NOT the ACTH concentration itself, that directly predicts whether a PPID horse will develop catastrophic laminitis!—an Oral Sugar Test was performed alongside her PPID workup, revealing a 60-minute post-syrup insulin of **165 uIU/mL**. Identifying silent hyperinsulinemia enabled strict low-NSC dietary restriction and therapeutic shoeing alongside pergolide therapy. [1, 4, 9]

## Monitoring Pergolide (Prascend) therapy and avoiding stress/TRH-shortage pitfalls

Once oral pergolide mesylate therapy is started, plasma ACTH (and insulin) should be rechecked at **30 days** and subsequently **twice yearly**—once in the spring and once during the **August–October autumn peak**—to verify that the pergolide dose suppresses the autumn seasonal surge. Notably, if a horse is already receiving pergolide and a TRH stimulation test or basal ACTH is planned, pergolide should be given on schedule (unless specifically testing untreated baseline), and clinicians must avoid drawing basal ACTH immediately after **trailer transport, dental floating, twitching, acute colic/laminitic pain, or intense exercise**, all of which trigger physiological pars distalis stress ACTH spikes that can falsely mimic PPID. [1, 4, 10]

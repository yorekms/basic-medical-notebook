# V023: Canine adrenal function testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog
- **Clinical Category**: Endocrinology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 2)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When a dog has recurrent stomach upset or excessive thirst, how can testing show too little adrenal hormone, too much, or a normal stress response that resembles disease?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency for suspected adrenal crisis

Collapse or profound weakness with persistent vomiting and deterioration needs emergency veterinary assessment now. A screening result alone does not establish a crisis.

## One gland system can fail in opposite directions

Hypoadrenocorticism means inadequate function of the adrenal cortex, the hormone producing outer layer, with deficiency of glucocorticoids such as cortisol, mineralocorticoids involved in salt and water regulation, or both. Primary disease originates in the adrenal glands; secondary disease can arise from insufficient stimulation from the pituitary gland at the base of the brain. Cortisol deficiency with normal sodium and potassium is recognized. Normal electrolytes therefore do not close the investigation. [6]

## Recurrent gastrointestinal disease can conceal adrenal deficiency

Vomiting, diarrhea, poor appetite, weight loss, and lethargy can suggest a gastrointestinal disorder while also occurring with hypoadrenocorticism. A 2020 multicenter prospective study enrolled 151 dogs with gastrointestinal signs lasting more than three weeks and performed a standardized workup. Six were diagnosed with hypoadrenocorticism by adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation testing; none had the classic combination of low sodium or high potassium. The presenting history, examination, and routine laboratory variables did not distinguish them reliably from dogs with other chronic gastrointestinal disease. This is evidence for considering adrenal function in that clinical setting, not a claim that four percent of all dogs have adrenal disease. Referral patterns, enrollment criteria, and the diagnostic protocol define the population. Low albumin, a major blood protein, or persistent gastrointestinal signs can justify a broader investigation, but neither finding specifies the adrenal glands as the cause without functional testing. [5]

## Testing for excess cortisol starts with clinical suspicion

The authored ACVIM consensus recommends endocrine testing for spontaneous hyperadrenocorticism, excessive cortisol effects also called Cushing syndrome, when compatible clinical signs are present. Routine biochemical abnormalities alone do not establish it. Increased thirst and urination, increased appetite, panting, muscle weakness, abdominal enlargement, and skin changes can build suspicion, but competing disorders must remain in view. Every screening and differentiating test has false results, and imaging is complementary. Hypothetical worked example one: a dog has an isolated high alkaline phosphatase activity, a blood enzyme measurement, on a wellness panel but no convincing clinical pattern. That enzyme abnormality is a reason to investigate its cause, not sufficient evidence to diagnose Cushing's syndrome. The example is invented and has no reported outcome. Testing a low suspicion population can produce misleading positives, so the question to be answered should be explicit before choosing the hormonal challenge. [7]

## The sample needs the right identity and timeline

A cortisol test and an endogenous ACTH test require different specimen handling. Cornell describes serum for cortisol: blood clots, cells are separated, and serum is transferred and kept appropriately chilled or frozen for shipment. Endogenous ACTH, the dog's own pituitary hormone, is collected as EDTA plasma, the liquid portion of blood prevented from clotting by an additive. This fragile hormone is particularly vulnerable to heat and time on cells, so prompt processing, suitable plastic containers, and cold transport matter. A falsely low ACTH can create an incorrect impression about the source of disease. With a stimulation or suppression test, every tube also needs its true time relative to the administered agent. Laboratory protocols differ, including formulation and timing details; the clinician follows the receiving laboratory's current validated protocol rather than combining instructions from different institutions. The resulting record should identify the actual procedure performed. [3]

## Assay validation matters even when the hormone is familiar

Immunoassays use antibodies to recognize a substance and convert a signal into a concentration. A chemiluminescent method uses light as that signal. In a 2003 canine ACTH validation study, Scott Moncrieff and colleagues evaluated precision, recovery after dilution, and biological response after corticotropin releasing hormone, an upstream pituitary stimulus, in three dogs. The chemiluminescent assay correlated with an established immunoradiometric method but measured systematically lower ACTH. Adding the protein degrading enzyme inhibitor aprotinin also lowered the measured result in that system. Thus, an additive that preserves a hormone under one analytical arrangement cannot be assumed suitable for every method. Precision describes repeatability; it does not guarantee agreement with another assay or clinical truth. This small validation study informs sample and method interpretation, not a universal diagnostic interval. Results need the laboratory's own validated limits and the specimen conditions used to establish them. [10]

## A resting cortisol is mainly an exclusion tool

Gold and colleagues' 2016 retrospective study evaluated 522 dogs investigated with ACTH stimulation, including 163 with hypoadrenocorticism, 351 with other illness, and eight equivocal cases. Its results supported the value of a sufficiently high resting cortisol for making adrenal deficiency unlikely, using the study's methods and decision limits. Low values still required interpretation and often confirmatory testing. Numerical decision limits should be obtained from the laboratory performing the current assay. Hypothetical worked example two: a dog with chronic diarrhea has a low resting cortisol and normal electrolytes. That does not prove Addison disease and does not exclude it. The veterinarian checks prior drugs and arranges appropriate confirmatory testing. This is an invented reasoning example with no treatment or outcome, not a recommendation to apply one research threshold to every assay. [8]

## Stimulation and suppression ask different questions

An ACTH stimulation test measures cortisol before and after a clinic administered synthetic version of the pituitary signal. It asks about adrenocortical reserve, the gland's capacity to respond. An inadequate response can support cortisol deficiency, but prior drug exposure can imitate that pattern. The low dose dexamethasone suppression test instead challenges feedback control: dexamethasone is an administered glucocorticoid expected to suppress pituitary stimulation and cortisol output in an appropriately responsive system. The pattern across timed samples helps investigate Cushing's syndrome. These dynamic tests examine a response over time rather than one unprovoked concentration. The European endocrine society emphasizes that laboratory decision limits must be established for the methods used and that endogenous ACTH is a differentiating test rather than an initial screening substitute. A resting cortisol alone does not screen for Cushing's syndrome. Urinary corticoid screening is also method dependent and should not be presented as universally sensitive. [1]

## Drugs can alter the gland and the measurement

Michigan State's laboratory guidance separates analytical interference from biological suppression. Some administered glucocorticoids cross react in cortisol assays, so an apparent cortisol concentration can partly represent medication. Chronic glucocorticoid exposure can also suppress the pituitary adrenal system and cause adrenal atrophy, producing a weak stimulation response. Skin, ear, and eye preparations belong in the drug history as well as tablets and injections. Dexamethasone does not cross react in that laboratory's cortisol assay but can still suppress endogenous hormone production. Nonadrenal illness can cause false positive tests for hypercortisolism, and uncontrolled diabetes can make interpretation particularly difficult. These distinctions require a complete medication and illness timeline. They are not instructions to stop a steroid or delay essential care. The clinician chooses an investigation compatible with the dog's immediate needs and discusses the actual assay with the laboratory when recent treatment complicates interpretation. [2]

## Normal electrolytes do not measure aldosterone reserve

Aldosterone helps regulate sodium, potassium, and fluid balance. Baumstark and colleagues' 2014 prospective study measured it in 70 dogs with hypoadrenocorticism, 22 with mimicking diseases, and 19 healthy dogs. Basal and stimulated aldosterone were markedly lower in affected dogs, including dogs whose sodium and potassium were not abnormal. This challenges the assumption that normal electrolytes necessarily prove preserved mineralocorticoid function. The findings belong to the assay and protocol studied. They do not turn one low aldosterone concentration into a universally definitive diagnosis or establish the mechanism maintaining an individual dog's electrolyte balance. Hormone production and the final electrolyte concentrations are related but different observations; compensating processes and clinical circumstances matter. When further classification affects management, the veterinarian may combine the hormonal profile, endogenous ACTH, follow-up electrolytes, and other findings rather than relying on the older label atypical alone. [9]

Current Project ALIVE terminology explicitly notes limitations in reliably measuring and interpreting aldosterone, and classifies clinical presentations by sodium and potassium status. That caution should accompany the older primary studies. A laboratory offering a measurement and a measurement being decisive for every clinical distinction are separate claims. [6]

## Suppression patterns are evidence rather than a rigid code

A 2021 university hospital retrospective study revisited low dose dexamethasone response patterns, including lack of suppression, partial suppression, escape, and an inverse pattern. Escape means an early fall in cortisol followed by a later rise; inverse describes the opposite time pattern relative to the study's cut point. Lack of suppression strongly supported hyperadrenocorticism in that selected population, whereas escape did not show the diagnostic association traditionally assumed. Dogs with acute nonadrenal illness and several interfering treatments were excluded. Classification also used follow-up, treatment response, and available imaging or postmortem findings. These choices are essential when interpreting the reported likelihood ratios, which express how a result changes disease odds. The report argues for combining patterns with clinical probability and further tests, not for treating any named curve as an infallible subtype label. A failure to suppress does not by itself identify an adrenal tumor rather than pituitary disease. [12]

## The setting of urine collection changes cortisol output

The urinary cortisol to creatinine ratio relates urinary cortisol output to creatinine to reduce the effect of urine dilution. It samples a longer period than a momentary blood cortisol, but does not remove stress effects. Citron and colleagues prospectively compared paired home and hospital voided urine specimens in 36 healthy client owned dogs in 2020. The cortisol ratio was higher in hospital. The study was designed primarily to investigate relationships between stress and urinary protein, and it did not establish diagnostic accuracy for Cushing's syndrome in sick dogs. Its contribution here is direct evidence that collection setting changes the endocrine signal even without spontaneous hypercortisolism. The veterinarian can arrange home collection under the laboratory's instructions, but an increased ratio still needs contextual interpretation and appropriate confirmation. A result that reflects stress is a real physiological measurement; it becomes misleading when stress related output is mistaken for autonomous disease. [11]

## Locating the source follows evidence of disease

Once excess cortisol is established, endogenous ACTH and adrenal imaging can help distinguish pituitary driven disease from autonomous adrenal disease. A 2001 prospective study of 29 dogs combined ACTH measurement and ultrasonography; the two indicated the same cause in 27. The study's small groups and method specific results should remain visible rather than being turned into universal accuracy guarantees. An adrenal mass is a structural observation, while cortisol secretion is a functional one. The interpretation asks whether hormone production fits the imaging and the pituitary signal. Disagreement can justify specialist review or additional imaging instead of selecting the more dramatic finding. Localization matters because surgical options, medical management, and prognosis differ. It should not be confused with the first question of whether the dog has clinically relevant cortisol excess, and neither a pituitary nor an adrenal image supplies a complete hormonal assessment by itself. [15]

## A published Addison case explains a normal electrolyte profile

Hatoya and colleagues reported one eight year old intact male Pointer in the 2023 issue of the Journal of Veterinary Medical Science, published online in 2022, DOI 10.1292/jvms.22-0322. Lethargy and hypoalbuminemia prompted investigation. Ultrasound showed thin adrenal glands, and serum cortisol remained very low both before and after ACTH stimulation despite no sodium or potassium abnormalities. The dog was classified as having atypical hypoadrenocorticism and received glucocorticoid replacement with prednisolone. Clinical condition improved and blood abnormalities normalized. During 818 days of observation, electrolytes remained without the expected classic disturbance. The dog then died and underwent postmortem examination: inner cortisol producing cortical layers were disrupted and atrophic, while the outer zona glomerulosa was relatively preserved. This actual tissue evidence supports selective cortical involvement in that animal. It does not show that every dog with normal electrolytes has the same pathology, quantify aldosterone secretion throughout life, or prove that its eventual death was caused by an adrenal crisis. Long-term treatment also limits inference about completely untreated natural history. [13]

## A published cortisol excess case tests an unusual mechanism

Galac and colleagues reported one six year old Vizsla with food dependent hypercortisolism, published in 2008 after online publication in 2007, DOI 10.1016/j.tvjl.2007.04.008. History, physical findings, biochemical changes, and increased urinary corticoid ratios prompted further testing. Repeated endogenous ACTH was suppressed; ultrasound and computed tomography showed uniformly enlarged adrenal glands without an identified tumor, and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging was not enlarged. Urine ratios and blood cortisol rose after meals while ACTH remained low. A supervised pharmacological challenge with the somatostatin analogue octreotide prevented the meal related cortisol rise. The cortisol synthesis inhibitor trilostane improved clinical signs, and the dog remained in good condition at twenty six months. The authors used the sequence to support an unusual adrenal response rather than ordinary pituitary disease. The abstract does not report direct receptor expression testing, so the proposed abnormal receptor mechanism remains an inference. This single case does not establish frequency, a universal meal challenge protocol, or a reason for owners to manipulate feeding or medication before routine testing. [14]

## A protocol that monitors treatment may not diagnose disease

Aldridge and colleagues' 2016 prospective study compared two synthetic ACTH doses in healthy dogs, 39 dogs being investigated for hyperadrenocorticism, and dogs receiving mitotane or trilostane. The responses were equivalent for monitoring the treated groups but were not equivalent in dogs being screened: clinical interpretation differed between doses in nearly a quarter of the suspected cases. The report provides a concrete reason to keep diagnostic and monitoring protocols separate. A procedure appropriate for one purpose is not automatically validated for another, even within the same species. It also explains why this document does not supply a general injection recipe or a universal stimulated cortisol threshold. Formulation, amount, route, sample timing, assay, and intended question form one protocol. Altering one element and retaining another protocol's interpretation can produce a false negative or a misleading comparison, so the laboratory needs to know what was actually done. [16]

## Monitoring records connect a number to a treated dog

Michigan State's adrenal testing questions request the treatment amount, administration frequency, interval from trilostane to sampling, and whether the monitoring visit occurs early in treatment. These details help the laboratory interpret an ACTH stimulation result in context. A cortisol concentration after treatment is not interchangeable with an untreated diagnostic value. The clinician also asks about thirst, urination, appetite, activity, and new illness; timing without clinical observations remains incomplete. A reproducible record is particularly useful when comparing visits or discussing an unexpected result with a specialist. Owners can contribute an accurate medication timeline and description of changes, while the veterinary team determines the testing plan. A single treated result should not independently trigger an owner administered dose change. If a dog is unwell, the investigation must address that condition rather than simply forcing its number into a monitoring target. [4]

## Urgency follows a possible crisis

Adrenal crisis can cause severe weakness, vomiting, reduced circulating volume, low blood pressure, and shock. Collapse or profound weakness with persistent vomiting and deterioration needs emergency veterinary assessment now. The dog should not wait for a routine endocrine appointment. An isolated screening result does not carry that same urgency. [6]

# H004: Resting electrocardiogram

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Cardiovascular function
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft. No independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: ECG, EKG, Resting ECG, Electrocardiogram

## Plain-Language Overview

An ECG turns electrical signals from the heart into a tracing that can be reviewed for rate and rhythm problems. It records activity during the test; symptoms occurring at another time may need a longer recording.

## Clinical Purpose

Records the heart's electrical activity to assess rate, rhythm, and signal timing. It supports investigation and monitoring of suspected or established heart conditions.

## How the Test Works

The sinoatrial node, the heart's usual pacemaker, initiates an electrical signal that spreads through the upper chambers. The atrioventricular node briefly delays conduction before activation reaches the lower chambers. Surface electrodes detect the resulting electrical changes. Different leads provide different views of this activity, so electrode position affects the recorded tracing.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

Adhesive electrodes are placed on the chest and limbs. The person lies still while a machine records the signals for a short period.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Usually no special preparation is needed. Report medicines and supplements; stop them only if the care team instructs this.

## Interpreting Results

The recording shows electrical waves and intervals. A clinician considers the pattern alongside symptoms, history, and other findings.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Assess recording quality before attributing waveform changes to disease. Electrode swaps, inaccurate chest electrode positions, and nonstandard torso placement can alter waveforms and sometimes mimic important abnormalities. Apparent changes between serial ECGs may reflect differences in recording technique. Comparison with a correctly recorded prior or repeat tracing can help resolve suspected placement errors.

## Limitations & Confounders

A resting ECG captures a brief snapshot and can miss intermittent abnormalities. Longer ambulatory monitoring may be needed for symptoms that come and go.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Electrode removal can cause mild discomfort or skin irritation. The recording device does not deliver electricity into the body.

## Species & Population Notes

This human entry does not supply animal lead placement, expected patterns, or reference intervals.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Keep resting ECG, exercise testing, Holter monitoring, and event monitoring as linked but distinct entries. An ECG finding alone does not establish every cardiac diagnosis. Preserve the tracing with its recording settings and any modified electrode placement. Treat automated interpretations as part of the recording to be reviewed in clinical context, particularly when electrode misplacement is suspected.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

Echocardiography uses ultrasound to assess cardiac structure, pumping, and blood flow. Ambulatory ECG monitoring samples electrical activity for longer periods. Blood tests such as troponin investigate myocardial injury, meaning damage to heart muscle; selection depends on the clinical presentation.

# H005: Chest radiograph

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Diagnostic imaging
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft. No independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: Chest X-ray, CXR, Chest radiography

## Plain-Language Overview

A chest X-ray provides a quick picture of structures in the chest. It can answer some clinical questions, while a normal image does not exclude every possible cause of symptoms.

## Clinical Purpose

Uses X-rays to image the chest, including lungs, heart, and chest wall. It helps investigate symptoms or monitor conditions such as pneumonia and heart failure.

## How the Test Works

An X-ray beam passes through the body. Tissues absorb or scatter different amounts of radiation, and the remaining pattern reaches a detector. This produces a projection image, with dense structures and air-containing areas appearing differently. Conventional radiography records static images; it is a different technique from CT, which collects detailed cross-sectional information.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

A technologist positions the person standing, sitting, or lying down. The person holds still and may briefly hold their breath during image acquisition.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Usually little preparation is needed. Remove items that interfere with images and tell the team if pregnancy is possible.

## Interpreting Results

A radiologist reviews the images and produces a report. The referring clinician relates the findings to the reason for testing and other clinical information.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Image adequacy and the clinical question matter. Stillness and an appropriate breath hold reduce blurring. The report may describe lung findings, heart appearance, fluid or air around the lungs, or the position of a medical device. These observations must be interpreted with symptoms, prior imaging, and other tests rather than equated automatically with a specific diagnosis.

## Limitations & Confounders

A chest radiograph cannot exclude every chest disorder. Small cancers may be missed, and pulmonary emboli are not directly seen on a conventional chest X-ray.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

The examination involves a small dose of ionizing radiation. Positioning may be uncomfortable for someone with an injury or limited movement.

## Species & Population Notes

Animal positioning and interpretation require veterinary guidance; this entry describes human imaging.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Record the views obtained and comparison studies. Follow-up or a different imaging test may be needed when the clinical question remains unresolved. Justify the examination against the clinical question and optimize exposure for diagnostic image quality. Document pregnancy considerations and relevant prior imaging. The FDA distinguishes justification, deciding whether the examination is warranted, from optimization, obtaining adequate information with appropriate radiation exposure.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

Chest CT can provide additional detail when the question remains unresolved. Depending on the suspected problem, lung function tests assess physiology and echocardiography assesses cardiac structure and pumping. These tests provide complementary information and are not interchangeable with the radiograph.

# H006: Spirometry

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Respiratory function
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft. No independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: Spirometry test, Forced expiratory spirometry

## Plain-Language Overview

Spirometry measures a strong outward breath to show how much air comes out and how quickly. The operator coaches the person through repeated efforts because the quality of the breathing maneuver affects the result.

## Clinical Purpose

Measures how much air a person exhales and how quickly it leaves the lungs. It contributes to assessment and monitoring of breathing disorders.

## How the Test Works

The device measures expired volume or airflow over time. Forced vital capacity, or FVC, is the amount exhaled during a full forced expiration; FEV1 is the amount exhaled in its first second. Their ratio relates early airflow to total expired volume. Flow-volume and volume-time curves let the operator inspect the maneuver as well as its numerical outputs.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

The person seals their lips around a mouthpiece, inhales deeply, and blows out forcefully as directed. Testing may be repeated after a bronchodilator, an inhaled medicine that opens the airways.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Wear comfortable clothing and follow the testing service's instructions about meals, activity, smoking, and inhalers. Do not withhold medicines without instructions.

## Interpreting Results

Measured airflow and expired volumes are compared with suitable reference values and considered with the person's symptoms and history.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Review acceptability and repeatability before interpreting physiology. Cough, leaks, an inadequate initial inhalation, or early termination can distort measurements. A low FEV1/FVC ratio supports airflow obstruction when judged against appropriate reference values. A low FVC with a preserved ratio can suggest restriction but does not confirm it; lung volume measurement may be needed to determine total lung capacity.

## Limitations & Confounders

Performance requires cooperation and adequate effort. Very young children may have difficulty. Spirometry alone does not identify every lung disease or replace other lung function tests.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Forceful breathing may cause temporary coughing, tiredness, dizziness, or lightheadedness.

## Species & Population Notes

Human voluntary spirometry does not describe veterinary respiratory testing or validate animal measurement methods.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Record whether testing was before or after bronchodilator and which medicines were withheld. Keep lung volumes and gas diffusion testing as separate linked procedures. Retain maneuver curves, quality grades, reference equations, and pre- and post-bronchodilator status. Document equipment quality assurance and operator assessment; repeatable numbers alone do not remove the need to inspect curve quality.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

Lung volume testing assesses total lung capacity, including air not measured by a simple forced exhalation. Diffusion testing evaluates gas transfer. Bronchial challenge testing addresses airway responsiveness and is a separate procedure requiring its own entry and precautions.

# H011: Coagulation testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Hematology and hemostasis
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 1)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational treatise. Independent clinical review pending.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Why can blood take longer to clot in a laboratory tube without implying that a person will bleed, while a reassuring clotting time can leave an important bleeding disorder unexplained?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency when severe bleeding is occurring

Severe bleeding requires emergency care now. Routine evaluation of an isolated abnormal result has a different urgency.

## The question starts before the tube

Coagulation testing investigates part of hemostasis, the body's system for limiting blood loss. The first measurement is a history: spontaneous bruising, bleeding after dental work, heavy menstrual bleeding, previous surgery, medicines, and affected relatives give a laboratory result its meaning. A complete blood count adds the number of platelets, the cell fragments involved in clot formation. Clotting times investigate different groups of soluble clotting proteins. These are complementary observations, not interchangeable tests. Testing may be ordered for unexplained bleeding, a known disorder, selected procedures, or treatment monitoring. A person undergoing an orderly investigation of bruising is in a different clinical situation from someone actively losing a large amount of blood. [1]

## The specimen can manufacture a disorder

The sample must preserve the clotting system without allowing it to finish clotting before the experiment begins. Collection into the correct citrate tube and appropriate filling are therefore analytical requirements. An underfilled tube can produce an artificially prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time, or aPTT. Blood drawn through a vascular access line can carry heparin, an anticoagulant, into the sample even when the line has been flushed. The resulting delay may describe contamination rather than the patient's physiology. An unexpected result should prompt questions about collection, reproducibility, and medicines before a complex explanation is adopted. This is why repeating a properly collected specimen can be an investigative step rather than an admission that the laboratory has learned nothing. [2]

## Starting a clot through tissue factor

The prothrombin time, or PT, times fibrin formation after a deliberately chosen trigger. In Mayo's documented optical method, tissue factor, phospholipid, and calcium are added to citrated plasma. This initiates reactions involving factor VII and the common pathway: activated factor X helps generate thrombin, and thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into fibrin. Fibrin formation supplies the endpoint. The result is a controlled experiment with a particular reagent, not a stopwatch placed beside a wound. Reduced activity of a relevant factor or an inhibitor can prolong the experiment. The assay deliberately bypasses several other components, including normal platelet participation, so its design also explains what it cannot evaluate. [3]

## Starting the experiment another way

The aPTT uses a different entrance to the same fibrin endpoint. Mayo describes incubating plasma with phospholipid and a contact activator, then adding calcium and measuring clot formation. Its optical detector follows the changing passage of light as fibrin develops. The assay screens much of the intrinsic and common coagulation pathways, laboratory names for linked groups of clotting reactions. It does not evaluate factor VII through this entrance, and it does not measure factor XIII, which stabilizes fibrin after the initial clot forms. Consequently, PT and aPTT answer related but different questions. Knowing which is prolonged can be more informative than calling the entire coagulation screen abnormal. [4]

## What the international normalized ratio standardizes

The international normalized ratio, or INR, expresses the PT in a form used to monitor vitamin K antagonist treatment such as warfarin. Its therapeutic interpretation depends on the reason anticoagulation was prescribed and the patient's clinical circumstances. A treatment target is a decision boundary chosen for a particular purpose; it is not the same as the reference interval describing people not receiving that treatment. The University of Iowa's laboratory guidance makes this distinction explicit by relating target INR to indication and bleeding risk. An INR should therefore travel with the medicine and clinical question. It should not be relabeled a universal measure of how thin the blood is. [9]

## Restoring a missing ingredient or revealing interference

A mixing study asks what happens when patient plasma is combined with normal pooled plasma. Improvement often points toward a deficient factor supplied by the normal plasma; failure to correct often suggests an inhibitor that remains active in the mixture. Those words are clues, not final diagnoses. The 2024 International Council for Standardization in Haematology recommendations emphasize local validation, reagent differences, medication history, and sometimes incubation to reveal time-dependent inhibition. Weak inhibitors can be missed. Anticoagulant drugs can resemble inhibitors. Factor replacement or bypassing treatment can mask an abnormality. A mixing result is therefore an experiment within an investigation: the ratio, timing, reagent, and definition of correction must be known before its apparent answer is trusted. [5]

## Measuring the material of the clot

Fibrinogen testing adds another dimension. The Clauss activity method exposes plasma to excess thrombin and relates the rate of fibrin formation to the amount of functional fibrinogen. University of Washington identifies its method as a Stago Clauss clotting assay. This is an activity measurement, which asks how the protein behaves in that experiment. Fibrinogen may be considered during investigation of disseminated intravascular coagulation, a systemic disturbance involving clotting and consumption, and in liver disease, inflammatory illness, or fibrinolytic treatment. The concentration is not a stand-alone label for any of those conditions. It belongs beside the clinical course and other measurements rather than replacing them. [6]

## Read a pattern rather than a verdict

ARUP's evaluation approach starts with the clinical presentation, which clotting times are prolonged, and relevant treatment. Further tests may include fibrinogen, D-dimer, individual factor assays, inhibitor testing, or von Willebrand factor studies. A prolonged clotting time can occur in someone presenting with bleeding, thrombosis, or neither. Conversely, normal PT and aPTT do not exclude all disorders of platelets, von Willebrand factor, or factor XIII. Recent transfusion and factor replacement can also change the pattern. The useful conclusion is narrower than blood normal or blood abnormal: this specimen, tested through these pathways, supports particular possibilities and leaves others open. The next test should resolve a remaining question rather than merely enlarge the panel. [7]

## When waiting is the wrong experiment

Severe bleeding is an emergency. Seek emergency care now and call the local emergency number; in the United States call 911. Do not wait for a coagulation result to decide whether major ongoing blood loss matters. NHS 24 lists severe bleeding among life-threatening emergencies. This urgency concerns the person's condition. It does not turn every mildly prolonged outpatient clotting time into an emergency or justify changing an anticoagulant without the treating team's assessment. [8]

## Questions that make the result useful

Which clinical event prompted testing? Was the sample suitable? Which medicine was present at collection? Is the abnormality reproducible? Does the pattern suggest a factor problem, an inhibitor, or a test that needs a different companion? These questions connect the laboratory experiment to a person's bleeding history. [1, 2, 7]

# H013: Inflammatory marker testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Clinical chemistry and hematology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 5)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does an inflammation signal tell us about illness, and when can a reassuring result conceal a serious problem?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Symptoms and clinical state determine urgency

Serious deterioration or new visual symptoms need urgent clinical assessment.

## What the tests are trying to detect

Inflammation is the body's response to tissue injury, infection and many other disturbances. It can help contain a problem and support healing, but an excessive or persistent response can also damage tissue. C reactive protein, abbreviated CRP, is made by the liver in response to inflammatory signals. Measuring it can help investigate symptoms or follow an established illness. An increased result does not identify the site or the cause. Infection, autoimmune disease and tissue injury can all produce inflammation, and the patient still needs an explanation of the underlying process. This chapter concentrates on human CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate and procalcitonin. They observe different parts of the response, rather than measure three interchangeable versions of the same substance. Their value depends on which clinical question is being asked and what other evidence is available. [1]

## How the laboratory measures CRP

Mayo Clinic Laboratories describes its routine serum CRP method as immunoturbidimetric. Antibodies recognize CRP, producing a reaction that changes how light passes through the sample; the instrument relates that optical signal to a calibrated concentration. This is a measurement of a protein, not a direct image of inflamed tissue or a count of bacteria. Results must retain their units. In a hypothetical comparison, 10 mg/L and 1 mg/dL describe the same concentration, because a liter contains ten deciliters. Reading those as a tenfold improvement would be a unit error. The example is arithmetic, not a patient or a decision threshold. Reference intervals and the laboratory's method help interpret the report, while the clinician supplies the context. A decimal place on the screen does not eliminate uncertainty about what caused the protein to rise. [2]

## ESR observes the behavior of red cells

The erythrocyte sedimentation rate, or ESR, observes how far red blood cells settle in a standardized blood sample over a defined period. Erythrocyte means red blood cell; sedimentation means settling. Inflammation can change blood proteins in ways that encourage cells to collect together and settle more quickly. The result is commonly expressed in millimeters per hour. It is influenced by more than inflammation, including age, pregnancy, anemia and other blood characteristics. Anemia means a reduced hemoglobin concentration for the relevant patient group. A high ESR therefore cannot be translated directly into a certain amount of infection. It can support the investigation or monitoring of selected conditions, but examination, symptoms and other tests are necessary to interpret it. Equally, an ESR within the reference interval cannot exclude every inflammatory illness. [3]

## The ESR method belongs with the number

The International Council for Standardization in Haematology developed a reference approach based on the Westergren method, specifying the blood preparation and characteristics of the measuring tube. Newer automated methods need comparison with an appropriate reference rather than merely adopting the familiar name. This matters when results from different laboratories or devices are compared over time. Changing the physical system can change the measurement, especially at higher values. A report should therefore be interpreted within the laboratory's validated procedure and reference information. Standardization is the work that makes a result reproducible enough to be useful; it does not make the test specific for one disease. For a patient following a long illness, retaining the laboratory and method alongside the date is more informative than keeping only a list of numbers copied from different reports. [4]

## Procalcitonin has a different biological signal

Procalcitonin, abbreviated PCT, is another blood marker used mainly in seriously ill patients when bacterial infection is a concern. Mayo's listed assay uses electrochemiluminescence, an antibody based measurement with a light producing detection system. The laboratory explicitly cautions that raised concentrations also occur with severe trauma, shock, recent surgery, burns, renal impairment and some other conditions. Conversely, a low result does not exclude all infections or every clinical presentation of bacterial disease. The timing of collection and the patient's broader condition remain important. PCT can change the probability assigned to a diagnosis, but it cannot identify an organism or select a suitable antibiotic by itself. The method's role is to add evidence to a clinical assessment, not provide an independent verdict that the patient does or does not have sepsis. [5]

## What the patient experiences

A procalcitonin measurement generally requires a small blood sample from a vein. The person may notice a brief sting or a small bruise, while the principal clinical burden comes from the illness prompting the test rather than from the measurement itself. The ordering team should explain whether a single result or a sequence is intended. Testing may help assess a seriously ill patient or monitor progress during treatment, and preparation instructions should come from the service performing the test. The result should be connected to the collection time, symptoms and any treatment already given. Keeping these details prevents a later reader from assuming that a measurement taken after resuscitation or antibiotics necessarily describes the patient's state before those interventions. A timeline is part of the evidence, even though it does not appear as another number on the laboratory report. [6]

## Disagreement between markers can be meaningful

Costenbader and colleagues examined 2,069 patients with ESR and CRP measured on the same day. They defined substantial disagreement by one result falling in the highest third of values and the other in the lowest third, finding 87 such cases. Kidney impairment, infection and low albumin were associated with particular patterns of disagreement. These findings concern the study's strict definition, rather than the frequency of every mildly mismatched pair in general practice. They show why one marker should not simply be used to overrule the other. Different biology, the timing of the response and other patient characteristics may be involved. The useful next question is whether the pattern fits the illness being investigated. Repeating both tests without a defined question can generate more data while leaving the underlying uncertainty unchanged. [7]

## Starting treatment and stopping treatment are different decisions

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign's 2026 adult guideline suggests clinical evaluation alone over adding procalcitonin when deciding whether to start antimicrobials for possible or probable sepsis or septic shock. A low marker should not delay treatment that the clinical assessment indicates is needed. The same guideline suggests that PCT combined with clinical assessment may help decide when to stop antibiotics once the source is adequately controlled and the best duration remains uncertain. Source control means addressing the underlying focus, such as draining an infected collection when appropriate. These recommendations concern different decisions at different points in an illness. Neither says that an individual should start, stop or change prescribed treatment by reading a laboratory result at home. They illustrate why a useful biomarker needs a defined population, purpose and place within a treatment pathway. [8]

## A large trial tested whether marker guidance changed care

The ADAPT Sepsis trial assigned 2,760 critically ill adults with suspected sepsis to daily PCT guided advice, daily CRP guided advice or standard care. PCT guidance reduced average antibiotic exposure from 10.7 to 9.8 days, with an adjusted difference of 0.88 days. It met the trial's predefined noninferiority criterion for death by 28 days. Noninferiority means that a confidence interval excluded the prespecified unacceptable increase in risk; it does not prove identical mortality or absence of all harm. CRP guidance did not demonstrate the same reduction in antibiotic duration, and its mortality comparison was inconclusive. Thus the two markers were not interchangeable treatment tools. The results apply to structured protocols in critical care, where clinicians could consider the whole patient. They are not a rule that every falling inflammatory marker permits antibiotics to be stopped. [9]

## A primary care trial asked a different question

Cals and colleagues studied 431 patients with lower respiratory tract infection across 20 Dutch general practices. Clinicians using a point of care CRP test prescribed antibiotics at the first consultation to 31 percent of patients, compared with 53 percent without the test. Training in communication also reduced prescribing, and recovery and satisfaction were similar across study groups. The study demonstrates a practical outcome: a test used with assessment and explanation can change prescribing without an observed loss of recovery in that population. It does not show that CRP alone identifies every bacterial pneumonia, nor was it a study of unstable patients in intensive care. The communication result also matters. An explanation of expected recovery and reasons for reassessment can be part of good diagnostic care rather than an optional addition after a number has been obtained. [10]

## Normal markers can occur in a disease threatening vision

Giant cell arteritis is inflammation of large arteries that can interrupt blood supply to the eye. Kermani and colleagues studied 764 people who underwent temporal artery biopsy; 177 had a biopsy supporting the diagnosis. A small proportion of biopsy positive patients had both ESR and CRP within the normal range without prior glucocorticoid treatment. This is a concrete reason to avoid using normal markers as an absolute exclusion rule. It is also important to keep the denominator clear: the study concerned people already selected for biopsy, not an unselected population with headaches. A normal result changes probability within an assessment; it does not erase sufficiently concerning symptoms. The clinician may need imaging, biopsy or urgent treatment decisions based on the whole presentation, while still considering alternative explanations for the symptoms. [11]

## A published case ended with persistent visual disability

A 2026 report described a 75 year old man with sequential eye involvement despite ESR and CRP remaining within their reference ranges. Vascular imaging did not show a critical atherosclerotic narrowing, while PET imaging showed increased activity along the vertebral arteries that supported the clinical diagnosis of giant cell arteritis. He received corticosteroid treatment with specialist involvement. At follow up he described complete loss of sight in one eye and very restricted remaining vision in the other, with no further worsening after treatment began. That was stabilization with major disability, not restoration of sight. The diagnosis was supported by clinical and imaging assessment; this account does not relabel it as biopsy proven. A single report cannot establish how often this presentation occurs or prove what an earlier intervention would have achieved. It does show why a reassuring blood result must not close an investigation when vision is changing. [12]

## Treatment can suppress the expected signal

Bari and colleagues reported a 65 year old man with rheumatoid arthritis receiving several medicines, including tocilizumab, which blocks a key inflammatory signaling pathway. His painful swollen knee eventually prevented weight bearing, but he was otherwise systemically well and CRP was normal. Fluid from the joint and blood cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus. He completed six weeks of antibiotics with complete symptom resolution, while CRP stayed normal throughout. The authors considered prior tocilizumab treatment the likely explanation for suppression of the expected response. This is an observed case, not a frequency estimate or a reason to change the medicine independently. It demonstrates why a medication history belongs beside the marker result. A low CRP may reflect an altered ability to produce that signal rather than evidence that no serious infection exists. [13]

## A very high procalcitonin did not settle the cause of shock

Altermanini and colleagues described a 23 year old man undergoing investigation of severe anemia. During colonoscopy, worms were seen and he developed fever, a rapid heartbeat and very low blood pressure. PCT reached 95.2 ng/mL, initially raising concern for sepsis. The authors ultimately favored anaphylactic shock because of the abrupt clinical sequence and rapid improvement after emergency allergy treatment; blood cultures were negative. They proposed that disturbance of the worms was the trigger, but did not obtain tryptase testing, so that mechanism remained an inference. He was discharged five days later, and follow up showed normalization of inflammatory markers and subsequent blood counts. This case does not prove that infection was impossible. It demonstrates that a striking PCT concentration can occur in a different life threatening inflammatory state, and that the diagnosis depends on the full clinical sequence rather than the biomarker alone. [14]

## High sensitivity CRP answers another clinical question

A high sensitivity CRP assay measures the same protein with performance suited to lower concentrations. The adjective does not mean that the patient has a more sensitive immune system or that the result is a general test for every hidden illness. Mayo distinguishes its high sensitivity assay, used in cardiovascular risk assessment, from routine CRP requested for other inflammatory disorders. This is an example of purpose shaping interpretation. A concentration used in a discussion of future vascular risk should not be read through a sepsis decision rule, and an acute inflammatory illness can make that risk assessment difficult to interpret. The reader should preserve the exact test name and the reason it was ordered. A result becomes useful when its analytical range and intended clinical task agree with the question being asked. [15]

## A forensic case revealed inflammation after death

Hashida and colleagues reported a 74 year old man evaluated for lower back pain and found dead three days after treatment for presumed lumbar disc disease. Autopsy found an unusually positioned perforated appendix, a large abscess behind the abdominal lining and associated soft tissue infection. Microbiological testing of pus identified several bacteria, and CRP in cardiac blood was 43.5 mg/dL. This was a postmortem measurement, not a documented missed living blood result. The authors attributed death to sepsis arising from the infection. Their conclusion depended on the combined anatomical, microscopic, microbiological and biochemical findings. The case illustrates how an inflammatory marker can support a forensic explanation without locating the source by itself. It does not establish that every backache requires CRP, that the same value existed at the earlier consultation, or that a particular test would certainly have prevented death. [16]

## Postmortem marker research needs its own evidence

Palmiere and colleagues investigated PCT, CRP and another inflammatory marker in a forensic series including sixteen sepsis related fatalities and control individuals. Their work examined whether biochemical measurements could contribute to postmortem diagnosis. This is a different setting from deciding how to treat a living patient. Death, specimen source, time before sampling and the circumstances of the investigation must be considered, and a study in selected autopsy cases cannot establish a universal cutoff for every body or laboratory. The relevant forensic conclusion connects biochemical findings with the clinical history, examination of organs, tissue microscopy and microbiology. An elevated marker alone cannot identify the responsible organism, determine whether care was negligent or establish the precise time at which an infection became irreversible. Those are further questions requiring their own evidence. [17]

## An acutely unwell person needs assessment before a marker returns

NICE's current adult suspected sepsis guidance bases assessment on the history, examination, physiological observations and risk factors. People may be seriously ill without a high temperature, and a change noticed by the patient or family can be important. The process is not reduced to whether CRP or PCT crosses a single threshold. Severe deterioration, confusion, difficulty breathing or other alarming changes require urgent medical assessment, rather than waiting at home for a repeat inflammatory panel. This distinction keeps the time scales clear: recognition of possible organ dysfunction, collection of appropriate tests and interpretation of a later trend are separate tasks. Once the immediate problem is addressed, a series of measurements may help monitor progress. A favorable laboratory trend should be understood alongside the person's actual recovery and any evidence that the source of illness remains uncontrolled. [18]

## New visual symptoms have a separate urgent pathway

NHS guidance on temporal arteritis explains why suspected disease is treated urgently: loss of blood supply can cause permanent blindness or stroke. New visual symptoms, especially with a new headache, scalp tenderness or pain while chewing, need prompt clinical assessment. The clinician may arrange a same day eye assessment when vision is affected and begin treatment before all confirmatory results are available. This is a clinical response to the possible consequence of the disease, not a universal emergency category assigned to every mildly high ESR or CRP. For routine monitoring, retain the test name, units, date, symptoms and medicines, and ask what change would alter the treatment plan. For an important new symptom, report the symptom rather than relying on the reassurance of an old or newly normal marker. [19]

# H014: Arterial blood gas analysis

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Respiratory physiology and acid base testing
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 1)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational treatise. Independent clinical review pending.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can an oxygen reading look reassuring while breathing is failing to remove carbon dioxide, and why does a small bubble in a blood sample have the power to change the story?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency when acute respiratory failure is suspected

Sudden breathing difficulty with confusion or blue skin or lips requires emergency assessment now rather than waiting for a test.

## Three connected questions in one small sample

Arterial blood gas analysis, or ABG, investigates oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base balance. Oxygenation concerns oxygen entering arterial blood; ventilation concerns effective movement of gas that removes carbon dioxide; acid-base balance describes the chemical environment in which cells function. The AARC guideline identifies arterial oxygen tension, carbon dioxide tension, and pH as central measured variables, with other reported quantities sometimes calculated. Clinicians may use the test to investigate illness, follow cardiopulmonary disease, or assess a response to treatment. It is a selected physiological measurement, not a routine requirement for everyone who coughs. An arterial sample and a venous sample also retain different meanings, especially when the question concerns arterial oxygenation. [1]

## Pressure is not the whole oxygen supply

The letter P in PaO2 and PaCO2 means partial pressure: the pressure associated with a gas dissolved in blood. The a identifies arterial blood. Most oxygen in blood is attached to hemoglobin inside red cells; PaO2 reflects the much smaller dissolved portion. Oxygen saturation instead describes hemoglobin's oxygen binding. These concepts are connected but do not describe the same physical quantity. Radiometer's parameter guidance also identifies carbon dioxide pressure as a respiratory component of acid-base balance, influenced by ventilation. A useful report is therefore read as several measurements of a transport system. One oxygen number does not disclose every aspect of how much oxygen the blood can carry or how effectively carbon dioxide is leaving. [2]

## Preserving a moment of physiology

Collection and analysis are performed by trained staff using a suitable heparinized whole-blood device. The sample must remain representative of the circulation rather than exchanging gases with the room. An air bubble permits equilibration with ambient air and can bias oxygen, carbon dioxide, pH, and saturation. Meanwhile the cells are still alive: they continue consuming oxygen and glucose and producing carbon dioxide and lactate after collection. Delay therefore changes the specimen being measured. Clots can obstruct the analyzer or disturb its sensors. Radiometer's guidance recommends dry electrolyte-balanced heparin and emphasizes prompt analysis, sealing, and appropriate mixing. The practical lesson is preservation, not a universal transport countdown divorced from the syringe, analyzer, and local validation. [3]

## A result needs its surroundings

The arterial label, collection time, and measurement conditions should remain attached to the values. University of Iowa's arterial blood gas service specifies whole blood in an appropriate heparin syringe, immediate delivery, and absence of air bubbles. It also states that analyses and calculations assume a standard temperature unless the service is informed otherwise. These are examples of a laboratory's requirements, not instructions to perform an arterial puncture yourself. The sampling procedure and sample handling are separate sources of risk and error from the underlying illness. Retain the reporting laboratory's units, reference interval, and any temperature correction rather than importing numbers from a different sampling context. [4]

## How the analyzer turns blood into signals

The Siemens RAPIDPoint 500e provides one documented implementation. Its pH sensor uses potentiometry, measuring an electrical potential difference; its carbon dioxide sensor uses a related approach based on the Severinghaus principle. Its oxygen sensor uses amperometry, measuring an electrical current associated with the analyte. The system then derives quantities such as bicarbonate from measured values and established equations. Its co-oximetry module uses light absorption at multiple wavelengths to distinguish hemoglobin-related components. These are different analytical operations even when they appear on one printout. A calculated bicarbonate is consequently not an independent second measurement confirming pH and carbon dioxide, and a calculated saturation should not silently be treated as a direct multiwavelength measurement. [8]

## Read the relationships rather than memorizing a label

Interpretation begins with pH, then asks how carbon dioxide and bicarbonate contribute. Acidemia means the blood is on the acidic side of its applicable interval; acidosis names a process tending to lower pH. The corresponding alkaline terms distinguish a measured state from a process. A respiratory disturbance primarily involves carbon dioxide; a metabolic disturbance involves the nonrespiratory component. The body may compensate, so a nearly ordinary pH can coexist with important disturbances. The American Thoracic Society's teaching approach asks whether compensation is appropriate and whether another process is present. Electrolytes and an anion gap, a calculation comparing measured positive and negative ions, can clarify metabolic causes. This pattern analysis does not by itself identify the disease responsible. [9]

## Oxygen treatment changes the question

An oxygen tension obtained while breathing room air cannot be interpreted as though it was obtained on supplemental oxygen. The oxygen delivery device and inspired concentration, when known, belong with the sample and bedside observations. The British Thoracic Society guideline emphasizes documenting oxygen alongside saturation and using blood gases when needed in breathless or acutely ill adults. It also distinguishes arterial measurements from selected arterialized capillary alternatives. A different sample type may be useful for a particular question without becoming an interchangeable substitute for every parameter. When a treatment changes, a later sample answers how the patient is functioning under new conditions, not simply whether an earlier number has improved in isolation. Arterial sampling can be painful and can rarely injure an artery or compromise hand circulation; appropriate technique and clinical assessment therefore matter. [5]

## Why the finger clip cannot settle every question

Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation without obtaining an arterial specimen. That convenience is valuable, but the FDA warns that the estimate can be affected by circulation, skin pigmentation, temperature, skin characteristics, tobacco use, and nail polish. The reading needs symptoms and clinical assessment. It is not a direct assay of arterial carbon dioxide or acid-base balance. A reassuring display should therefore not be promoted into proof that every part of breathing and gas exchange is adequate. Conversely, an unexpected low value may need verification of both the patient and the measurement. ABG and pulse oximetry are complementary tools with different physical measurements and different sources of error. [6]

## When physiology becomes an emergency

Sudden trouble breathing, confusion, or a bluish appearance of the skin or lips can accompany acute respiratory failure. Seek emergency care now; in the United States call 911. NHLBI explicitly describes acute respiratory failure as requiring emergency treatment. Do not wait for a home oxygen reading or a planned blood test to become more abnormal before seeking help. Stable monitoring of a chronic condition is a different context. The urgency is established by the clinical condition and the treating team's assessment, not by assuming that every ABG order announces impending collapse. [7]

## Questions to carry back to the report

Was this arterial blood? What oxygen support was present? Which values were measured and which calculated? Was the sample preserved correctly? Does the pattern fit one process, compensation, or several processes together? The answers make the numbers interpretable. [1, 3, 5, 9]

# H015: Cardiac troponin testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Cardiovascular biomarkers
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 1)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational treatise. Independent clinical review pending. The official 2026 definition slide set was directly consulted; inaccessible full journal text was not represented as read.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When a blood test detects injury to heart muscle, how does the clinical team decide whether it represents a heart attack, another cardiac process, or a misleading analytical signal?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency when heart attack is suspected

Suspected heart attack requires emergency care now. Do not wait at home for a troponin result.

## A protein signal with more than one explanation

Troponin is part of the machinery regulating muscle contraction. Cardiac troponin I and T provide blood markers used to investigate injury involving heart muscle. A concentration is therefore evidence to interpret, not a picture of a coronary artery. Mayo's high-sensitivity troponin I catalog notes that an elevation can accompany conditions other than myocardial infarction, including myocarditis, heart failure, kidney failure, or pulmonary embolism. Myocarditis means inflammation of heart muscle. [1]

Myocardial infarction means heart muscle cell death caused by prolonged ischemia, an insufficient blood supply to the muscle. The clinical investigation must establish the mechanism. Giving every elevation the same diagnosis loses the very distinction the test is intended to help make. [3]

## The specimen must keep its identity

The laboratory needs a validated specimen, accurate collection time, and the assay identity. Mayo's HSTNI service, for example, specifies lithium-heparin plasma and rejects gross hemolysis, meaning substantial breakdown of red cells in the specimen. Other assays have their own requirements. A serum or plasma result, an older assay, and a different manufacturer's assay should not be silently merged into one numerical sequence. The order and report must preserve what was actually measured. [1]

## How a very small concentration becomes measurable light

An immunoassay uses antibodies as molecular recognition tools. The FDA's review of the Roche Elecsys Troponin T Gen 5 assay describes a particular sandwich design: two antibodies recognize cardiac troponin T, with biotin and a ruthenium label forming part of the capture and detection system. Streptavidin-coated particles help hold the resulting complex, and electrochemiluminescence produces a measurable light signal after electrical stimulation. Calibration connects signal to concentration. This is one documented platform, not the architecture of every troponin test. Its ability to recognize tiny quantities depends on reagent design and analytical performance. It is also why a substance that interferes with antibody binding or capture can alter a result without changing the amount of injury in the heart. [2]

## Separate injury from infarction

The official 2026 Fifth Universal Definition slide set defines acute myocardial injury using a rise or fall in cardiac troponin with at least one value above the assay's sex-specific 99th percentile upper reference limit. That percentile is tied to a reference population and assay, not a universal concentration. Infarction requires a clinical mechanism involving myocardial ischemia, inadequate blood supply to the muscle. The statement distinguishes primary, secondary, and procedure-related infarction and uses clinical, electrocardiographic, and imaging evidence to refine the diagnosis. A persistent elevation also needs investigation rather than an automatic label of chronic injury. The blood result contributes evidence about injury; the clinical evaluation determines what happened, why, and how confidently the mechanism has been established. [3]

## Why the second sample changes the reasoning

A serial measurement adds time to concentration. The 2022 ACC emergency chest pain pathway combines high-sensitivity troponin with clinical assessment and an electrocardiogram, the tracing of cardiac electrical activity. Interpretation depends on when symptoms began, when samples were collected, the initial concentration, and change on the same assay. Very early sampling can precede a detectable rise. A small change near an analytical limit is not equivalent to the same numerical change at another concentration or on another platform. Validated decision pathways handle these distinctions. Their rule-out thresholds, repeat intervals, and change criteria belong to the pathway studied. A single universal delta, meaning change between samples, should not be invented and applied to every assay or every presentation. [4]

## The ECG can require action before the blood result

Acute coronary syndrome describes a group of urgent presentations associated with acute coronary disease. The 2025 US multisociety guideline emphasizes prompt clinical evaluation and rapid ECG acquisition. An initially nondiagnostic ECG does not exclude acute coronary syndrome; changes can evolve and repeat recordings may be needed. When the ECG establishes a presentation requiring emergency reperfusion, treatment to restore blood flow must not be delayed while waiting for a biomarker result. This is a crucial limit on laboratory-centered thinking. Troponin is central to assessment, but the laboratory does not own the entire timeline. The patient's condition and other diagnostic evidence can already justify urgent specialist action before the next sample is analyzed. [5]

## When an antibody makes an elevation look real

A discordant result can require analytical investigation. The IFCC's 2023 recommendations describe heterophilic antibodies, which can crosslink assay components, and macrotroponin, long-lived complexes involving troponin and antibodies. These can produce misleading elevations. A laboratory may investigate with an alternative assay, dilution studies, blocking approaches, or procedures that remove immunoglobulins, the antibody proteins. No single maneuver proves every case, and an interference can be specific to a platform. The important trigger is a result that does not fit the clinical evidence or behaves unexpectedly over time. The right response is communication between clinician and laboratory, preserving both the possibility of genuine injury and the possibility that the analytical system is being misled. [6]

## A supplement can hide the signal

False reassurance is possible as well. The FDA warns that biotin, a vitamin present in some supplements, can interfere with susceptible laboratory assays and has caused falsely low troponin results. The risk is method-dependent: some manufacturers have modified assays to mitigate it, and an old warning should not be assumed to describe every current reagent. Patients should tell the clinical team about supplements, especially products marketed for hair, skin, or nails. Laboratory staff need the current manufacturer's instructions when evaluating suspected interference. An emergency should not be postponed for a self-chosen supplement washout, and a low result that conflicts with a concerning presentation needs clinical reassessment rather than blind acceptance. [7]

## The emergency begins with symptoms

Chest pressure or discomfort, upper-body pain, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or lightheadedness can accompany a heart attack; symptoms can be mild or come and go. If a heart attack is suspected, seek emergency care now and call 911 in the United States. NHLBI advises calling even when unsure. Do not wait for an outpatient troponin test or a repeat result at home. The purpose of testing in acute care is to clarify an urgent clinical question while professionals assess and manage the whole situation. [8]

## A forensic reminder about mechanism

Gentile and colleagues reported a 14-year-old who died several days after blunt chest trauma in a moped accident. Autopsy and histology identified a contusion with myocardial rupture; the authors found no coronary abnormality and attributed the injury to trauma rather than post-traumatic infarction. The report does not provide the patient's troponin concentrations. It therefore cannot establish that troponin was negative or that a particular test would have prevented death. Its narrower lesson is about mechanism: tissue injury, coronary ischemia, and a biomarker result are different kinds of evidence. A credible explanation must connect the clinical history with cardiac findings. [9]

## Questions that keep the interpretation honest

Which assay was used? How long after symptom onset was blood collected? What changed on serial testing? What does the ECG show? Does the proposed mechanism explain the result, and has the laboratory investigated a serious mismatch between the number and the clinical evidence? [4, 6]

# H032: Computed tomography

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Imaging
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 2)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *CT can reveal an otherwise hidden problem and can also make different materials look alike. Understanding the question, acquisition, and confirmatory evidence is essential to understanding the result.*

## The clinical question comes before the scan

Computed tomography, usually called CT, produces cross sectional images that can locate disease, map an injury, guide a biopsy, or follow a response to cancer treatment. These purposes ask different questions. Finding an abnormality, establishing its cause, and showing that a testing strategy improves survival are separate achievements. A scan may contribute to each, but an image alone does not establish all three. Knowing why the examination was requested makes its eventual report much easier to understand. This chapter follows human diagnostic CT; the two published cases illustrate interpretation rather than recommended protocols for every patient. [2]

## How measurements become an image

A rotating X ray source sends beams through the body while detectors measure what emerges. The table moves through the scanner opening, and a computer combines measurements from many angles to reconstruct slices. The scanner therefore measures the passage of X rays and calculates an image; it does not take an ordinary photograph of exposed organs. Attenuation means the reduction of the beam as it passes through material. Differences in attenuation allow structures to be distinguished. The same acquired measurements can support views in several planes, helping a radiologist follow a structure through its surroundings. [1]

A useful CT examination requires both justification and optimization. Justification asks whether the expected information is worth obtaining for this person. Optimization asks how to obtain adequate information with an appropriate exposure. More radiation is not automatically better care, and reducing exposure until a scan cannot answer its question is not an improvement either. The clinical team chooses the body region and technique around the problem. This is why the phrase a CT scan does not specify one interchangeable examination. [1]

## What the person experiences

The scanner has a short central opening and a narrow moving table. A technologist operates it from a nearby workstation while maintaining visual and voice contact. Some examinations require breath holding or remaining very still. Clothing or removable metal may need to be changed or removed. Before the appointment, tell the team about possible pregnancy, recent illness, kidney or thyroid disease, medications, and previous contrast reactions. Bring the actual details of a prior reaction if available, because the substance and symptoms matter more than an imprecise allergy label. Ask for the instructions for the exact examination booked. [4]

Preparation varies with the examination and whether sedation is involved. Instructions about eating, drinking, medicines, and arrival time should come from the imaging service. A general web description cannot resolve a special protocol. The technologist should also know if pain, anxiety, limited movement, or difficulty holding a breath might interfere with positioning. Discussing these barriers before scanning gives the team a chance to adapt the examination rather than discovering the difficulty halfway through the acquisition. [4]

## What contrast changes

Contrast material temporarily changes how structures interact with the imaging beam. In CT, iodine based material may be injected into a vessel, while oral or rectal materials may help outline parts of the digestive tract. These routes serve different purposes. Contrast does not permanently stain organs and does not itself emit the scanner radiation. Its distribution changes the appearance of tissues and vessels, allowing questions about their boundaries or blood supply that may be harder to answer without it. Whether contrast is useful depends on the diagnostic question; its presence is not a general quality score. [5]

An injection can produce sensations such as warmth, but breathing difficulty, throat swelling, or a concerning reaction needs immediate attention from the imaging team. Tell staff promptly rather than waiting for the scan to finish. Severe kidney dysfunction also changes the discussion about intravenous iodine. The benefit of a needed diagnosis and the individual risk must be considered together. A prior uneventful scan does not replace the current history, especially if kidney function or health has changed since then. [5]

The ACR manual distinguishes kidney injury occurring after contrast from injury actually caused by contrast; timing alone does not prove causation. It identifies acute kidney injury and severe chronic kidney dysfunction as circumstances needing particular assessment. A previous reaction to the same class of contrast is an important part of screening. Shellfish allergy does not confer a special iodine contrast risk beyond other unrelated allergies. These distinctions help prevent a broad label from replacing a useful clinical history. Decisions about precautions, alternative imaging, or medication changes belong with the treating and imaging teams. [6]

The manual also states that routine fasting is not required solely for modern intravascular contrast administration, while sedation or anesthesia brings its own requirements. This does not cancel the imaging service instructions for a particular procedure. If instructions conflict, ask the service to clarify them before changing food intake or prescribed medicines. A clear explanation should identify the actual reason for a restriction rather than implying that every CT examination follows one preparation rule. [6]

## A CT number needs a measurement context

Hounsfield units, abbreviated HU, express attenuation relative to reference materials. They are useful measurements, but a number is not a unique chemical fingerprint. Levine and colleagues examined elemental powders at several tube voltages to study the material information available from CT. Their measurements and modeling showed both the potential of combining energy dependent information and limits on which compositions can be distinguished. For a reader, the practical lesson is to ask what acquisition and reconstruction produced the number before treating it as the identity of an unknown object. [7]

A region of interest is an area selected for measurement in an image. Its result should be understood with the material being sampled and the scan conditions. Al Hayek and colleagues tested phantoms, manufactured objects used to evaluate imaging systems, on two CT scanners. Changing vertical positioning, object size, and tube voltage changed measured CT numbers. These were controlled equipment experiments rather than a patient outcome trial. Their findings support careful positioning and measurement interpretation; they do not supply a universal correction that a reader should apply to every hospital scan. [8]

## A forensic case in which dense nodules were not pellets

Zambiasi and colleagues reported a 76 year old man found dead with advanced decomposition. Four small hard lung nodules resembled retained pellets. Focused retrospective postmortem CT showed an endobronchial arrangement, density around 3000 HU, and no metallic streak artifacts. Material analysis found calcium and phosphorus rather than expected ballistic metals; histology supported broncholithiasis, calcified material associated with airways. The nodules were incidental. No ballistic injury was identified, and the cause of death remained officially undetermined. Premortem history and treatment were unavailable. The case was located through the Forensic Medicine source collection and checked against the original report. CT guided the investigation; complementary examination established composition. Neither the high HU value nor this single case establishes a universal cutoff for identifying metal, a test accuracy estimate, or broncholithiasis as the cause of death. [13]

## A living patient in whom iodine resembled bleeding

Garda and colleagues described a 74 year old man transferred after confusion and a head CT suspicious for intraventricular and subarachnoid hemorrhage, bleeding in or around the brain fluid spaces. At the receiving hospital he was alert without focal neurological deficits. Dual energy CT, which uses different X ray energy information, produced iodine subtraction images attributing the dense appearances to iodine. Gradient echo MRI also supported absence of hemorrhage. He remained hospitalized for several weeks and was discharged to a retirement home. Prior contrast exposure was suspected, but the outside records were unavailable; a preceding myelogram was not documented. Exact length of stay and long term function were not reported. This is a documented diagnostic resolution, not evidence that every dense area is contrast or that the scan itself caused recovery. The uncertainty in the exposure history remains part of the case. [14]

## A hypothetical example of interpreting a report

Teaching example only: imagine a report describing an indeterminate lesion and recommending comparison with earlier imaging. This is not a published patient and no outcome is being asserted. Indeterminate means the scan has not settled the nature of that finding. The useful next conversation concerns the specific location, what earlier studies exist, and the recommendation made by the radiologist. A radiologist interprets the examination and sends a report to the referring clinician. Some questions need another technique, such as MRI, because different methods show different tissue details. Treat the report as a clinical communication that needs to be connected to symptoms and prior information, rather than translating every finding into a diagnosis independently. [3]

## A hypothetical calculation about scan length

A scanner dose report may list CTDIvol in milligray, or mGy, and dose length product, or DLP, in mGy cm. CTDIvol is a standardized scanner dose index based on a reference phantom; it is not a direct measurement of an individual organ dose. DLP incorporates scan length. Teaching calculation only: if CTDIvol stayed at 10 mGy over 30 cm, the product would be 300 mGy cm. Extending the same idealized exposure to 40 cm would give 400 mGy cm. This arithmetic isolates length while holding output constant; real protocols can vary output and contain multiple acquisitions. The numbers are invented for explanation, not targets for a patient. A single dose index should not be interpreted as the probability that that person will develop cancer. [9]

Absorbed dose describes deposited energy; effective dose is a calculated quantity that combines organ contributions with weighting factors. Its unit is the sievert, commonly expressed as millisievert in medical discussions. These quantities answer different questions. Converting a displayed scanner index into a neat personal risk percentage introduces assumptions that the index alone does not provide. When reviewing exposure, ask which quantity is being discussed and what was calculated rather than measured. [10]

## Risk depends on the patient and the purpose

Children require particular attention because developing tissues are more radiation sensitive and there is more remaining lifetime for a radiation associated cancer to appear. The NCI advises size appropriate techniques and avoiding unnecessary repeat acquisitions or contrast phases. A phase is an additional acquisition at a selected point in the contrast sequence; extra phases add exposure. These considerations support planning the examination around the question, not withholding useful imaging from a sick child. The appropriate comparison is the expected benefit and risk of the available choices for that child and situation. [11]

Radiation is one possible harm. CT can also reveal incidental findings that lead to additional tests, including invasive procedures, without ultimately identifying important disease. The FDA notes substantial uncertainty in estimates of cancer risk at the low exposures relevant to many imaging examinations. A useful discussion acknowledges that uncertainty while explaining the expected diagnostic benefit. An apparently reassuring scan cannot promise freedom from all disease, and an unexpected abnormality does not automatically establish a dangerous illness. The next action should follow the finding and clinical context. [12]

## An outcome trial with a defined population

The National Lung Screening Trial randomized 53,454 participants to three rounds of low dose chest CT or chest radiography. Participants were 55 to 74 years old, had at least 30 pack years of smoking, and were current smokers or had quit within 15 years. Pack years means packs smoked per day multiplied by years smoked. Lung cancer deaths numbered 356 versus 443; rates were 247 versus 309 per 100,000 person years. The relative reduction in lung cancer mortality was 20.0 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 6.8 to 26.7 percent. Across screening rounds, 96.4 percent of positive CT screening results were false positives, meaning lung cancer was not confirmed. That percentage uses positive screens as its denominator, not all participants. The trial demonstrates an outcome benefit and a follow up burden in a defined high risk population. It is not evidence for indiscriminate whole body screening, and its entry criteria are historical trial criteria rather than a statement of current screening eligibility. A person considering screening needs the current local eligibility and follow up pathway. [15]

## When urgency belongs in the explanation

Sudden face or limb weakness, difficulty speaking or understanding speech, new visual trouble, or abrupt loss of balance can be signs of stroke. In the United States, call 911 immediately for suspected stroke. Do not wait for an outpatient CT appointment or for a symptom to become severe enough to feel convincing. The emergency service must assess the person and arrange the appropriate investigation. A chapter about imaging cannot determine the cause remotely. Urgency here comes from the acute symptom pattern, not from the fact that CT exists or from an invented universal time limit for every scan. [16]

# H039: Blood pressure measurement

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Physiological and functional testing
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 3)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *A blood pressure reading helps answer whether pressure is too high or too low. The cuff, setting and repeated record determine how confidently that question can be answered.*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Context dependent



## What the two numbers describe

Blood pressure is the pressure of blood against the walls of arteries, the vessels carrying blood away from the heart. The top number, systolic pressure, describes the peak as the heart pumps; the bottom number, diastolic pressure, describes the lower pressure between beats. Results are reported in millimetres of mercury, abbreviated mmHg: 130/80 means two pressures, not a fraction to divide. High blood pressure often causes no symptoms, so feeling well cannot establish a normal result. Measurement may investigate dizziness or fainting, screen for otherwise silent hypertension, or monitor treatment. The clinical question changes the meaning of the same number: a routine check, a symptom assessment and monitoring during serious illness are different tasks. This chapter concerns adult human measurement; children and pregnancy require their own interpretation pathways. [1]

## How a cuff produces a result

A cuff temporarily compresses an artery in the upper arm. With the auscultatory method, a trained observer uses a stethoscope to hear Korotkoff sounds as cuff pressure falls. Their appearance and disappearance identify systolic and diastolic pressure. Most electronic monitors instead sense small pressure pulses in the cuff and use a calculation to estimate the two values; this is called the oscillometric method. The machine is not listening with a hidden stethoscope. Its particular calculation and cuff matter, so different models are not automatically interchangeable. Automated office systems can take several measurements after a rest period and average them, with staff present or absent. Manual measurement also needs training and equipment checks. Neither method becomes dependable merely because the display has precise digits. A catheter inside an artery measures pressure directly in selected hospital situations; ordinary cuff measurements are indirect estimates. [2]

## Preparing a reproducible seated measurement

For routine home measurements, the American Heart Association advises avoiding smoking, caffeine and exercise during the preceding 30 minutes, emptying the bladder, and resting quietly for at least five minutes. Use a bare upper arm, avoid talking or using the phone, and support the arm so the middle of the cuff is at heart level. Sit with the back supported, feet resting flat and legs uncrossed. Follow the monitor's fitting instructions rather than placing its cuff over clothing. At each session, take two readings one minute apart and record both. Agree on measurement times and frequency with the clinician. A record should preserve the actual numbers rather than only the lowest one. Bringing the monitor to an appointment lets staff check technique, fit and agreement with office equipment. A home result should inform the agreed treatment plan; it is not a reason to stop prescribed medication independently. [3]

## Cuff size has a measured effect

Cuff labels describe the arm circumference range for a particular cuff and monitor. A cuff that closes around an arm is not necessarily the correct size. In the 2023 Cuff(SZ) randomized crossover trial, 195 adults had repeated automated measurements using appropriate and inappropriate cuff sizes. When a regular cuff was used on participants who needed an extra-large cuff, systolic pressure averaged 19.5 mmHg higher than with the appropriate cuff; when a large cuff was required, the average excess was 4.8 mmHg. These were group comparisons under the study protocol, not a correction table for an individual. Subtracting 19.5 from somebody's result would introduce an unsupported estimate. The useful response is to measure the arm, obtain the compatible cuff and repeat the measurement correctly. Repeating an incorrectly fitted cuff many times can reproduce the same systematic error. [4]

## Arm position can move the numbers

The 2024 ARMS randomized crossover trial studied 133 adults using an automated monitor. Compared with a supported arm on a desk with the cuff at heart level, resting the hand on the lap increased systolic pressure by an average 3.9 mmHg; leaving the arm unsupported at the side increased it by 6.5 mmHg. Diastolic pressure also rose. This experiment measured differences in readings, not later strokes or medication injuries. The side position changed both height and support, so the result does not isolate one mechanism. It nevertheless makes a practical point: an arm dangling from an examination table is a different measurement condition. A repeat performed in the same poor position may look reassuringly consistent while retaining the same bias. The study averages should not be used to adjust a person's recorded pressure arithmetically. [5]

## Home monitoring needs a complete schedule

NICE's UK adult pathway, updated in February 2026, uses two seated home readings at least one minute apart, morning and evening, for at least four days and ideally seven. It discards day one and averages the remaining readings. Its diagnostic framework pairs clinic pressure at least 140/90 with daytime ambulatory or home average at least 135/85 mmHg; either component can be raised. These are specified UK criteria, not universal treatment targets. Hypothetical arithmetic example, not a patient: four retained readings of 140/88, 136/86, 132/82 and 128/80 have mean systolic pressure 134 and mean diastolic pressure 84 mmHg. Average each column separately. Four readings alone do not satisfy that complete monitoring schedule. An average made from selectively saved readings is also not the average required by the protocol. Ask which schedule and threshold the local service is using. [6]

## Ambulatory monitoring follows a day and night

Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, or ABPM, uses a wearable recorder and cuff to collect repeated measurements during usual waking activity and sleep, commonly over 24 hours. It answers a different question from a few quiet home readings. The report should distinguish waking, sleeping and whole-record averages, identify how many readings were usable, and show individual measurements with their times. A diary of actual sleep, symptoms, activities and medication timing helps explain the record; clock time alone may misclassify a night worker's sleep. Cuff inflation can disturb sleep or be uncomfortable, and movement can interfere with individual measurements. Missing or erroneous readings need review before a smooth-looking average is accepted. A night-time pattern is an interpretation of a recording obtained under particular conditions, not a diagnosis from one isolated peak. Follow the fitting service's instructions and report a disturbed or atypical night. [7]

## Validation is evidence about a device

A validation study compares a monitor with a reference method using a defined protocol, a range of pressures and arms, and rules for judging disagreement. The AAMI/ESH/ISO collaboration explains why testing needs adequate participants, cuff representation and separate consideration of special populations. Validation is about the tested model and configuration; a familiar brand name is not enough. Evidence in ordinary adults does not automatically establish performance in pregnancy, very young children, unusually large arms or atrial fibrillation, an irregular heart rhythm. Importantly, passing a protocol does not guarantee that every individual's reading is accurate. The collaboration explicitly acknowledges remaining individual errors. Ask whether the exact monitor, compatible cuff and intended population have appropriate validation evidence. A repeated discrepancy with clinical measurements warrants checking the patient, technique and equipment rather than assuming either the home device or the clinic must always be right. [8]

## Wearable claims need a specific check

The US Food and Drug Administration warned in September 2025 about unauthorized products claiming to measure or estimate blood pressure, including software on smartwatches and rings. A number appearing in a health app does not establish that the feature has been evaluated for this medical purpose. The agency recommends checking the exact device name and intended use in its authorization records and discussing an appropriate authorized device when care depends on the result. This warning does not mean every wearable is unauthorized, or that all products have identical capabilities. Product status can change. Authorization for one feature also should not be assumed to cover a separate blood pressure claim. A seller's medical-looking advertisement, a wellness score and an authorized blood pressure measurement function are different pieces of information. Do not use an unverified estimate as the sole reason for changing medication or delaying assessment of concerning symptoms. [9]

## Standing pressure asks another question

Orthostatic hypotension means a sustained pressure fall after standing: conventionally at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes. It can coexist with high resting pressure. The 2024 AHA scientific statement describes assessment after a period lying down, followed by standing measurements; the timing, starting posture and symptoms affect what is detected. A single normal seated result cannot exclude a posture-related problem. Clinicians consider patterns, medicines, other illness and the person's risk of falls rather than treating every low reading as the same condition. Do not attempt unsupported standing measurements when fainting or unsteadiness makes standing unsafe. Recording when dizziness occurs and whether it follows standing gives the clinical team a useful question to investigate. The aim is to understand pressure regulation and symptoms together, rather than simply pursuing the lowest possible seated number. [10]

## Actual case: low arm pressure concealed higher pressure elsewhere

Nasrullah and colleagues reported a 59-year-old woman with severe vascular disease, anemia and dark stools whose pressure was initially 64/55 mmHg. Fluids, transfusions and escalating vasopressors, medicines that raise pressure, did not normalize arm readings. Even catheters in both wrist arteries recorded low pressures. On day four, a femoral artery catheter measured 174/71 while a wrist catheter measured 64/50 mmHg. Review of imaging identified severe narrowing of the arteries supplying both arms. Treatment was redirected using lower-limb measurements, and vasopressors were stopped within 24 hours. The outcome was not an uncomplicated recovery: the report describes strokes, ventilator-associated pneumonia and delirium during a prolonged admission; a later successful vascular repair is not documented. This was a site-dependent pressure problem, not proof that the cuff's electronics had failed. One case neither establishes its frequency nor justifies dismissing ordinary low pressures as artifact. [11]

## Actual case: hypertension was a clue to an obstructed aorta

Jurcut and colleagues described an adult woman with hypertension difficult to control despite several medicines. Arm pressures were 145/80 and 135/80 mmHg, but examination found no palpable femoral or more distal leg pulses. Echocardiography and CT identified coarctation, a narrowing of the aorta, the main artery leaving the heart. A catheter measured a 70 mmHg pressure difference across that narrowing. After balloon treatment and placement of a covered stent, the catheter gradient fell below 10 mmHg and leg pulses returned. She was discharged three days later. At six months, pulses remained present, low-dose antihypertensive treatment was still needed, and ultrasound showed a residual peak gradient of 27 mmHg. Ultrasound and catheter gradients are different measurements. The case illustrates why pressure, pulses and anatomy are interpreted together; it does not show that a routine arm result alone can identify the cause of hypertension. [12]

## Office and home disagreement can be informative

The 2025 AHA/ACC adult guideline recommends averaging repeated measurements rather than treating a single office value as usual pressure; at least two readings on at least two occasions can reduce error. It uses US hypertension categories beginning at systolic 130 or diastolic 80 mmHg, illustrating why the guideline and setting must accompany a label. White-coat hypertension describes high office but lower out-of-office pressure in an untreated person; masked hypertension describes the reverse. These are documented patterns, not proof that a patient is anxious or a monitor faulty. Hypothetical example, not a published patient: clinic 150/92 and a properly collected home average 128/78 suggest a possible white-coat pattern, requiring confirmation and follow-up. The reverse discrepancy also warrants attention. In treated people, related terms are white-coat effect and masked uncontrolled hypertension. Both records remain relevant; selecting whichever number is preferred defeats the purpose of measuring in two settings. [13]

## An outcome trial explains why targets require context

The SPRINT final report analyzed 9,361 participants at increased cardiovascular risk who did not have diabetes or previous stroke, randomized to systolic targets below 120 or below 140 mmHg. During the intervention period, with median follow-up 3.33 years, the primary combined cardiovascular outcome occurred at 1.77% per year with intensive treatment versus 2.40% per year with standard treatment. The hazard ratio was 0.73, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.63–0.86. That comparison concerns the rate over follow-up, not a 27 percentage point reduction in a person's absolute risk. The combined outcome included heart attack, other acute coronary syndromes, stroke, acute heart failure and cardiovascular death. Several serious adverse events, including hypotension, electrolyte abnormalities, acute kidney injury and syncope, were more frequent with intensive treatment. This supports an assessed treatment strategy in the trial population; it does not make its target a universal home-reading instruction or prove that measurement alone prevented events. [14]

## Home readings can improve care when somebody acts on them

In TASMINH4, 1,182 adults with treated but inadequately controlled hypertension were randomized in UK general practices to usual clinic-guided care, self-monitoring, or self-monitoring with electronic transmission of results. General practitioners used the readings to adjust treatment. At 12 months, 1,003 participants contributed to the primary available-case analysis. Compared with usual care, adjusted clinic systolic pressure was lower by 3.5 mmHg in the self-monitoring group and 4.7 mmHg in the transmission group. The difference between those two intervention groups was not statistically established. Adverse events were similar across groups. These are actual trial results for blood pressure control, not demonstrated reductions in deaths or strokes during this study. The intervention connected a measurement schedule to clinical review and medication decisions. Merely purchasing a monitor or collecting an ever-longer spreadsheet does not reproduce that care process; agree who reviews results, how they are shared and when feedback should occur. [15]

## When a reading needs prompt action

The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline defines severe hypertension in nonpregnant adults using systolic pressure above 180 or diastolic above 120 mmHg. Either component can cross its threshold; both do not have to be raised. [13]

The AHA's public guidance distinguishes very high pressure without new concerning symptoms from a possible emergency. Without emergency symptoms, repeat a very high reading after at least one minute. If it remains very high, contact a health professional as soon as possible. If pressure is in that range with chest pain, breathlessness, new weakness or numbness, visual change or difficulty speaking, call the local emergency service immediately; do not wait for pressure to settle. Pregnancy has a separate pathway. Keep the reading, time and medicine list available. Routine confirmation and emergency assessment serve different purposes. [16]

Do not let a reassuring home estimate delay assessment of severe new symptoms, or improvise medication changes from an unverified device result. An unauthorized wearable can miss an important pressure change as well as generate a false alert. [9]

# H040: Pulse oximetry

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Respiratory physiology and bedside monitoring
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 3)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft as of 7 November 2026. Independent clinical review pending. Abstract-only cases and the draft status of regulatory guidance are explicitly identified.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can a bright oxygen percentage be useful at the bedside and still conceal a dangerous problem with breathing or oxygen delivery?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical deterioration overrides the display

Suspected carbon monoxide exposure with confusion, collapse, or breathing difficulty requires emergency assessment now, even if a conventional pulse oximeter appears reassuring.

## What the display is estimating

A pulse oximeter uses light to estimate arterial hemoglobin oxygen saturation, usually displayed as SpO2. It also reports a pulse rate; these are separate measurements, and neither alone diagnoses the cause of breathlessness. At home, the FDA advises following the particular device's instructions, keeping the hand warm and relaxed, avoiding movement, removing nail polish from the measurement finger, and waiting for a steady reading. Recording the time, reading, and symptoms makes a trend more interpretable than an isolated screenshot. Poor circulation, temperature, pigmentation, and other conditions can affect accuracy even when a number appears. Device category matters too: some over-the-counter medical-purpose oximeters have FDA clearance, whereas general-wellness or sporting products are not evaluated for clinical decisions. Buying without a prescription does not, by itself, identify which category applies. The measurement should be read alongside how the person looks and feels and the monitoring plan agreed with their clinical team. [1]

## How two colors become an oxygen percentage

Oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb red and infrared light differently. A conventional oximeter illuminates tissue and detects how the received light changes through the pulse cycle. The pulsating component helps distinguish changing arterial blood volume from background absorption. Photoplethysmography, the optical recording of these volume-related changes, provides the signal from which the device compares the two colors. The ratio is converted through calibration into the displayed saturation estimate. The FDA's 2024 technical summary describes typical red and infrared wavelengths near 660 and 940 nanometers; exact optical designs vary. A transmission sensor detects light passing through tissue, whereas a reflectance arrangement detects returning light. Neither directly counts oxygen molecules or continuously samples arterial blood. Movement, an inadequate arterial signal, or additional light-absorbing substances can violate the assumptions behind the conversion. Understanding this pathway explains why a plausible display may still need confirmation when it conflicts with the clinical situation. [2]

## A cleared device has a particular scope

The FDA record for the MD300W512, MD300W314, and MD300W314B4 wrist oximeters illustrates why the model and intended use belong in the interpretation. Its 2018 clearance covers measuring, displaying, storing, and transmitting functional arterial saturation and pulse rate in adults, adolescents, children, and infants, with spot checking or data collection in specified settings. It is a prescription device. This is not a blanket statement about all wrist wearables, every sensor attachment, or every clinical purpose. A clearance applies to the described system and indications, rather than to the general idea of shining light at skin. A downloaded series of readings therefore still requires the device identity, suitable sensor, population, and recording conditions to be meaningful. A device capable of storing data has not automatically been established as a diagnostic test for the cause of nocturnal desaturation, sleep apnea, or a particular respiratory disease. Those questions may require a wider clinical investigation. [3]

## Worked example oxygenation is not ventilation

Consider an invented patient who becomes drowsy after a respiratory-depressant medicine while receiving supplemental oxygen. The pulse oximeter reads 98%. This is a hypothetical interpretation exercise, not a published case or a recommendation to adjust treatment. The number cannot establish that ventilation, movement of air sufficient to remove carbon dioxide, is adequate. SpO2 estimates hemoglobin saturation; PaO2 is the partial pressure of oxygen dissolved in arterial blood, and PaCO2 is its carbon dioxide counterpart. They answer different questions. The British Thoracic Society guideline advises blood-gas assessment when respiratory-depressant exposure or otherwise unexplained confusion raises concern for carbon dioxide retention. Supplemental oxygen can preserve an apparently satisfactory saturation while ventilation remains impaired. The unresolved question is therefore not simply whether the oxygen percentage is high enough, but whether breathing and acid-base balance are adequate. Clinical examination and appropriate blood gases or respiratory monitoring can answer questions the optical percentage cannot. [4]

## Published validation consumer numbers and arterial measurements

Harskamp and colleagues compared ten popular consumer oximeters with simultaneous arterial measurements in 35 intensive-care patients, DOI 10.1136/bmjresp-2021-000939. They obtained 2,258 oximeter readings alongside 234 arterial samples. None of the tested consumer devices met the study's cited overall accuracy criterion, although negative predictive values for its defined hypoxemia outcome were high in that particular sample. Predictive value depends on how often the condition occurs in the tested population; it cannot be carried unchanged into every home setting. The investigation excluded hemodynamically unstable patients and evaluated specific products available then. It did not test every inexpensive device sold in 2026 or establish a universal rule that all consumer measurements are unusable. It demonstrates why apparent agreement at many ordinary readings does not ensure that an individual low value, or a reassuring value in an ill person, is correct. Validation should address clinically consequential errors, not merely whether two sets of readings rise and fall together. [5]

## Published cohorts a disparity hidden by an ordinary display

Sjoding and colleagues' 2020 report, DOI 10.1056/NEJMc2029240, compared pulse oximetry with arterial co-oximetry in hospital data. In its Michigan subset with displayed SpO2 of 92% to 96%, arterial saturation below 88% occurred in 88 of 749 measurements from Black patients, compared with 99 of 2,778 from White patients. These are measurement pairs within a defined display band, not population-wide percentages of people with undiagnosed hypoxemia. The investigators found a similar disparity in a separate multicenter cohort. Race was a recorded patient category, not a direct measurement of skin melanin, and cannot specify the exact error for a particular person. The study highlights clinically important overestimation that can hide low arterial saturation. It does not support subtracting a fixed number from everyone in one racial group. Discordance deserves assessment using the actual patient, signal, device, and, when indicated, a direct arterial comparison rather than an identity-based correction formula. [6]

## Pigmentation evidence from a controlled experiment

A laboratory experiment answers a somewhat different question from a hospital-record study. Bickler, Feiner, and Severinghaus tested three named oximeter systems in eleven darkly pigmented and ten lightly pigmented healthy volunteers, DOI 10.1097/00000542-200504000-00004. During controlled oxygen-saturation plateaus, the systems overestimated arterial saturation more in the darkly pigmented group, especially at low saturation. The experiment directly examined pigmentation groups under controlled conditions, helping separate this issue from the use of race as a proxy in routine records. Its small volunteer sample and the particular 2005 devices limit generalization to every contemporary sensor or clinical situation. Healthy volunteer accuracy also cannot fully reproduce shock, motion, tissue changes, or the rapidly changing physiology of illness. The experiment establishes that optical measurement can interact with pigmentation; it does not tell a home user how many points to add or subtract. Better validation needs representative participants and clinically relevant conditions, not confidence based on a single average error. [7]

## Published outcome cohort beyond measurement disagreement

Fawzy and colleagues studied 24,504 patients hospitalized with COVID-19 at 186 US facilities, DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.30856. Pulse oximetry more often overestimated arterial saturation in recorded minority racial and ethnic groups. In a subset of 8,635 patients without an immediately recognized treatment indication, an unrecognized oxygen-based indication was associated with slower receipt of remdesivir or dexamethasone and higher odds of 30-day hospital readmission. The study did not find an association with in-hospital mortality or length of stay. This is outcome evidence beyond simply demonstrating a difference between instruments, but it remains observational. It cannot establish that an individual measurement error caused a particular readmission or that correcting an oximeter alone would prevent it. Recorded race and ethnicity were not direct skin-pigmentation measurements. The treatment context was a particular pandemic-era pathway, so its decision criteria should not be reused as universal oxygen or emergency thresholds for unrelated patients. [8]

## What current regulatory proposals do and do not establish

The FDA's January 2025 pulse-oximeter guidance proposes updated performance testing and labeling recommendations for medical-purpose devices, including better assessment across skin pigmentation. On the date checked, 7 November 2026, the official page still identifies it as draft guidance, not for implementation. That status matters: a proposal is not a final rule and does not retroactively validate every product already in use. Conversely, the existence of a draft is not evidence that all prior measurements are invalid. The clinical issue remains whether a particular system performs adequately for its intended population and decision. A clear description should distinguish a manufacturer's claimed performance, the conditions used to demonstrate it, and independent clinical evidence. Useful questions include whether darker pigmentation was represented, which saturation range was tested, and how poorly performing or absent signals were handled. An average accuracy statistic can conceal a subgroup difference or a clinically important individual error. [9]

## Published case normal pulse oximetry during carbon monoxide poisoning

Roth and colleagues reported a 53-year-old woman with vomiting, diarrhea, unilateral headache, tingling, and palpitations, DOI 10.1016/j.jemermed.2009.05.017. The report appeared online in 2009 and in the 2011 journal issue. Conventional pulse oximetry was described as normal, while a cutaneous carbon monoxide oximeter indicated carboxyhemoglobin, COHb, of 28%. The accessible primary abstract does not give the ordinary SpO2 number or a separate laboratory blood COHb result. She received oxygen with noninvasive positive-pressure support. Investigators found a malfunctioning water heater in her residence; the reported COHb fell to normal, she felt well, and she was discharged the next day. This is an actual example of misleading reassurance from conventional oximetry, but one successful detection does not establish the accuracy of every noninvasive CO monitor. The case facts are limited to the primary abstract; unreported neurological follow-up or blood confirmation should not be supplied from inference. [10]

## Carbon monoxide needs a different analytical question

Carboxyhemoglobin is hemoglobin bound to carbon monoxide. Conventional two-wavelength pulse oximetry cannot reliably distinguish this situation from normally oxygenated blood. CDC guidance recommends clinical assessment and COHb testing, commonly using multiwavelength laboratory co-oximetry on venous or arterial blood. This technique separates additional absorbing hemoglobin species rather than applying the ordinary two-color assumption. The time since exposure ended and oxygen already received can substantially change the measured COHb. A concentration therefore needs that history and does not closely track every patient's severity or later outcome. Suspected carbon monoxide exposure with significant symptoms, breathing difficulty, confusion, or collapse warrants emergency assessment now; a reassuring finger reading does not make waiting safe. Follow-up also matters because neurological effects may emerge after apparent initial improvement. The questions are whether exposure occurred, which hemoglobin forms are present, and how the patient is affected, rather than whether the ordinary SpO2 has crossed a single cutoff. [11]

## Published case benzocaine changed the hemoglobin being measured

Nappe and colleagues described a 29-year-old man with weakness, breathlessness, headache, dizziness, and grayish skin after self-treatment of toothache with benzocaine gel, DOI 10.1155/2015/670979. His SpO2 was 88% despite supplemental oxygen. Arterial testing showed PaO2 178 mmHg and a reported calculated saturation of 99%, but co-oximetry measured oxyhemoglobin 71.9% and methemoglobin 27.4%. These are different quantities, not competing measurements that can simply be averaged. After intravenous methylene blue, his symptoms improved; repeat co-oximetry showed methemoglobin 0.9%. He was discharged after four hours with symptoms resolved and advice to stop the exposure and obtain dental care. The full primary case supports this immediate outcome, not a long-term prognosis or a safe amount of benzocaine. It illustrates why a high dissolved oxygen pressure or calculated saturation can coexist with abnormal hemoglobin chemistry and impaired effective oxygen carriage. Co-oximetry resolved the discrepancy that an ordinary pulse percentage could not. [12]

## Methemoglobin changes the optical assumptions

Methemoglobin contains oxidized ferric iron rather than the usual ferrous iron and cannot carry oxygen normally. Inherited disorders or acquired oxidant exposures can increase this fraction. The EHA and EuroBloodNet consensus, DOI 10.1002/ajh.26340, emphasizes unexpected pulse-oximeter results or discordance with clinical appearance as reasons to investigate. With appreciable methemoglobin, many conventional instruments tend toward a reading near 85%, with limited ability to track the true severity. That characteristic behavior is not a diagnostic cutoff or a way to estimate the methemoglobin percentage from the screen. A plateauing SpO2 can conceal either deterioration or improvement. Laboratory measurement of the hemoglobin fractions and consideration of medications, chemicals, and inherited causes are needed. Severe symptoms require emergency assessment, whereas a stable person with a mild incidental finding needs a different evaluation pathway. The purpose of additional testing is to identify the abnormal absorber and its clinical effects, not simply to find another instrument displaying a preferred oxygen number. [13]

## Published trial detection and patient outcomes are different endpoints

Moller and colleagues randomized 20,802 surgical patients to perioperative pulse-oximetry monitoring or no such monitoring in a 1993 trial, DOI 10.1097/00000542-199303000-00007. Detected hypoxemia increased markedly with monitoring, and clinicians changed care more often. This does not mean the monitor caused the oxygen abnormalities; it made otherwise unrecognized episodes visible. The study did not find a significant reduction in overall postoperative complications, in-hospital deaths, or hospital stay. Its primary abstract supports those trial-level findings, not detailed reconstruction of individual participants or contemporary monitoring systems. The distinction remains useful: detection, response to detection, and improved clinical outcomes are separate links in a pathway. A tool may reveal a problem without a particular trial demonstrating a downstream mortality benefit. Equally, failure to show such a benefit in this setting does not establish that oximetry is useless in another population or that it can safely replace examination, staffing, or other respiratory monitoring. [14]

## Worked example a percentage is not the amount carried

Consider two explicitly hypothetical people with the same SpO2 of 97% but very different hemoglobin concentrations. Their percentage of available hemoglobin carrying oxygen may be similar while their total oxygen-carrying capacity differs substantially. This is an explanatory comparison, not a reported patient pair, transfusion rule, or treatment threshold. An oximeter does not measure how much hemoglobin is present. Jay and colleagues studied seventeen nonhypoxemic patients with acute severe anemia, DOI 10.1016/S0196-0644(94)70158-X, and found small average differences between pulse and arterial saturation. That finding is compatible with the teaching example: an accurate saturation percentage can coexist with anemia and does not quantify oxygen delivery to tissues. Their small study did not establish accuracy under every combination of severe anemia, low saturation, or circulatory failure. When oxygen delivery is the clinical question, a blood count, circulatory assessment, and the underlying cause may matter alongside saturation rather than being replaced by it. [15]

## Published forensic case beyond any living finger measurement

Tabian and colleagues reported an 80-year-old man with impaired mobility who died in an outdoor fire involving vegetation and household waste, DOI 10.1111/1556-4029.14649. The article appeared online in 2020 and in the 2021 journal issue. Postmortem femoral blood contained COHb 73.7% and hydrogen cyanide 1.3 micrograms/mL. Cyanide was analyzed 48 hours after death, limiting reconstruction of its concentration at death. Autopsy and toxicology documented severe burns and substantial toxic exposures; the authors discussed contributions from carbon monoxide and cyanide rather than providing a living oxygen-monitoring record. No premortem SpO2 was reported, so this cannot be presented as an observed pulse-oximeter failure or a case in which a particular monitor would have prevented death. No premortem treatment sequence is reported. The forensic contribution is the need for complementary analytes and anatomical findings when reconstructing a fatal fire. A pulse percentage, a blood hemoglobin fraction, a toxicant concentration, and tissue injury answer related but distinct questions and should retain their specimen and clinical context. [16]

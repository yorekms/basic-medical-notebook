# V013: Veterinary diagnostic radiography

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat, Horse, Hermann tortoise
- **Clinical Category**: Diagnostic imaging
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 4)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can an X ray reveal about hidden injury or disease, and when does the animal need another view or another test?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical signs determine urgency

Distension with repeated retching without bringing up material, respiratory distress, shock, or penetrating trauma requires urgent veterinary assessment; a familiar radiographic pattern need not be present.

## What an X ray image actually records

Radiography makes an image from X-rays passing through an animal to a detector. Different materials absorb or scatter different proportions of that beam. Gas, fat, soft tissue or fluid, mineralized bone, and metal therefore produce different shades. Radiopaque means relatively resistant to the beam and usually lighter on the displayed image. Radiolucent means relatively easy for the beam to traverse and usually darker. This is a projection: a three dimensional body is compressed into a two dimensional picture, with structures overlapping. Veterinarians consequently select several views rather than expecting one photograph to answer every question. Chest studies in dogs and cats commonly include both side views and a view from the front or back. Position and breath phase influence what becomes visible. The central lesson is that a radiograph records differences in physical attenuation; the diagnosis requires an interpretation of those differences in the correct anatomical and clinical setting. [1]

## Protecting people is part of obtaining the image

The American College of Veterinary Radiology recommends keeping radiation exposure as low as reasonably achievable while preserving diagnostic quality. This principle, abbreviated ALARA, applies to patients, staff, and the public. A brief exposure is still ionizing radiation, meaning it has enough energy to remove electrons from atoms. A clinic needs trained operators, equipment checks, protective equipment, and exposure monitoring for people who remain near the patient. The College advises that people leave the room during an exposure unless their presence is essential for animal welfare. Positioning aids and appropriately chosen sedation can help achieve this. Protective clothing reduces scattered radiation; it does not turn holding an animal into a risk free activity. For an owner, a useful question is how the team will obtain the necessary images while limiting handling, repeat exposures, and human presence close to the beam. [2]

## Why a painless test may still require sedation

Making the exposure does not hurt, but remaining motionless or being positioned can be difficult for an animal with fear, breathing difficulty, or a painful injury. International radiological protection guidance explains that sedation or anesthesia, when appropriate for the patient, can reduce the time people must remain close to the animal and improve positioning. Fewer failed images can also reduce repeat exposures. These benefits must be considered alongside the individual animal's medical condition and the risks of medication and handling. There is no universal rule that every radiograph requires anesthesia, or that avoiding sedation is always safer. The decision belongs to the veterinary team after assessment. The relevant comparison is between feasible ways of obtaining an adequate examination, including the stress and exposure associated with repeated unsuccessful attempts, rather than between an ideal motionless animal and the patient actually present. [3]

## Cropping the screen does not narrow the original beam

Collimation means restricting the X-ray beam to the intended anatomical area before exposure. Digital cropping happens afterwards and cannot undo radiation already delivered. A 2021 investigation of canine pelvic computed radiography combined review of 54 clinical images with a controlled experiment using one canine cadaver. The investigators examined image quality and scattered radiation at a location simulating a restrainer's eyes. Increasing exposure did not reliably buy better diagnostic images, while exposure choices influenced scatter. The study supports attention to beam size and exposure settings rather than assuming a brighter looking digital image is better. Its controlled cadaver measurements are not a direct estimate of every worker's lifetime dose. This distinction also explains why owners may see a carefully limited field of view: the examination should cover the clinical question adequately without making a large exposure merely to obtain a picture of more body regions. [4]

## Image quality comes before disease recognition

A technically poor image can conceal disease or create an apparent abnormality. In a 2023 study of automated quality assessment, investigators specifically labeled problems such as rotation, incorrect limb or neck positioning, blur, underexposure, overexposure, and objects superimposed on the chest. The algorithm performed differently across these tasks; identifying some errors accurately did not mean that it could classify every normal image with equal confidence. For readers, the useful idea is the sequence of decisions: first ask whether the correct area has been included and whether movement, position, or exposure compromises interpretation; only then evaluate the organs. A request for another view may be an effort to resolve a technical uncertainty rather than evidence that something alarming has been found. This study concerns canine chest images and a particular research system, so its results should not be transferred to every species, body part, or commercial product. [5]

## A clear chest image cannot exclude every small lung nodule

A nodule is a small rounded focus in tissue; an imaging finding alone does not establish its microscopic cause. In a 2012 study, 21 dogs with a primary tumor underwent computed tomography, or CT, and computed radiography, and nine also had film screen images. CT reconstructs cross-sectional images from X-ray measurements. It detected more nodules and smaller nodules than radiography. The modalities often agreed on whether a dog had any nodules at all, yet differed in the number identified and the observers' confidence. That is an important distinction between finding obvious disease and fully mapping its extent. A chest radiograph reported as showing no visible pulmonary metastases means none were detected with that examination; it cannot certify the absence of microscopic spread or every tiny deposit. The selected cancer population also cannot supply a universal detection rate for healthy dogs or cats. Additional imaging depends on whether greater detail could change the treatment decision. [6]

## An obstruction may be visible through its effects

An intestinal foreign object need not be directly outlined on an X-ray for the examination to be useful. Obstruction can produce an abnormal arrangement or dilation of bowel, so veterinarians evaluate patterns as well as visible material. A prospective 2016 study compared abdominal radiography and CT in 20 dogs, including eight with surgically confirmed mechanical obstruction. Experienced readers achieved useful diagnostic performance with both approaches. Although some numerical estimates favored one method for detecting obstruction and others favored the other method for recommending surgery, the differences were not statistically significant in this small sample. Sensitivity describes how often a test detects the condition among affected cases; specificity describes how often it is negative among unaffected cases. Neither is a promise about an individual animal. Persistent vomiting or deterioration can justify reassessment even when the first set of pictures is equivocal, meaning uncertain rather than definitely normal. [7]

## A real case when the expected stomach pattern was absent

A published 2022 case concerned a six year old neutered male golden retriever mix with restlessness, increased breathing effort, retching without bringing up material, and loss of appetite. Radiographs showed severe stomach dilation but lacked the usual configuration of clockwise gastric dilatation and volvulus, a dangerous stomach twist. Granular material near the stomach outlet initially suggested sand impaction. The dog developed aspiration pneumonia and remained unwell despite medical treatment. Surgery after approximately 72 hours revealed a counterclockwise twist; the stomach was corrected and secured surgically. The dog was healthy without recurrent signs at three months. This is a case report, not a safe waiting period: the authors linked the missed unusual configuration to delayed definitive treatment and greater morbidity. Repeated retching with a distended abdomen or respiratory distress warrants urgent veterinary assessment. An image that fails to show a familiar pattern does not override a persistently concerning patient. [8]

## A real case when small skin wounds concealed extensive injury

An eight year old male Vizsla presented after being missing for a day, with numerous small puncture wounds and signs of shock. The published report describes chest radiographs, point of care ultrasound, CT, and further assessment working together to locate pellets and characterize internal injury. The dog required blood transfusion, thoracic and abdominal surgery, and treatment of a pulmonary infection. Repeated chest radiographs helped monitor the lung changes; follow-up images after treatment showed resolution of pulmonary injuries. The dog was discharged after about two weeks in hospital, and the owner reported it alive and doing well three months after presentation. This outcome belongs to combined critical care, imaging, and surgery, rather than to an X-ray alone. The case illustrates why being able to walk and having only small visible wounds cannot establish that a penetrating injury is superficial. The report does not establish a general survival probability for gunshot injuries. [9]

## Forensic imaging documents objects without deciding intent

A separate forensic report described a five year old male Cane Corso found dead and submitted for investigation at the public prosecutor's request. Whole body radiographs taken before necropsy, an animal postmortem examination, showed multiple metal dense foreign objects consistent with shotgun pellets. Necropsy then documented their locations and internal tissue injuries; the authors attributed death to the consequences of multiple organ trauma and shock. The animal was already dead when these images were made: this was evidence gathering, not a diagnostic opportunity during treatment. The bounded lesson is that imaging can preserve the distribution of retained objects and direct a systematic postmortem examination. A pellet's appearance does not by itself establish who fired a weapon, intent, or a reliable firing distance. Those questions require additional evidence and specialist interpretation. No court verdict is reported here, and the paper's ballistic interpretations are not treated as independently proven conclusions in this chapter. [10]

## Dental radiographs can reveal disease below the visible crown

The part of a tooth seen in the mouth is only part of the structure being assessed. A prospective study of 226 dogs referred for dental treatment compared clinical examination with full mouth radiography. Images of teeth without visible clinical lesions sometimes supplied clinically important information; where clinical lesions were already present, radiographs often added information relevant to treatment. The population was selected for dental care, so these findings cannot be read as the prevalence of hidden disease in every dog. They do explain why looking at an awake animal's teeth is not equivalent to a complete dental assessment. A radiograph can show structures beneath the gum and relationships to surrounding bone that inspection cannot directly reveal. The practical outcome of imaging is a better informed treatment plan, not simply a collection of tooth pictures. The study supports diagnostic value while leaving individual decisions to the examination and findings. [11]

## Fracture follow up is a sequence rather than one reassuring picture

Radiographs are used to follow whether a broken bone is joining, whether its alignment is maintained, and whether fixation remains intact. A retrospective study of 442 dogs with 461 fractures examined delayed union, nonunion, and malunion. Union means the fracture has joined; delayed union means joining is taking longer than expected; nonunion describes a failure of progression that may require a different intervention; malunion means healing in an abnormal position. The investigators reviewed clinical records together with images and found that infection, implant failure, and other factors were associated with unfavorable healing categories. These are associations in treated clinical cases, not proof that any one factor caused an individual failure. The presence of some new bone is therefore only part of the assessment. Follow-up timing and permitted activity need to reflect the repair, symptoms, and progression, rather than a universal calendar for all fractures. [12]

## A horse purchase examination assesses risk in context

In a horse being considered for purchase, a radiographic abnormality needs to be interpreted alongside history, clinical examination, and the buyer's intended use. The current Merck Veterinary Manual account emphasizes that a comprehensive purchase examination will find some abnormalities in almost every horse. It is not a guarantee of future soundness. The significance of an imaging finding may differ between a horse intended for light leisure work and one expected to perform demanding sport. This does not make the image meaningless; it makes the question more specific. Which structure is abnormal, is the finding associated with current clinical signs, and what is known or unknown about future risk? The veterinarian's report should communicate that uncertainty. A buyer should preserve the actual images and report, since a later description such as passed the X-rays omits the scope and qualifications of the examination. [13]

## A real reptile case shows why species context matters

Radiography also serves animals whose anatomy is very different from that of dogs and cats. In a seven case report involving turtles and tortoises, a 51 year old female Hermann's tortoise had an abnormal presumed egg visible on radiographs two years before referral. After hibernation it became unresponsive and stopped eating; later evaluation found severe dehydration and a persistent large mineralized structure in the body cavity. Following supportive care, surgery removed a markedly thickened retained egg, and the tortoise's appetite returned. It remained without clinical signs during eight months of follow-up. These are the reported observations, not evidence that two years of egg retention is safe. The shell, reproductive anatomy, nutritional state, and clinical condition influenced the team's diagnostic and surgical choices. Applying a mammalian imaging assumption or home treatment to a reptile would miss those constraints. A radiopaque structure still needs anatomical identification and a clinical explanation. [14]

## Computer interpretation requires evidence beyond impressive accuracy

Artificial intelligence can assist image classification, but its performance depends on the images used to develop and test it. A 2021 canine chest study explicitly noted that its training and test datasets came from the same institution, even though different acquisition systems were represented. Poorly positioned or exposed images and some uncommon findings were excluded. The authors therefore stated that performance in other clinical settings still needed evaluation. This is a concrete example of a validation boundary: success on selected research images does not establish equal reliability on a frightened moving patient, an unusual breed, another hospital's equipment, or a rare lesion. An automated normal label should be understood as the output of a particular system under particular conditions. Clinical interpretation still needs the original images, history, examination, and an explanation of uncertainty. This chapter makes no recommendation for a specific commercial imaging algorithm. [15]

## Agreement between readers is different from a confirmed diagnosis

A 2025 study compared assessment of the chest on whole body images with assessment on more tightly collimated chest images in dogs and cats. Readers often reached equivalent assessments within the study's selected population. However, the investigators excluded some severely affected or poorly cooperative patients, obtained good quality images in an academic setting, and did not establish diagnoses with advanced imaging or pathology. Thus agreement about an image finding was the measured result; proof that every patient truly had or lacked a disease was not. This distinction helps readers evaluate imaging claims generally. Two observers can agree and still share the same limitation of the available view. The study does not justify replacing every targeted examination with a single large image. The necessary field and views should follow the clinical question, patient welfare, and radiation protection rather than an assumption that more anatomy always means a more complete answer. [16]

## The patient remains the reason for the image

A retrospective trauma study reviewed 63 dogs and 96 cats with histories of blunt injury and chest radiographs. Radiographic signs attributed to thoracic trauma occurred in roughly half of the dogs and nearly two thirds of the cats. Listening to the chest supplied useful clinical information, but respiratory rate alone did not predict thoracic injury reliably in that population. Importantly, radiographic findings did not correlate with outcome in the way the initial overall trauma score did. A dramatic image and a poor prognosis are therefore different statements. Conversely, an animal that initially seems less affected may still require assessment for hidden injury. The useful final report joins the imaging findings to clinical stability, other tests, and the next decision: treatment, monitoring, another view, specialist interpretation, or another modality. A normal or uncertain image should come with an explanation of what it can and cannot exclude in the animal being assessed. [17]

# V014: Veterinary diagnostic ultrasonography

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat, Horse
- **Clinical Category**: Diagnostic imaging
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 4)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What changes when a veterinarian can watch organs and fluid in real time, and what can still remain hidden?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: A focused scan supports urgent assessment

Trauma, a deteriorating puppy with intestinal signs, or severe persistent equine colic needs clinical assessment; a negative limited scan cannot guarantee safety.

## A moving picture built from returning sound

Ultrasonography uses sound above the range of human hearing to construct images from echoes returning to a probe, also called a transducer. Unlike X-rays, it does not use ionizing radiation. The familiar grayscale image is called B mode. A bright area returns stronger echoes than a dark area, but brightness is not a direct label for healthy or diseased tissue. The machine combines the timing and strength of many echoes into an image of a thin anatomical plane. Moving the probe explores adjacent planes in real time. This is especially useful for soft tissues, fluid spaces, and movement. Gas and bone interfere with transmission, however, and can conceal structures behind them. Lower frequency probes reach deeper tissues at the cost of some detail. The useful question is therefore specific: which structure can be examined adequately through the available acoustic window, and what information can that examination add? [1]

## A complete abdominal examination has a defined scope

The joint ACVR and ECVDI abdominal ultrasound consensus sets out a systematic examination for dogs and cats, including documentation, suitable equipment, patient preparation, and review of abdominal structures. The history, examination findings, and reason for the scan should be available to the person performing it. Hair clipping and coupling material usually improve contact; positioning and sedation are individualized to comfort and the clinical situation. Preparation instructions should come from the clinic, especially for an ill, young, or metabolically vulnerable animal. The operator adjusts depth, focus, gain, and probe frequency as the examination proceeds. Gain changes displayed echo brightness; it does not change the animal's tissue. A short bedside scan directed at a single urgent question is not automatically a comprehensive abdominal study. The report should make its scope and any structures that could not be adequately assessed clear, rather than allowing the word ultrasound to imply that every organ was fully examined. [2]

## The operator is part of the diagnostic method

The American College of Veterinary Radiology emphasizes that accurate ultrasound depends heavily on the examiner's training and skill. Finding the correct anatomical plane, recognizing artifacts, and knowing what else could explain an abnormality are learned clinical abilities. Interpretation also requires correlation with other imaging and laboratory findings. A sharp still image cannot show which structures were never scanned or whether the probe was angled incorrectly during a measurement. For an owner, asking who will perform and interpret the study, whether specialist review is available, and whether images and video clips will be retained is reasonable. These questions concern the completeness of the examination rather than the sophistication of the machine alone. A written report should distinguish observations from possible diagnoses and explain the recommended next step. Remote interpretation can be useful, but it relies on the diagnostic information actually captured and made available to the reviewing clinician. [3]

## Trauma ultrasound can detect fluid without identifying its nature

In a prospective 2004 study of 100 dogs evaluated after motor vehicle accidents, a focused abdominal ultrasound examination detected free fluid in 45. Fluid sampling was performed in 40 of those dogs: 38 had blood in the abdominal cavity and two had urine. Thus the image finding free fluid and the diagnosis abdominal hemorrhage were separate steps. The study supported rapid focused assessment by clinicians with limited previous ultrasound experience, but it did not turn that limited examination into a complete survey of every injury. This is also a useful forensic distinction. A clinical scan can document what was visible at a recorded time after a reported event. Fluid by itself does not prove the exact mechanism of trauma or deliberate harm. Establishing cause requires the history, examination, other investigations, and, in fatal cases where indicated, a systematic postmortem investigation. That final distinction is an interpretation of the test's scope, not a conclusion about these accident cases. [4]

## A changing fluid score may change the clinical picture

A later prospective study followed 101 dogs with motor vehicle trauma using an abdominal fluid score based on the number of locations where fluid was detected. Fluid was found in 27 dogs. Higher scores were associated with larger decreases in measures such as packed cell volume, the proportion of blood occupied by red cells, and with greater transfusion requirements. Among the 71 dogs receiving serial scans, 12 changed score, usually in the direction of more fluid. The score was a semiquantitative description, meaning an ordered estimate rather than a direct measurement of every milliliter in the abdomen. These findings explain why repeating a focused examination may be informative when the patient changes. They do not establish a universal schedule for every trauma case, nor does an initially negative scan guarantee that fluid will not subsequently accumulate. Clinical condition and the purpose of monitoring determine the next assessment. [5]

## An obstruction study illustrates why uncertainty matters

In a cross-sectional study of 82 acutely vomiting adult dogs, 27 had small intestinal obstruction by a foreign body confirmed at surgery or necropsy. Ultrasound produced a definite obstructed or not obstructed classification in 80 dogs, compared with 58 for radiography. It also gave greater diagnostic confidence in that study. The relevant ultrasound findings included a visible obstructing lesion, abnormal folding of intestine, or segmental dilation. These results support ultrasound as an important tool when an abdominal radiograph leaves uncertainty. They do not establish the same accuracy for every examiner, animal species, or cause of vomiting. Nor does the number of definite reports mean that every definite report was correct: confidence and truth are different quantities. The clinical task is to combine the image with the patient's signs and subsequent evidence, and to investigate persistent concern when findings and clinical progress disagree. [6]

## A real puppy series includes survival and death

Intussusception occurs when one part of intestine slides inside another, rather like a collapsing telescope. A 2012 report described three puppies with vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, and other abnormalities. Ultrasound showed concentric intestinal layers consistent with intussusception; surgery revealed double intussusceptions. One puppy underwent reduction of the affected bowel and two required removal and reconnection of damaged segments. Two puppies survived. The second case, a Saint Bernard puppy with severe anemia, a low red blood cell concentration, before surgery and nonviable bowel, died on the fourth postoperative day, according to telephone follow-up. The authors proposed possible contributors to its death; the report does not establish a single proven cause. The lesson is more substantial than recognizing an attractive target pattern on a screen: diagnosis, the viability of the bowel, circulatory and blood abnormalities, and postoperative care all influence outcome. The interval described in a case report should never be used as permission to wait with a deteriorating puppy. [7]

## A real toothpick case connected diagnosis and image guidance

A ten year old spayed female bull terrier with abdominal pain underwent ultrasound and CT that identified a foreign body passing through the spleen. Ultrasound showed a bright linear structure with an acoustic shadow, a dark region behind an object that blocks or strongly redirects sound. In this selected case, the team removed a toothpick under ultrasound guidance without opening the abdomen widely. Monitoring and repeated scans found no complication, and the dog was discharged after 24 hours. At two months it was in good condition without recurrent signs, and follow-up ultrasound identified no lesion. This was a specialist procedure with preparation for possible bleeding or failure, not an owner treatment technique. The reported success does not establish that every splenic foreign body can be managed the same way. The team selected this approach from the object's location, surrounding findings, and the animal's condition; CT supplied complementary anatomical information. [8]

## A real grass awn case shows how a small object can migrate

A 2020 case report concerned a 13 year old male mixed breed dog with blood in the urine and fever. Ultrasound of the urinary and genital tract identified a migrating grass awn in the right lobe of the prostate. The awn was removed surgically with ultrasound assistance. Recovery was uneventful, and at four months the owner reported complete resolution of signs and return to normal activity. The report establishes the object, its location, the intervention, and that follow-up outcome; it does not establish how often this diagnosis explains similar signs in other dogs. A grass awn is plant material that can lodge and move through tissues, so the final site need not correspond to a conspicuous external wound. In this example, imaging changed the problem from an unexplained collection of symptoms into a localized, treatable lesion. The history and laboratory investigation remained relevant even after the object became visible. [9]

## A mottled feline spleen is not a microscopic diagnosis

Echotexture describes how an organ's echo pattern is distributed. A spleen may be described as mottled or moth eaten, but that visual description should not be silently translated into cancer. A multicenter retrospective study included 195 cats with splenic ultrasound and cytology, an examination of cells collected by needle sampling. The moth eaten pattern showed poor agreement with malignant neoplasia on cytology. A mass larger than one centimeter was more suggestive of malignancy, but this feature had low sensitivity, detecting few affected cases, so its absence could not reliably exclude malignancy. The probe frequency also influenced how often a marbled appearance was recorded. These observations show why image settings and equipment are part of interpretation, and why sampling may be needed when a diagnosis would change management. Cytology itself was the study's comparison method; the findings are not a claim that every lesion was definitively classified by whole organ histology or that a cat with a bland scan is free of cancer. [10]

## A cavity inside a canine splenic mass does not establish cancer

A 2021 study examined 106 dogs with blood in the abdomen, splenic lesions, surgery, and microscopic examination of the removed tissue. The investigators asked whether describing a lesion as cavitated, meaning containing cavity like areas on ultrasound, predicted hemangiosarcoma or malignancy. It performed poorly. For hemangiosarcoma, sensitivity, detection among dogs with that cancer, was about 42 percent; specificity, correctly negative classification among dogs without it, was about 51 percent in this selected group. Those results do not support treating cavitation as a cancer diagnosis. This matters because an owner may need to make an urgent treatment decision while the eventual microscopic diagnosis remains uncertain. Ultrasound can locate a lesion and fluid, guide planning, and identify other abnormalities while still leaving the nature of the mass unresolved. The study included dogs that reached surgery and tissue diagnosis; it cannot directly describe animals euthanized without confirmation or provide a universal prognosis for all dogs with a splenic mass. [11]

## Finding one urinary stone may not complete the search

A prospective study of 51 cats compared ultrasound with nonenhanced CT for stones in the ureters, the narrow tubes connecting kidneys to bladder. CT identified more stones and helped localize them, particularly where ultrasound could not follow the whole ureter. Ultrasound also provided information about dilation of the kidney's collecting system and the ureter. Neither detecting a stone nor measuring dilation alone answered every question about obstruction or kidney function. The study specifically investigated imaging detection and did not verify every finding against surgery or necropsy; it also selected cats on clinical suspicion and ultrasound findings. Its results therefore cannot be converted into an all purpose screening accuracy claim. For readers, the useful principle is to ask whether the entire relevant structure was adequately assessed. When clinical concern persists, a second method may answer a question that the first examination could not resolve. [12]

## Normal size depends on which animal and which structure

A measurement becomes meaningful through an appropriate comparison. A study of 50 healthy dogs described the appearance of medial iliac and superficial inguinal lymph nodes, including their size, shape, margins, and relative echo brightness. Larger body dimensions and weight were associated with larger nodes. This makes a single size threshold applied to every dog an oversimplification. A node is also not judged by size alone: its architecture, location, surrounding tissues, and the clinical reason for examination matter. These were preliminary reference observations in healthy dogs, not a test proving which enlarged nodes contain cancer. A useful report preserves the actual measurement, anatomical site, and interpretation rather than leaving an owner with only the word enlarged. Comparisons over time are strongest when the same structure is measured in a consistent plane, with differences in technique and patient context considered before declaring true biological growth. [13]

## Guided sampling adds information and a different set of risks

Ultrasound can guide a needle toward a lesion, but the safety of scanning should not be confused with the safety of taking tissue. A study of 102 dogs undergoing percutaneous ultrasound guided liver biopsy found decreases in packed cell volume in most dogs. Forty three met the study's definition of major hemorrhage, although clinically recognized complications occurred in only two. The word major reflected a defined blood measurement change and should not be read as 43 dogs suffering collapse. The population largely had normal or only mildly abnormal coagulation variables, so the results cannot establish safety in every patient with a bleeding disorder. The clinical team must weigh diagnostic benefit, the type of sample required, alternatives, and monitoring. An owner should understand whether the planned appointment is imaging alone or imaging followed by a potentially invasive procedure, since consent and aftercare differ. [14]

## Lung ultrasound uses surface signs and artifacts

Gas limits direct visualization of normal aerated lung, but the pleural surface and patterns of returning sound still provide clinical information. B lines are bright vertical artifacts extending from the lung surface image; they are not literal white strands inside the lung. A prospective study of 200 dogs and cats with breathing difficulty compared probe types and a score based on B lines. Results with microconvex and linear probes agreed closely, while the score's ability to distinguish selected causes of breathing difficulty was only fair to moderate and differed between dogs and cats. A high score should therefore not automatically be called heart failure. The history, heart assessment, other lung features, and response to treatment can be essential to interpretation. This example also demonstrates that useful ultrasound information sometimes comes from understanding an artifact, while recognizing that an artifact can change with the equipment and examination method. [15]

## A horse with colic needs a focused clinical question

Ultrasound in a horse with colic can look for abnormal intestine and free abdominal fluid without requiring the same examination used in a small dog. A prospective study developed a fast localized abdominal sonography protocol, abbreviated FLASH, in 36 horses referred for colic. Findings were compared with serial examination, clinical outcomes, surgery, or postmortem information. The scan could reveal important abnormalities promptly, and distended tense small intestinal loops helped identify horses requiring surgery. Its predictive values were imperfect, however, and depended on the study population. The result supports a focused emergency tool rather than replacement of the full colic assessment. Severe or persistent abdominal pain remains a clinical reason for urgent veterinary attention; an apparently reassuring limited scan is not a guarantee that surgery will be unnecessary. Decisions about referral combine the scan with circulation, pain, repeated examination, and the other information available. [16]

## A readily available probe has both value and limitations

A later equine study compared a low frequency curvilinear abdominal probe with a rectal format linear probe used on the body surface during FLASH examinations. Both could detect useful abnormalities, but they did not visualize every structure equally well. The stomach was identified more often with the abdominal probe, and the investigators discussed the effects of haircoat and operator experience. Rectal format describes the probe design here; it does not mean the owner should attempt an internal examination. The study is useful because field clinicians may have limited equipment and still need to make timely referral decisions. It does not establish that changing probe design has no consequences or that a structure not seen with one probe is normal. A report should distinguish not visualized from examined and unremarkable. That distinction preserves uncertainty and prevents absence of an image from becoming an unwarranted absence of disease. [17]

## Imaging recovery and clinical recovery can follow different clocks

In a prospective 2024 study of dogs with suspected pancreatitis, inflammation of the pancreas, investigators repeatedly assessed clinical severity, blood markers, and abdominal ultrasound over 28 days. Ultrasound abnormalities improved more slowly than some clinical and blood measures. Consequently, persistent imaging changes did not necessarily mean the animal remained as ill as it had been initially. The study was small, lacked pancreatic histological confirmation, and included no deaths; it does not define the course of every severe pancreatitis case. Its practical lesson is to compare like with like and ask what is being monitored. Pain, appetite, inflammatory markers, and tissue appearance are related observations, not interchangeable measures of the same instant. A follow-up report should describe the direction of change and its limitations. When symptoms, blood results, and images disagree, the disagreement deserves clinical interpretation instead of choosing whichever result seems most reassuring or most alarming. [18]

# V018: Veterinary fine needle aspiration cytology

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Cytology and tissue examination
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 4)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can a few cells from a lump reveal and what information remains outside the sample?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Urgency depends on the animal and the lesion

A cat with difficulty breathing needs immediate veterinary assessment while the cause is investigated. A cytology appointment or pending report does not replace that assessment.

## A small sample can answer a large question

Fine needle aspiration cytology examines cells collected through a thin needle from a lump, enlarged lymph node or selected internal lesion. Cytology means the study of cells; histopathology examines a piece of tissue with its internal arrangement preserved. This chapter concentrates on dogs and cats, because that is where the cited clinical evidence applies. The 2026 AAHA oncology guidelines emphasize that feeling or imaging a mass cannot reliably establish what it is. A fine needle sample may identify a fatty lump, inflammation or a round cell tumor such as lymphoma or a mast cell tumor. Other lesions require tissue biopsy. Learning which question has actually been answered is as important as learning the diagnosis: identifying tumor cells does not automatically establish their aggressiveness, complete surgical removal or spread elsewhere in the body. [1]

## What the pathologist is looking for

After staining, a veterinary clinical pathologist examines the kinds of cells, their appearance and their relationship to the material around them. Inflammation, infection and neoplasia, meaning abnormal new tissue growth, may produce different patterns. Cells can also be damaged during collection or preparation, making normal structures misleading or obscuring useful detail. Some tumors release cells readily; connective tissue tumors can be difficult to sample because their cells remain firmly attached. Nondiagnostic means insufficient information for a diagnosis, rather than absence of disease. The word suspicious likewise carries uncertainty rather than supplying a complete cancer diagnosis. Merck's veterinary cytology guidance recommends histology when findings are doubtful or do not fit the clinical picture. A reassuring interpretation from an unrepresentative sample cannot resolve a growing or otherwise unexplained lesion. [3]

## The diagnostic journey starts before the microscope

The laboratory needs the animal's identity, age, breed and sex, the exact sample location, how long the lesion has been present, relevant medications and the clinical or imaging findings. These details help determine whether the cells could plausibly explain the problem. Cornell's eClinpath guidance also stresses slide quality: thick material, crushed cells, blood contamination and poorly cellular fluid can reduce what can be concluded. Examining a preparation before the animal leaves may reveal an inadequate collection while additional sampling is still feasible. Air dried cytology slides and formalin preserved tissue require different handling; formalin fumes can interfere with staining. These are professional collection and laboratory decisions. For the owner, an important question is whether the submitted material actually represented the intended lesion and arrived in interpretable condition. [2]

Fine needle sampling can be performed with or without suction, but the best choice is not identical for every site. Whitlock and colleagues compared both methods in 104 canine and feline lymph nodes, with each node sampled by both techniques and the interpreting pathologists unaware of the method. Samples obtained with aspiration were more often diagnostic and contained more cells; cell preservation and dilution by blood did not differ significantly. This was a study of ultrasound guided lymph node sampling, rather than proof that suction is always superior in every organ or tumor. It shows why apparently small technical choices can affect the chance of obtaining an answer. A repeat sample may be worthwhile when the first collection failed, provided the veterinarian judges the intended target and procedure appropriate. [5]

## Accuracy numbers need their missing samples

In Ghisleni and colleagues' study of 292 skin and subcutaneous masses from dogs and cats, 49 specimens were excluded because they contained too few cells. Among the remaining 243 specimens, cytology and histology agreed in 221, or 90.9 percent. For identifying neoplasia, reported sensitivity was 89.3 percent and specificity 97.9 percent. Sensitivity describes detection among lesions that truly had neoplasia; specificity describes correct exclusion among those that did not. Neither number means that every negative result is equally reassuring. In this selected series, the negative predictive value was 68.7 percent, and 21 lesions classified as nonneoplastic by cytology were neoplastic on histology. The study supports the value of the test while exposing two distinct limitations: some collections never yielded an interpretable result, and some interpretable samples still missed neoplasia. The percentages apply to this population and comparison, not every newly discovered lump. [4]

## A published case in which a mass was an infection

Boyd and colleagues described a six year old neutered male domestic longhair cat referred for rapid and difficult breathing. Echocardiography, an ultrasound examination of the heart, showed a mass in the cranial mediastinum, the front portion of the chest between the lungs, touching the heart. Fine needle aspirates contained round yeast structures consistent with Histoplasma, a fungal organism. The cat received oral fluconazole under veterinary care. The published abstract reports marked improvement in clinical findings, lesion size and antigen concentrations at subsequent checks. It does not establish a lifelong cure or provide enough follow-up detail to invent a final survival time. The lesson is specific: imaging located a real mass, while cell examination supplied evidence of an infectious cause that imaging alone could not establish. This unusual case cannot tell us how often other heart-adjacent masses are fungal. [11]

## A published series in which deeper lumps were tumors

Robinson and colleagues reported seven dogs with mast cell tumors located within muscle. All seven had a palpable mass diagnosed as a mast cell tumor by fine needle aspiration, followed by CT or ultrasound to assess location and disease extent. Six underwent surgery as their main treatment; three also received additional chemotherapy. All six surgical patients were alive at follow-up of at least seven months. One individual, a ten year eight month old Lhasa apso, was reported alive with a nineteen month disease free interval after surgery and additional chemotherapy. The remaining dog received chemotherapy without surgery and died twenty-three days later. These are observed outcomes in a small selected referral series, not a controlled comparison proving that surgery caused the survival difference. Cytology identified the tumor family; imaging, tissue examination and follow-up supplied other parts of the clinical story. [12]

## Lymphoma diagnosis and classification are different tasks

An aspirate of an enlarged lymph node can be highly useful when lymphoma, a cancer of lymphocytes, is suspected. Nevertheless, reactive lymph nodes can enlarge during infection or immune stimulation, and some lymphomas are composed of deceptively mature-looking cells. The Oncology Pathology Working Group's 2025 consensus considers cytopathology acceptable for diagnosing many canine nodal lymphomas when the clinical probability is high and exact subtype is not required. Complete classification requires histopathology with immunohistochemistry, which uses markers to identify cells within tissue. Flow cytometry measures markers on cells in suspension and helps determine their lineage. A molecular clonality result is not a replacement for this characterization. If an apparently negative aspirate conflicts with strong clinical suspicion, the consensus supports further sampling or histopathology rather than treating that result as the final word. These recommendations concern canine nodal lymphoma; feline intestinal disease is a different diagnostic setting. [14]

## Bone lesions show why classification can remain incomplete

Sabattini and colleagues studied 68 dogs with destructive bone lesions, comparing initial cytology or biopsy findings with a final diagnosis based on surgical or postmortem tissue, or follow-up for nonneoplastic lesions. Overall cytologic accuracy was 83 percent, but the tumor type was correctly specified in only half of the evaluated cases. Thus, recognizing a malignant process and naming its precise origin were different achievements. This matters when a painful bone lesion could represent a primary bone tumor, metastatic disease or a nonneoplastic condition requiring different treatment. The study does not justify choosing irreversible treatment from a vague cytology phrase alone. It supports integrating the sample with imaging, examination and any further tissue assessment needed for a defensible diagnosis, while keeping the limitations of this retrospective canine population visible. [6]

## The liver needs its tissue arrangement

The liver is an especially important limit to extrapolation. Merck's liver guidance describes useful applications of aspirates, including supporting hepatic lipidosis, or fat accumulation in liver cells, in cats, and recognizing certain infections or tumors. Many other liver disorders depend on how changes are distributed through the tissue. Individual cells on a slide cannot preserve that map. A sample therefore may fail to distinguish a reactive change from disease that requires architectural assessment, and absence of malignant cells does not exclude cancer. Liver biopsy remains the standard for diagnosing most hepatic disorders. A useful owner discussion separates the narrow question an aspirate could answer from the broader question the veterinarian still needs answered before recommending long term treatment. [7]

## A splenic result can miss a heterogeneous mass

A splenic mass may contain blood, dead tissue, reactive tissue and neoplasia in different regions. Holter and colleagues tested 25 canine spleens after surgical removal, using ultrasound guided aspiration and needle core sampling through an artificial skin covering. Final excisional histology was the comparison standard. FNA accuracy was 72 percent, with sensitivity 71 percent and specificity 75 percent for neoplasia; the tested core approaches did not provide a statistically demonstrated improvement. Crucially, these were ex vivo samples, collected outside the living animal. The experiment cannot establish the bleeding risk of sampling an unstable dog, and it was not a survival trial. It illustrates why collecting cells from a small part of a mass may leave substantial diagnostic uncertainty. A negative or nondiagnostic aspirate should not be translated into a guarantee that a concerning splenic mass is benign. [8]

## Chest sampling has a different risk setting

Vignoli and colleagues examined 62 animals, comprising 52 dogs and 10 cats, that underwent both CT guided aspiration and tissue core biopsy of thoracic lesions. Cytology yielded a diagnosis in 43 of the 62 samples. The detailed results describe CT detected mild to moderate pneumothorax, meaning air entering the space around the lung, and mild hemorrhage. No clinical manifestations or need for further therapeutic intervention were reported. Complication category counts differ within the publication, and both procedures were performed, so these findings should not be presented as a precise FNA-only complication rate. They also cannot estimate the risk of sampling a simple skin lump. The relevant decision balances diagnostic information against lesion location, surrounding structures and the animal's condition. A needle procedure inside the chest has a different clinical risk setting from sampling a superficial lump. [10]

## Diagnostic yield is not the same as a confirmed answer

Cordner and colleagues retrospectively reviewed pancreatic aspiration in 92 dogs. Diagnostic yield was 73.5 percent: this measures how often the samples allowed a cytologic diagnosis, rather than how often every diagnosis was proven correct. The main process identified on cytology agreed with confirmatory testing in ten of eleven evaluable cases, a much smaller comparison group. Seven adverse events were recorded during the following forty-eight hours, all in dogs with substantial additional illness or other invasive procedures. Those associations cannot show that aspiration caused every event, or that it caused none. The study offers useful evidence about feasibility and observed problems while leaving uncertainty about accuracy and causation. Reading only the favorable yield figure would conceal both the nondiagnostic samples and the limited independent confirmation. [13]

## A rare reported complication deserves proportion

Mizuno and colleagues described a fourteen year old spayed female miniature Dachshund with a mass at a previous incision site seven months after removal of a lung adenosquamous carcinoma, a malignant epithelial tumor. The authors presumed needle tract implantation following earlier transthoracic fine needle biopsy. The new mass was widely removed, and tissue examination supported adenosquamous carcinoma with a marker associated with lung origin. No local recurrence was observed, but the dog died. The abstract does not establish that implantation caused the death. This is an individual report of a presumed mechanism, with the incidence for this tumor stated to be unknown. It establishes a possible concern requiring professional judgment and follow-up, rather than a percentage risk that can be applied to all aspirations. Equally, calling an event uncommon is not a reason to erase it from the consent discussion when the particular lesion makes it relevant. [9]

## Follow-up cytology can inform rather than guarantee

Lee and colleagues studied 36 incompletely removed mast cell tumors in 33 dogs whose surgical scars were aspirated within sixty days. Local recurrence occurred in five tumors overall. Among the 31 tumors grouped as lacking cytologic evidence of residual tumor, two recurred; among the five with residual tumor on cytology, three recurred. The negative predictive value was therefore 93.5 percent in this study. This is useful prognostic information, but a negative scar aspirate did not eliminate recurrence. Sampling methods and additional therapies were not standardized, and the retrospective design limits causal conclusions. The practical question becomes whether the result changes the recommendation for further local treatment or monitoring in this particular dog. It does not certify that every microscopic tumor cell has been removed or determine the optimal follow-up schedule for every patient. [15]

## Digital images still need the right cells

A scanned slide can allow a pathologist elsewhere to review an aspirate, but digitization does not correct an unrepresentative collection. The ASVCP digital cytopathology quality guidelines address preparation, scanning, image quality, staff training and validation of the particular system. A scan can omit a relevant region or fail to resolve fine detail; some cases require review of the original glass slide. The reader should therefore distinguish a laboratory's validated diagnostic workflow from a casual photograph or an automated label. The relevant question is whether the system captured and displayed the information needed for this specimen and whether a qualified interpreter assessed its limitations. These guidelines do not establish that an unvalidated artificial intelligence application can independently diagnose a pet's mass. [17]

## Urgency follows the animal as well as the sample

For cats, difficulty breathing is a reason for immediate veterinary assessment, even before the cause is known. Cornell's Feline Health Center explains that respiratory distress is a clinical sign with many possible causes, including heart disease, airway disease, fluid around the lungs and tumors. An owner should not wait for a scheduled cytology result or attempt to solve the breathing problem at home. This does not mean every skin lump creates the same emergency. It means a test appointment cannot substitute for assessing a change in the animal's condition. Urgency comes from the animal's condition while the diagnostic team works to establish its cause. [16]

## Forensic use requires a record larger than a slide

A forensic investigation asks questions about injury, disease and care in a legal setting, and its conclusions require more than identifying cells. Touroo and colleagues' minimum standards for examining suspected abused animals emphasize complete records, diagnostic investigation, preservation of evidence and documentation of changes over time. Necessary treatment to protect health and relieve suffering takes precedence over evidence collection. Applied to an aspirate, this means preserving the identity and origin of the specimen and linking the laboratory result to the examination, photographs, history and other findings. Inflammation on a slide alone does not establish who caused a lesion, the person's intent or how long a condition was neglected; those would be additional inferences requiring separate evidence. A tumor diagnosis may explain a lesion medically, while the investigation still examines whether and how the animal's needs were addressed. This is an interpretive boundary, not a claim that cytology can reconstruct an entire event. [18]

# V019: Veterinary tissue biopsy and histopathology

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *{'text': 'A tissue diagnosis depends on what was sampled, what survived processing and how the microscopic findings fit the dog or cat in front of the veterinarian.', 'sources': [1]}*

## The diagnostic question begins before the tissue reaches the laboratory

A biopsy is tissue collected from a living animal for examination. Histopathology is the study of that tissue's microscopic structure: which cells are present, how they are arranged, and whether they invade neighboring structures. Cytology examines individual cells or small groups, often from a needle sample. The American Animal Hospital Association's 2026 oncology guidelines describe why cytology can establish many round-cell tumor diagnoses, while histology often adds architecture, invasiveness and grade. Neither method automatically makes the other unnecessary. A fatty-looking lump, an inflamed lesion and a malignant tumor can resemble one another clinically; the investigation should identify what uncertainty the proposed sample will resolve. [1]

Patient history is part of histopathologic interpretation. Species, breed, age, lesion location, duration, distribution, previous treatment and imaging findings can change the meaning of the same microscopic pattern. The University of Prince Edward Island requests this history, relevant laboratory results and a description of the lesion's size and appearance; clinical photographs can help. Signalment means the animal's basic identifying clinical characteristics. A report saying 'ulceration and inflammation' may be accurate yet incomplete if the submitted fragment came from an ulcer covering a deeper mass. Explaining exactly where the specimen came from helps the veterinarian and pathologist decide whether the original question has actually been answered. [2]

## Select the lesion and the depth that can answer the question

For skin disease, the newest visible abnormality is not always the best specimen, and the oldest damaged patch is often misleading. Texas A&M's diagnostic laboratory favors well-developed primary lesions: changes caused directly by the disease, rather than scratching, scarring or healing. Crusts and scales can contain important evidence. Sampling several appropriately selected sites can show a pattern that one fragment misses. Previous corticosteroids and secondary infections may alter that pattern, so preparation belongs in an individualized veterinary plan. Owners should provide a complete medication history; this does not translate into stopping treatment themselves or applying a universal waiting period to a deteriorating animal. [3]

Depth matters because skin includes several different diagnostic compartments. Disease centered in the epidermis, the outer surface, differs from disease of hair follicles or subcutaneous fat. The University of Guelph explains that a superficial sample can miss panniculitis, inflammation of that deeper fat, and that fragile lesions require special consideration. The appropriate edge or center also depends on the suspected process: active pigment loss, a pustule, an ulcer and hair loss do not share one ideal sampling location. When a feature expected clinically is absent from the slides, the team should consider whether the lesion was represented before treating a negative-looking section as proof that the disease is absent. [4]

## Preserve both tissue structure and the tests that may still be needed

Fixation stabilizes tissue so that its cells and architecture survive processing. Neutral buffered formalin is a standard laboratory fixative, but penetration into a large or dense specimen is limited; an intact-looking surface can surround a poorly preserved center. The University of Missouri's submission guidance therefore addresses specimen thickness, prompt fixation and recorded fixation time. Receiving laboratories differ in some handling requirements, so the submitting veterinary team should follow the laboratory's instructions for that specimen. A large excision, an eye and tiny endoscopic fragments need different planning. Fixation is a controlled diagnostic step, not simply placing every possible specimen into the same container. [5]

Some questions need material that has not been fixed. Bacterial or fungal culture seeks organisms that can grow, while toxicology and other ancillary tests may require separately handled tissue. Ancillary means additional to the routine microscopic examination. Calgary's Diagnostic Services Unit explicitly notes that fresh samples may be necessary to confirm a suspected diagnosis. This allocation is best planned before collection because formalin-preserved tissue cannot later be converted back into a fresh culture specimen. The clinician and laboratory decide which portions serve histology and which serve other tests, maintaining their individual identities. Finding inflammation without having reserved appropriate material can leave the cause unresolved despite technically good slides. [6]

## Orientation connects a microscopic finding to a place in the animal

A surgical margin is the cut boundary of an excised specimen. If tumor reaches one boundary, the surgeon needs to know whether that boundary was deep, toward the head, or on another side. Tennessee's biopsy service recommends orientation markers and surgical marking dye when margin assessment is needed. These markers require a clear key; an unexplained suture does not identify an anatomic direction. The submitted specimen's orientation should be preserved during handling and laboratory trimming. Cutting through an important boundary without documenting it can make a later measurement difficult to interpret. A useful margin result is a mapped observation, not merely the word 'clear' printed without context. [7]

The direction in which tissue is sectioned can also determine whether a lesion is visible. Hair follicles are three-dimensional structures, and a folded or poorly oriented skin fragment may not show them in an interpretable plane. Georgia's dermatology guidance describes both orientation concerns and crush artifact, distortion caused by squeezing delicate tissue. Damage may erase subtle inflammatory features or make cell types unrecognizable. The reader should distinguish a genuinely normal sampled structure from a structure the preparation did not preserve. If the clinical pattern strongly conflicts with the report, discussion with the pathologist may identify a need for differently oriented sections or a better representative specimen. [8]

## From the specimen to the slide

In the laboratory, gross examination records visible features and selects tissue for microscopic processing. Histotechnicians dehydrate the selected pieces, embed them in supporting paraffin wax, and cut very thin sections with a microtome, a precision cutting instrument. The University of Delaware describes how orientation is maintained through this sequence. Sections are placed on glass and routinely stained with hematoxylin and eosin, abbreviated H&E, so tissue relationships become interpretable under the microscope. The finished slide is a thin plane through selected material. It is not a transparent view of every cell in the entire mass; selection and sectioning remain part of what the result can establish. [9]

Hard tissues require additional processing. Michigan State University's guidance explains that bone specimens may need decalcification, removal of mineral so thin sections can be cut, and that superficial bone samples can miss the underlying disease. Radiographs help the pathologist relate a sampled area to lysis, destruction of bone, or new bone formation. An inflammatory reaction around a bone tumor and an infection can overlap in a small fragment. The location of the biopsy therefore belongs with the imaging. Extra processing or consultation can extend reporting time without implying that the specimen has been forgotten. Clinical support for an unstable or painful animal proceeds while that diagnostic work continues. [10]

## Recognize damage and sampling limits before interpreting disease

Artifact is a change introduced by collection, storage or processing rather than by the living disease. Texas A&M illustrates how heat from cautery can distort cells at surgical edges, freezing can leave crystal-related gaps, and crushing can destroy detail. After death, autolysis, breakdown by the tissue's own enzymes, and bacterial overgrowth can further obscure lesions. Severe artifact may prevent a confident diagnosis or margin assessment. It should be described as a limitation rather than silently interpreted as normality. The appearance of damaged tissue also cannot by itself establish when an injury occurred, whether it happened before death, or who was responsible for an animal's condition. [11]

Sampling limitations were demonstrated directly in Kemp and colleagues' study of livers from seventy dogs undergoing necropsy. Diagnoses from needle, cup and punch samples agreed with larger comparison samples in 66%, 60% and 69%, respectively; the differences among those proportions were not statistically significant. Even the larger punch specimens did not solve every disagreement. Portal triads are small regions containing a branch of the bile duct and blood vessels, used to judge how much liver architecture is represented. This was a postmortem sampling experiment, not evidence that one technique improved survival. Its practical lesson is that a technically acceptable fragment may still incompletely represent disease within an organ. [12]

## Intestinal biopsies require species and compartment context

The 2026 American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine guidance for canine chronic inflammatory enteropathy emphasizes adequate specimens from the relevant intestinal segments and proper orientation. Enteropathy means disease of the intestine. A well-oriented mucosal sample shows the villi, the fingerlike absorptive projections, and underlying structures; a collection of villous tips cannot answer all the same questions. The guideline recommends systematic assessment using World Small Animal Veterinary Association criteria or their modified form. Additional stains and molecular tests are selected according to the clinical suspicion and initial histology. These are canine recommendations, and endoscopic mucosal samples do not automatically represent deeper wall layers or every intestinal segment. [13]

Cats present a particularly difficult distinction between lymphoplasmacytic inflammation and low-grade intestinal T-cell lymphoma. Lymphoplasmacytic means the infiltrate contains lymphocytes and plasma cells; T cells are one family of lymphocyte. The 2023 American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine consensus found that no single known marker reliably separates these conditions in every cat. Histopathology remains central, but the diagnosis integrates symptoms, imaging, tissue distribution and additional testing. A dense lymphocyte population is not a complete explanation by itself. The distinction matters because prognosis and management can differ, while the evidence supporting some diagnostic recommendations remains moderate or low rather than definitive. [14]

## Additional stains refine an interpretation rather than replace it

Immunohistochemistry, or IHC, uses antibodies to detect selected proteins, called antigens, within tissue sections. A pattern of markers can help identify a tumor's cell lineage or support an infectious diagnosis. Veterinary laboratory guidelines emphasize that antigen detection alone does not necessarily diagnose the disease: staining must be interpreted with tissue appearance and other findings. Validated methods and appropriate controls help distinguish the intended signal from background staining or technical failure. Likewise, a negative stain can reflect absent antigen, poor preservation or limitations of the antibody in that species and specimen. A panel chosen for the actual diagnostic question is more meaningful than one isolated positive label. [15]

Polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangements, shortened to PARR, examines rearranged lymphocyte receptor genes. A dominant repeated pattern supports expansion of a related cell population, called clonality. Clonality is not synonymous with cancer, and a negative result cannot exclude every lymphoma. Colorado State University's assay guidance shows why specimen type and species matter: canine formalin-fixed samples use a different primer set from fresh specimens, and performance estimates do not transfer freely between them. Primers are short DNA sequences that direct what the reaction can amplify. Low target-cell numbers, preservation and the tested gene regions can all affect the answer, so morphology and clinical context remain essential. [16]

## Documented examples show why an abnormal slide needs clinical follow-up

Marsilio and colleagues investigated intestinal specimens from twenty clinically healthy, client-owned cats aged at least three years. Integrated histology, immunostains and clonality were interpreted as small-cell lymphoma in twelve and emerging lymphoma in one. Yet seventeen cats remained without signs of chronic enteropathy after a median follow-up of 709 days. Three developed gastrointestinal signs; two were euthanized 295 and 654 days after endoscopy. These observations do not prove that every suspicious result was false or that future disease was impossible. They show that diagnostic patterns developed in selected populations may overstate clinically consequential disease when applied to outwardly healthy older cats, and that follow-up remains part of interpretation. [17]

An individually reported eight-year-old Mastiff illustrates the opposite danger: assuming that a later change must be the same disease becoming worse. Initial lymph-node cytology, histology, IHC and flow cytometry supported T-zone lymphoma. Thirteen months later, progressive node enlargement was associated with a large B-cell lymphoma. Sequencing performed after euthanasia supported two distinct concurrent clones rather than simple transformation of the first clone. The dog was euthanized two months after the B-cell diagnosis, fifteen months after the original lymphoma diagnosis; no necropsy was performed. The case supports reassessing a changed clinical and cellular pattern. It does not establish how often two separate lymphomas occur together. [18]

## Grade describes biological behavior within a defined tumor type

Tumor grade summarizes microscopic features associated with behavior, such as differentiation and cell division. It differs from stage, which describes the extent of disease in the animal. Kiupel and colleagues studied ninety-five canine cutaneous mast-cell tumors from ninety-five dogs, with blinded grading by twenty-eight pathologists. All dogs received surgical excision alone; surviving dogs were followed for up to four years. Their proposed two-tier system separated high-grade tumors associated with a median survival under four months from low-grade tumors associated with survival beyond two years. These are historical group outcomes, not a countdown for an individual dog. The result belongs to canine tumors arising in skin; it should not be transplanted unchanged to feline mast-cell disease or to tumors in a different tissue compartment. [19]

A 2026 prospective study enrolled thirty-two dogs to examine how soft-tissue sarcoma grade varied within one tumor. Although motivated by preoperative planning, investigators obtained one central and two superficial peripheral punch samples after removal, comparing their grades with the resection specimen. Reported single-biopsy grade agreement was 71% centrally and 59% peripherally. These percentages describe biopsy-to-resection comparisons; peripheral tissue was sampled twice per dog, so they are not independent dog-level outcomes. Undergrading occurred at both locations, and multiple samples improved agreement. The study did not test a survival benefit. A small low-grade specimen therefore may incompletely represent a heterogeneous mass. [20]

## Margins are measured evidence with a sampling method

International veterinary surgical-pathology recommendations distinguish the submitted specimen, the way it is trimmed, and the information eventually reported. A perpendicular section can show tumor-to-edge distance; a tangential section examines tissue along a boundary and answers a different geometric question. Neither approach inspects every possible point of the entire surgical surface. Reports should identify relevant margins, distance when measurable, and the tissue composing the intervening boundary. The Kamstock consensus itself acknowledges that many technical recommendations arose from collective experience rather than definitive trials. Thus, 'complete in the sections examined' is an evidential statement whose meaning depends on orientation, processing and the tumor's known behavior. [21]

A study of ninety-four dogs with ninety-eight cutaneous soft-tissue sarcomas compared three margin categories. At three years, estimated cumulative local recurrence was 41.2% with infiltrated margins, 22.5% with tumor-free but close margins, and 7.1% with tumor-free margins. These are follow-up estimates, not simple fractions of all enrolled dogs, and four dogs had two tumors. The findings show why collapsing every tumor-free boundary into one category loses information. Conversely, an infiltrated margin did not mean that every tumor recurred. The veterinarian combines the microscopic measurement with grade, location, other treatment and the feasibility and burden of further intervention for that animal. [22]

## Record the outcome as well as the diagnosis

In a separate selected series, thirty-three dogs underwent scar revision after a narrowly or incompletely excised soft-tissue sarcoma, despite having no visible residual tumor. One dog developed local recurrence; median follow-up was 1,127 days, with a wide range of one to 3,192 days. That recurrence occurred 201 days after revision, although no residual tumor had been identified in the revision specimen. The result supports discussing further assessment after an unplanned excision while retaining uncertainty: the study was not a randomized comparison with observation alone. Neither an apparently empty scar specimen nor a favorable group recurrence rate guarantees freedom from later disease in an individual dog. [23]

A nasal-biopsy series included sixteen dogs and fourteen cats. Computed tomography, or CT, helped guide sampling; twenty-eight of thirty samples were diagnostically adequate, and twenty-six yielded clinically relevant diagnoses. Eleven animals had minor immediate nasal bleeding. One dog required readmission for increased bleeding the next day; bleeding settled during observation but recurred intermittently after discharge. That dog had a malignant neoplasm, and additional immunostaining was not pursued. This individual outcome matters alongside the diagnostic-yield figures. A useful tissue diagnosis does not make the procedure risk-free or settle subsequent treatment, and continuing bleeding or clinical deterioration requires reassessment rather than waiting passively for a final label. [24]

## Forensic meaning and the next clinical decision

In a welfare or forensic investigation, histology sits within a broader record. Liverpool's forensic pathology service describes examination of the major organs, photographs, continuity of specimen custody and additional testing when indicated. Continuity of custody records who handled the evidence and helps establish that the examined material belongs to the identified animal. Microscopy may support disease, tissue injury or a biological explanation for wasting, but an isolated lesion does not establish neglect, the precise time of an event or a responsible person. Those conclusions require the clinical history, scene and husbandry information, competing explanations, and a clearly stated account of what the specimen could not preserve or reveal. [25]

The final report should be read with the question that prompted sampling. Does it identify a specific disease, describe a pattern with several possible causes, or explain why the specimen was nondiagnostic? Michigan State University's biopsy guidance encourages direct discussion with the pathologist and accepts existing slides or paraffin blocks for consultation and additional testing. A mismatch between the animal and the report can justify review of deeper sections, another stain or a more representative sample; it does not automatically make either observer wrong. The useful endpoint is a documented next decision tied to the animal's condition, with uncertainty and follow-up recorded alongside the microscopic findings. [26]

# V020: Veterinary bone marrow examination

## Marrow examination asks why the blood is abnormal

Bone marrow is the tissue that produces blood cells inside bone. Examining it can help distinguish inadequate production from destruction, loss or abnormal movement of cells elsewhere in the body. Cornell's veterinary guidance lists persistent unexplained anemia, low counts affecting several cell lines, atypical circulating cells and suspected infiltrative disease among the indications. Anemia means too few circulating red cells or too little oxygen-carrying hemoglobin; neutropenia means too few neutrophils, important infection-fighting white cells; thrombocytopenia means too few platelets. A marrow examination is selected to answer a clinical question, not automatically ordered for every slightly abnormal blood count. Other causes and the pattern over time remain relevant. [1]

The animal's immediate condition takes priority over obtaining a complete marrow label. Severe anemia can cause pale tissues, weakness, a rapid heartbeat and circulatory compromise; a dog or cat that is collapsing or struggling to breathe needs prompt veterinary assessment. Merck's anemia guidance distinguishes a regenerative response, in which the marrow releases more young red cells, from an inadequate response. Reticulocytes are those young red cells. A low response shortly after a new problem is not interpreted in the same way as persistent failure to respond. Stabilization and investigation can proceed together; a proposed sampling date or expected laboratory turnaround is not a reason to postpone care for deterioration. [2]

## A good submission includes blood as well as marrow

A complete blood count, or CBC, measures the circulating red cells, white cells and platelets. A blood smear adds direct inspection of their appearance. Minnesota's clinical pathology laboratory requests current blood information together with marrow aspirate and core material because these specimens answer complementary questions. Hemodilution means that ordinary blood has diluted the aspirate; an impressive-looking red sample can contain very little representative marrow. Low cellularity can likewise mean either genuinely sparse marrow or poor sampling. The laboratory should state when sample quality limits interpretation. A nondiagnostic aspirate does not establish normal marrow and should not be converted into a reassuring negative result. Cytology slides stay separate from formalin-preserved cores because formalin fumes can alter staining and cell appearance. [3]

An aspirate contains individual cells and small marrow particles spread on slides, making cell detail and maturation easier to examine. A core biopsy preserves a narrow piece of tissue, showing how blood-forming cells, fat, supporting fibers and infiltrating cells are arranged. Byers' feline review describes Romanowsky-type stains for aspirate slides, which reveal individual cell detail. Formalin-preserved cores are processed for microscopic tissue examination, retaining the relationships among cells, fat and supporting tissue. Both preparations are useful: a cell-rich aspirate does not replace architecture, and a core does not show every cellular feature equally well. Sampling is a veterinary procedure requiring pain management and an individualized sedation or anesthesia plan. The choice considers the cat's condition and the intended diagnostic benefit. These explanations concern what the specimens mean, not instructions for an owner to attempt collection. [4]

Preservation is part of the measurement. Material intended for cytology must retain cells that can be spread and stained, while a core intended for histology is preserved differently. Iowa State's specimen guide emphasizes correct containers, individual animal identification, specimen source, collection time and a complete history. The submitting practice coordinates these requirements with the receiving laboratory. Treatments, recent transfusion and the timing of earlier blood counts belong in that history because the pathologist is interpreting a changing patient. A well-labeled series can show progression or recovery; a collection of slides separated from the dates and animal identity cannot answer the same question reliably. [5]

Texas A&M's 2026 marrow guidance makes a central distinction: production can be effective or ineffective. If a blood-cell population is depleted but the marrow expands the appropriate precursor line and the blood later recovers, the response is functioning. If the marrow contains many red-cell precursors while anemia remains nonregenerative, cells may be failing to mature or surviving poorly before release. Cell abundance therefore is not the same thing as useful output. The laboratory needs a contemporaneous CBC and previous counts to judge that relationship. Medication exposures, imaging and current illness also help explain whether the marrow is responding to another disease or is itself the main site of injury. [6]

## Read cellularity and ratios as relationships

A dry tap yields little or no useful aspirate. Dense fibrosis, infiltration or technical factors can contribute, so failed retrieval is not itself a diagnosis and a core may be needed. Cellularity describes the proportion of blood-forming tissue relative to fat and other components. It depends on age, site and specimen quality; younger animals generally have more active marrow. Cornell recommends interpreting the myeloid-to-erythroid ratio, written M:E, alongside cellularity and the CBC. Myeloid here includes granulocyte and monocyte precursors, while erythroid refers to nucleated red-cell precursors. A high ratio can arise from increased myeloid cells, decreased erythroid cells, or both. A ratio within an expected range can conceal proportional loss of both populations. Consequently, the report should explain which line is abnormal and why; the ratio alone is not a diagnosis of infection, cancer or marrow failure. [7]

Lineage means a family of developing blood cells. Red-cell precursors progressively mature toward oxygen-carrying erythrocytes. Neutrophil precursors progress toward mature infection-fighting cells, while megakaryocytes are large marrow cells that generate platelets. Cornell's marrow biology descriptions emphasize orderly maturation, with earlier cells normally less abundant than later stages. The pathologist looks for the sequence and proportions, not merely a combined cell count. Supporting cells and the surrounding microenvironment also matter. A marrow that contains an appropriate total number of cells may still have an abnormal mixture, a missing later stage, or replacement of useful blood-forming tissue by a different population. [8]

## Descriptive abnormalities are not complete explanations

Hyperplasia means expansion of a cell population; hypoplasia means reduction, and aplasia indicates profound depletion. These describe what is seen without necessarily identifying the cause. Dysplasia means abnormal cell appearance or maturation. Cornell notes that dysplasia can accompany a primary neoplastic marrow disorder, but also medication effects, nutritional deficiency and accelerated production. Likewise, 'maturation arrest' usually describes a shortage of later stages rather than proving that every cell has literally stopped at one developmental checkpoint. Distinguishing reactive change from a myelodysplastic neoplasm requires the blood findings, persistence, degree of abnormality and other evidence. One unusual-looking cell should not be promoted into a cancer diagnosis. [9]

Iron provides a useful example of a species boundary. Special staining can reveal stored hemosiderin, one form of iron storage, in a suitable canine marrow sample. Yet normal cats generally lack readily stainable marrow iron. Cornell therefore cautions that a feline aspirate cannot be used to assess iron stores by simply applying the canine expectation. The staining reaction does not detect every form in which iron is stored. Even in dogs, the assessment is subjective and depends on representative particles containing storage cells. An absent stain in a poorly sampled specimen does not have the same evidential value as a technically adequate assessment in the appropriate species. [10]

## Platelet production and sampling difficulty need careful interpretation

The number of megakaryocytes cannot independently diagnose immune thrombocytopenia, in which immune mechanisms contribute to low platelet counts. The American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine's 2024 consensus found insufficient evidence that routine marrow examination improves diagnosis of primary immune thrombocytopenia in dogs, and does not recommend it as a routine diagnostic or prognostic test for that condition. Marrow assessment can be useful when additional unexplained cytopenias raise a different question. Increased, normal or reduced megakaryocyte numbers have all been described in affected dogs. Thus, an apparently preserved platelet-producing population does not settle every mechanism, while a reduced population is not an automatic prediction of treatment failure. [11]

A 2026 study examined cores from sixteen dogs with hematologic disease and found greater diagnostic capture when two sites were considered rather than one. Under its permissive interpretation rule, the estimate rose from 76.6% to 94.8%. However, the reference was a composite of the same study's readings, not an external gold standard. These figures cannot be read as universal clinical sensitivity or proof that every animal requires the same sampling plan. [12]

## New sampling evidence must retain its reference standard

A companion 2026 analysis of aspirates from the same sixteen-dog study also found important variation between sites and readers. Single-site interpretations matched the study's composite reference in 50.0% to 81.7%, depending on how strictly agreement was defined. A second site improved that probability; no one anatomic site was uniformly superior. These are related analyses, not two independent replications in different populations. Nondiagnostic samples were excluded from the main concordance analysis, another reason not to equate the figures with performance in every clinical submission. The useful lesson is that persistent disagreement between the blood, clinical picture and one specimen can justify reconsidering how representative that specimen was. [13]

In precursor-targeted immune-mediated anemia, abbreviated PIMA, red-cell production can be ineffective because developing cells are being lost within marrow. A study of sixty-six dogs found macrophages engulfing erythroid precursors in sixty-one; this observation is called rubriphagocytosis. Core biopsies showed collagen fibrosis in thirty-one of sixty-three evaluated dogs. Fifty-five dogs developed a regenerative response, at a median of twenty-nine days, and forty of those reached remission, at a median of fifty-nine days after diagnosis. Nine had confirmed thromboembolic events, blood clots that affected outcome. This selected treated cohort illustrates why a nonregenerative blood count and an active marrow can coexist; response, remission and complication-free recovery remain different endpoints. [14]

## Document both recovery and the burden of treatment

A later canine PIMA study separated its populations carefully. Twenty-seven dogs receiving prolonged immunosuppression were included in the response analysis: eighteen responded and nine did not. Sixteen responders improved within sixty days, while two responded at ninety-three and 126 days. Complications were reviewed in a broader group of fifty dogs, including pancreatitis, pneumonia and abscesses. These populations must not be merged into one response denominator. Selection for continued treatment also limits what the findings predict for newly diagnosed dogs. Delayed responses can occur, but the study is not permission to wait through deterioration. Repeated assessment is needed to judge ongoing transfusion needs, adverse effects and whether the original explanation still fits. [15]

In fifteen cats with nonregenerative immune-mediated anemia, seven were classified as having pure red-cell aplasia and eight as another nonregenerative immune-mediated pattern. Fourteen required transfusion support, and one developed congestive heart failure associated with that support. Eleven achieved remission twelve to forty-two days after immunosuppressive treatment began. Three of those eleven relapsed, and eight were still receiving treatment at follow-up. The distinction between marked absence of erythroid precursors and ineffective production was informative, but neither pattern guaranteed a simple course. This small retrospective feline series supports a guarded discussion of recovery and monitoring; remission in the blood count was not equivalent to a demonstrated permanent, treatment-free cure. [16]

## A sparse marrow can have a reversible cause

Stanley and Eatroff reported a male domestic shorthair cat with chronic weight loss, reduced appetite and lethargy. Blood testing showed nonregenerative anemia, neutropenia and thrombocytopenia; a core biopsy showed hypocellular marrow consistent with production failure. The cat also had a low cobalamin concentration. Cobalamin is vitamin B12, needed for normal cellular function. After veterinary investigation and supplementation, all three blood-cell counts normalized within two months. The published abstract does not provide longer follow-up. This is a single documented case, not a frequency estimate or a reason to assume every pancytopenic cat needs the same treatment. It demonstrates why finding marrow failure should lead to investigation of its cause rather than ending the diagnostic process. [17]

Fibrosis also should be demonstrated rather than presumed. Investigators examined core biopsies from ten dogs with Ehrlichia canis-associated aplastic pancytopenia, using special stains for collagen and reticulin, two supporting fiber components. They found no significant deposition of either in the samples. Seven dogs had depleted stainable hemosiderin. The findings argued against fibrosis being the main explanation for marrow failure in that particular group. The original abstract did not report treatment outcomes or survival, so those cannot be supplied from the stain results. A core showing profound suppression answers a different question from a core showing replacement by fibers, even when both animals have very low circulating counts. [18]

## Infectious investigations depend on what each assay detects

Marrow can contain infectious organisms as well as injured or abnormal blood-forming cells. MSD's histoplasmosis guidance notes that cytology may reveal the yeast in affected tissues and that marrow cytology can be diagnostic in cats. Histoplasma is an environmental fungus that can spread beyond the respiratory tract. Identifying convincing organisms within an inflammatory process can directly change the diagnostic interpretation, but not every pale or nonregenerative cat has fungal disease. Distribution and organism burden influence which tissue yields useful evidence. The veterinary team selects cytology, tissue examination, antigen testing or culture according to the case and laboratory requirements; a negative-looking fragment is not a survey of the whole animal. [19]

Feline leukemia virus, or FeLV, can contribute to marrow dysfunction, but its name should not make every marrow abnormality a viral diagnosis. The 2025 Australian and New Zealand guideline distinguishes antigen testing, which detects viral protein, from polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, which can detect proviral DNA integrated into the cat's genome. Blood and, in selected situations, marrow may be tested. Discordant findings require interpretation within the infection stage and assay used. The guideline also retains other contributors to feline anemia, including inflammation and concurrent infection. A positive viral result needs appropriate confirmation and clinical correlation, while a negative screening result does not rule out unrelated marrow neoplasia. [20]

## An individually documented leukemia case shows the value and limits of classification

Tagawa and colleagues described a twelve-year-old female domestic shorthair cat with weight loss, poor appetite and rapid breathing. Blood contained severe anemia, thrombocytopenia and many immature blast cells. Marrow cytology and cytochemical stains, which test cellular enzyme reactions, supported acute monoblastic leukemia, a cancer of immature monocyte-lineage cells. Blood antigen testing and marrow proviral testing for FeLV were negative. Despite chemotherapy and transfusions, the anemia remained transfusion-dependent. Further treatment was declined after two months, and the cat died several days later. The report demonstrates a specific diagnostic pathway and an unfavorable outcome; negative FeLV testing did not exclude leukemia, and correct cellular classification did not ensure treatment success. [21]

The 2021 UK pancytopenia investigation combined laboratory pathology with wider exposure evidence. Its surveillance analysis included 580 cats: 367 died, 210 were alive at follow-up, and three outcomes were missing. Marrow was examined in fifty-six, rather than all 580; most sampled marrows were hypo- or aplastic. Dietary histories and analysis of recalled foods supported a mycotoxin hypothesis, with important causal limitations acknowledged by the authors. Mycotoxins are toxins produced by molds. This surveillance cohort is distinct from a separate fifty-cat detailed report from the outbreak and should not be treated as an independent replication of it. Neither marrow depletion alone nor one exposure history proves a particular toxin, product fault or responsibility. [22]

## Translate a marrow description into an accountable clinical interpretation

When many blast cells are present, their appearance may establish suspicion of acute leukemia without identifying the exact lineage. Cornell describes the role of immunophenotyping, testing proteins associated with cell identity, and cytochemical staining. Flow cytometry measures selected markers on suspended cells; immunocytochemistry applies antibody-based methods to cells on slides. These methods complement morphology rather than replace a representative specimen. A leukemia arising in marrow also differs conceptually from lymphoma cells secondarily involving marrow. The distinction depends on the distribution of disease and the full clinical findings, not simply whether one abnormal cell was seen in blood. Diagnostic terminology should reflect the evidence actually available. [23]

The most useful final interpretation separates sample adequacy, observed abnormalities, the likely explanation and remaining alternatives. Messick's veterinary marrow primer emphasizes combining clinical history, blood findings, marrow morphology and ancillary data, with the clinician as an essential link. Follow-up therefore asks whether the circulating counts and the animal's condition improve as expected, and whether persistent or new findings require a revised explanation. A first result may justify further investigation rather than a final disease label. In a welfare or mortality review, similarly, describing marrow injury is different from demonstrating its cause. The record should retain timing, exposures, treatment, missing tests and the reported outcome without turning an isolated microscopic pattern into unsupported certainty. [24]

# V032: Veterinary skin scraping and surface cytology

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Pathology and dermatology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *{'text': 'A few cells or mites can redirect an itchy animal’s care. The sample’s site and depth determine what the microscope can reveal—and what it can miss.', 'sources': [1]}*

## Start with the animal, then examine the slide

Skin scraping collects material for microscopic examination, often to look for mites. Surface cytology collects cells and associated material to investigate inflammation, bacteria, yeasts or abnormal tissue cells. In dogs and cats, many different diseases cause similar redness, crusts, hair loss or itching, so neither test replaces a history and full examination. The veterinarian asks where the problem began, whether itching preceded the lesions, which treatments were used, whether other animals are affected, and whether there has been travel or recent bathing. These details help select a useful lesion and explain unexpected findings. A photograph of a rash may identify a pattern, but it cannot show whether mites or intracellular bacteria are present. [1]

## Basic tests come before an allergy label

The American Animal Hospital Association’s 2023 guidance recommends a minimum dermatologic database: a basic set of observations and samples, including flea combing, skin cytology and appropriate scrapings, with ear cytology when ear disease is present. The selection in cats is guided by the pattern of lesions as well as the history. Parasites, secondary bacterial infection and yeast disease can produce or intensify itching, including in an animal that also has an allergy. Finding and managing them helps clarify what remains. A skin scraping is not an allergy test, and a negative scraping does not establish allergy. Rechecking after initial care is part of the diagnostic process because different causes can coexist or become apparent over time. [2]

## Tape, impressions and swabs collect different material

An impression preparation transfers material from an accessible moist or draining lesion onto a glass slide. A swab can transfer discharge from a fold or other moist site to a slide. These approaches are usually quick, but their usefulness depends on the material that actually reaches the microscope. An impression of an ulcer surface may show inflammation and surface microbes while missing the deeper cause. The veterinary team chooses the method to match the lesion rather than assuming that every sample called “cytology” is equivalent. [3]

Adhesive tape collects superficial material such as scales, cells and organisms. It can help recover some surface-dwelling mites and is a different specimen from a deep scraping. The target organism’s location therefore matters when choosing between tape, hair examination and scraping. [4]

## Depth follows the suspected parasite’s habitat

The European Scientific Counsel Companion Animal Parasites, abbreviated ESCCAP, distinguishes surface sampling from methods that reach hair follicles. The epidermis is the outer skin layer; its outermost keratin layer contains flattened, dead cells. Some mites occupy this surface region, while follicular Demodex mites live deeper around hair roots and associated glands. A superficial sample suited to a surface-dwelling mite may therefore miss follicular disease. A trichogram, meaning microscopic examination of collected hairs and their roots, can provide another route to follicular material in selected sites. Sampling is a professional procedure with depth and location chosen for the animal and suspected disease; the explanation is not a recommendation for owners to scrape irritated skin at home. [4]

## Preparation errors can hide or imitate findings

A useful cytology sample needs enough well-preserved material spread so individual cells can be assessed. Thick debris, cell rupture, poor drying or staining problems can obscure the very structures being sought. Routine stains colour nuclei and cytoplasm differently, making cell types and small organisms easier to recognize. Cornell University’s eClinpath guidance also stresses the vulnerability of slides to formalin fumes: slides submitted near a formalin-preserved biopsy can acquire staining artifacts and become difficult to interpret. An impression can yield only surface contamination rather than representative lesional tissue. The receiving laboratory needs the animal’s history and precise sampling site, because a technically readable slide can still answer the wrong clinical question if its origin is unclear. [5]

## Inflammatory cells are clues rather than a complete diagnosis

Neutrophils are white blood cells commonly recruited to acute inflammation and many bacterial infections. Macrophages are cells that engulf material and can become prominent in longer-standing inflammation or responses to foreign material and some infections. Eosinophils can occur with parasites and hypersensitivity, among other causes. Their relative abundance helps describe a reaction pattern but does not uniquely identify its cause. Even neutrophils that look swollen or damaged are not absolute proof of bacterial infection: preservation artifacts, fungi and chemical irritation can produce overlapping changes. Conversely, neutrophils that look relatively normal do not exclude bacteria. The observer searches for organisms, their relationship to cells and the wider pattern before attaching a disease name to the sample. [6]

## Bacteria: being present and causing pyoderma are different

Pyoderma means bacterial skin infection. The International Society for Companion Animal Infectious Diseases, or ISCAID, recommends cytology in suspected canine pyoderma. Cocci are round bacteria and rods are elongated bacteria; these shapes guide interpretation but do not name a species. Bacteria engulfed within inflammatory cells provide stronger support for infection than a few organisms lying outside cells on otherwise healthy skin. Extracellular bacteria accompanied by damaged-cell material can also be important, and substantial bacterial overgrowth on an inflamed lesion may occur without obvious inflammatory cells. No universal bacterial-count threshold has been validated for every site and method. A negative surface preparation also has limited ability to rule out infection deeper in the skin. [7]

## Microscopy does not supply an antibiotic susceptibility result

Round bacteria on a slide cannot distinguish a susceptible staphylococcus from a resistant one. Culture identifies organisms capable of growing under the laboratory’s conditions, while antimicrobial susceptibility testing assesses their response to selected drugs. These results require interpretation alongside the lesion and cytology because colonizing bacteria may also grow. The World Association for Veterinary Dermatology’s guidance on meticillin-resistant staphylococci distinguishes colonization from infection and emphasizes reliable species identification and resistance testing. Detecting a resistant organism does not by itself mean that disease is more severe or untreatable. For deep or persistent disease, the veterinarian chooses a specimen that represents the affected tissue; a casual surface swab can misdirect treatment toward an incidental organism. [8]

## Yeast counts belong in clinical context

Malassezia yeasts, often recognizable as small oval or budding cells, can inhabit healthy canine and feline skin. They can also overgrow or contribute to inflammation when the skin environment changes. The World Association for Veterinary Dermatology, abbreviated WAVD, recommends interpreting lesional cytology together with compatible signs and response to appropriate care. Anatomical site, breed, sampling technique and the animal’s immune response affect the meaning of a count. Some sensitive animals can have important disease with relatively few yeasts, while detecting yeasts alone does not establish dermatitis. A report should therefore describe the method, approximate abundance and inflammatory context. The aim is to explain the animal’s sore or itchy skin rather than to sterilize a normally inhabited surface. [9]

## A thirty-dog study shows why the method changes the count

Rich and colleagues compared three cytology collection techniques in thirty client-owned dogs with atopic dermatitis and lesions consistent with bacterial or Malassezia skin disease. In the blinded comparison, a preparation suspending collected surface debris yielded an average of 12.7 more bacteria per evaluated sample than impression smears and 13.5 more than tape preparations. Tape detected slightly more Malassezia than the suspension method, averaging 0.22 additional organisms per sample. These were method-comparison outcomes, not evidence that one technique cured disease or was best for every lesion. The studied dogs were a selected clinical population. The practical implication is that an apparent change in microbial burden can partly reflect how material was collected and counted. [10]

## Demodex in dogs and cats: the species distinction matters

The Companion Animal Parasite Council, CAPC, describes different Demodex species and habitats. Canine Demodex canis inhabits hair follicles; excessive proliferation is associated with demodicosis. In cats, Demodex cati also occupies follicles, whereas Demodex gatoi lives superficially and can be contagious between cats. Grooming can remove surface mites, making them difficult to recover even from an itchy cat. Deep scrapings, hair examination and tape preparations therefore have different roles depending on the suspected species. In occasional difficult cases, a biopsy reveals mites despite negative earlier samples. Finding a mite can establish an important part of the diagnosis, but its species and location matter; the label “Demodex positive” alone leaves clinically relevant information unresolved. [11]

## The condition that allowed mites to multiply may need investigation

WAVD’s 2020 demodicosis consensus recommends looking for predisposing conditions when disease begins in adult dogs or cats. Immune suppression can arise from illness or medication, and an underlying cause is not always found even after investigation. The examination should therefore extend beyond the visible patches when the history warrants it. Improvement is assessed both clinically and through repeat sampling: hair regrowth or reduced redness can precede elimination of the abnormal mite population. Rechecks compare the same previously affected areas and the numbers and developmental stages recovered. A negative sample is valuable evidence of response, but its interpretation depends on the sites sampled, the method and previous findings rather than appearance alone. [12]

## Eight young dogs: early negative hair samples missed remaining mites

A 2019 pilot study by Barillas and colleagues followed eight dogs with juvenile generalized demodicosis. It compared deep skin scraping, microscopic hair examination and a veterinary tape-impression technique that also obtained follicular material. Hair examination recovered fewer mites during much of treatment. At week two, three dogs had negative hair examinations while mites remained detectable by the other techniques. By week four, two dogs were negative on scraping but positive on tape, while one showed the reverse mismatch. From week five to the study’s end, all techniques were negative for mite life stages. The endpoint was microscopic monitoring during that small study, not proof of lifelong cure. It demonstrates why one negative method should not prematurely end follow-up. [13]

## Two Finnish cats: diagnosis and follow-up stayed household-specific

Saari and colleagues reported Demodex gatoi in ten cats from six Finnish households containing twenty-one cats altogether. In household three, a two-year-old Exotic male and a three-year-old neutered Persian male had itching, hair loss and scaling, with mites recovered from superficial samples. Initial regimens were unsuccessful; a later veterinarian-directed regimen was rated effective using clinical examination and scraping, with twenty-four months of follow-up recorded. The historical treatment details are not current prescribing instructions. In a different household, all six cats remained symptomless despite mites being found on one cat. These observations separate an identified parasite from the severity of disease in every contact animal. Household examination and response helped interpretation; a positive cat did not establish when or from whom transmission occurred. [14]

## Sarcoptic mange can remain plausible after negative scrapings

Sarcoptes mites cause canine scabies, an often intensely itchy, contagious infestation. Characteristic distribution, possible exposure and itching in contact animals can support suspicion, but allergic and other skin diseases can resemble it. Merck Veterinary Manual describes the difficulty of confirming some cases because scrapings can be negative. The veterinarian assesses the whole pattern rather than interpreting “no mites seen” as a universal exclusion. When suspicion remains high, further sampling or a professionally supervised diagnostic treatment trial may be appropriate. That trial still requires clinical reassessment: improvement can be influenced by concurrent infections and other treatment. People with an associated rash should seek their own medical advice instead of using an animal’s parasiticide. [15]

## Scale on the coat can require a different search

Cheyletiella mites are associated with scaling often most apparent along the back, while Lynxacarus mites in cats cling to hair shafts. These nonburrowing parasites may be recovered through combinations of coat examination, combed debris, tape or microscopic hair assessment. Their eggs and attachment patterns can also help identification. A laboratory finding of “scale only” does not automatically exclude them if the sampled area or technique was unsuitable. CAPC notes that combining appropriate techniques can improve recovery. The appearance commonly called walking dandruff is a clue to investigate, not a reliable visual diagnosis for an owner. Finding a coat parasite also does not rule out a separate bacterial or yeast complication on damaged skin. [16]

## Mange findings do not establish neglect or a transmission history

Feline scabies caused by Notoedres cati commonly begins around the head and ears and can produce marked crusting and itching. CAPC describes confirmation by identifying the mite in skin scrapings and notes that grooming can also lead to mites being recovered on fecal examination. These are findings about an infestation, rather than a complete history of how it developed. A scraping can document organisms at a sampled site; it cannot alone establish how long an animal was affected, who transmitted them, what care was previously attempted, or whether a person neglected the animal. A welfare investigation needs the clinical course, physical condition, records and circumstances alongside the test. Visible suffering requires care while those questions are investigated. [17]

## A thirteen-dog study: DNA remained detectable after other findings fell

Díaz and colleagues studied thirteen dogs: eleven with Malassezia-associated paw inflammation and two healthy controls. Multiple sites produced fifteen pretreatment diseased samples, three healthy samples and four post-treatment samples from three affected dogs. Thus twenty-two samples did not represent twenty-two independent animals. The diseased samples had higher yeast counts and culture growth. After antifungal treatment, the four repeat samples showed very low or absent yeasts by cytology and no culture growth, while yeast DNA remained detectable by quantitative polymerase chain reaction, or qPCR. This small study compared measurement methods and selected repeat samples; it did not establish a universal molecular threshold for cure. A DNA signal alone could not demonstrate ongoing clinically important dermatitis. [18]

## Detached skin cells can suggest an immune-mediated disease

Keratinocytes are the main cells of the epidermis. Acantholysis means that these cells have lost their normal attachments and rounded up separately. Seeing numerous acantholytic cells with inflammation can suggest pemphigus foliaceus, an immune-mediated blistering and crusting disease. However, the finding is not uniquely diagnostic: some bacterial or fungal pustular diseases can produce overlapping changes. Guidance presented at the World Small Animal Veterinary Association congress emphasizes this distinction. The veterinarian considers fresh lesions, organisms, response to previous care and, when needed, biopsy before prescribing immune-suppressing treatment. Suppressing the immune system because a slide merely resembles pemphigus could worsen an unrecognized infection. A descriptive cytology finding should therefore remain separate from the final disease diagnosis. [19]

## Thirty-five cats with pemphigus: control did not mean permanent cure

Bizikova and Burrows reported an original series of thirty-five cats with pemphigus foliaceus, separately from their literature review. All thirty-five had acantholysis confirmed by cytology, and twenty-four also had histological confirmation. Disease control was achieved in thirty-one, with a median time of twenty-two days; control meant that new lesions stopped forming and existing active lesions largely or completely healed. Flare-ups occurred in twenty-four of those thirty-one cats. The original cohort’s median follow-up was fifteen months. This outcome supports a treatable disease while showing why a one-time improvement or slide cannot establish permanent recovery. The report’s original thirty-five-cat outcomes should not be merged with its larger reviewed collection of previously published cats. [20]

## When surface samples cannot answer the question

A skin biopsy preserves tissue architecture: the arrangement of layers, follicles, glands and inflammatory changes. Texas A&M’s dermatology service describes its use after initial tests when immune-mediated disease, follicular disorders, deep infection or cancer remain concerns. Several lesions may need sampling because an old ulcer and a fresh lesion can reveal different information. Local anaesthesia, sedation or general anaesthesia may be appropriate depending on the site and animal, with pain and anxiety addressed explicitly. A superficial sample showing only inflammation is not necessarily a failed test; it may correctly reveal that the surface cannot distinguish among deeper causes. Persistent, unusual or worsening lesions call for reassessment rather than repeating the same inadequate specimen indefinitely. [21]

## A negative routine slide does not exclude ringworm

Dermatophytosis, often called ringworm, is fungal infection of keratinized tissue such as hair and superficial skin. It is different from Malassezia overgrowth, and routine bacterial or yeast cytology is not a complete dermatophyte work-up. The American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals shelter guidance uses the clinical picture together with appropriate Wood’s lamp examination, direct hair assessment, fungal culture or molecular testing. A Wood’s lamp is a specialized ultraviolet examination, not proof that every glowing substance is infected hair. In shelters, the distinction matters because unnecessary isolation consumes care capacity while missed infection can spread. The veterinarian chooses additional fungal testing when the lesion pattern and exposure warrant it, despite an unrevealing routine surface slide. [22]

## Painful deep disease changes the urgency

Deep pyoderma can produce painful swelling, ulceration, crusts and draining tracts releasing blood or pus. These lesions deserve prompt veterinary attention, particularly if the animal is also lethargic, feverish, eating poorly or deteriorating. MSD Veterinary Manual distinguishes deep disease from surface and superficial infection and notes that recurrent deep disease in cats can accompany systemic illness or unusual infections. Surface microscopy alone cannot establish the full extent of such disease. The immediate priorities are comfort, assessment of the animal’s overall condition, and representative diagnostic material. A report of a few surface bacteria should not delay care for a painful deep lesion, and persistent redness should not automatically trigger repeated antibiotics without reviewing the underlying diagnosis. [23]

## Make the result reproducible and useful at the next visit

Record species, sampling site, lesion type, previous treatment and response. Include clinical context and previous results, so later clinicians can interpret the sampled finding. [24]

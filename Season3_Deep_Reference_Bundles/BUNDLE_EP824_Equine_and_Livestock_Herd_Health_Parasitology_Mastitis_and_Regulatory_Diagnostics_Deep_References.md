# V003: Fecal flotation in dogs and cats

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Dog, Cat
- **Clinical Category**: Parasitology
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft on 2026-11-05; no independent clinical review.

**Common Aliases**: Faecal flotation, Centrifugal fecal flotation, Qualitative fecal examination

## Plain-Language Overview

The laboratory separates certain parasite stages from stool so they are easier to see. A report saying none were detected describes this sample and method; it does not prove the animal has no parasites.

## Clinical Purpose

Looks for parasite eggs, cysts, or other stages in stool. It supports assessment of intestinal parasites in animals with digestive signs and in preventive care.

## How the Test Works

Flotation relies on density differences. Appropriate eggs, cysts, and oocysts rise in a denser solution while much fecal debris separates away. A coverslip collects material for microscopy. Centrifugation improves recovery, but flotation media and processing choices affect which stages remain recognizable.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

A laboratory processes feces with a flotation solution and examines recovered material microscopically. Centrifugation concentrates parasite stages. The solution and method influence what can be detected.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Collect a fresh, individually identified sample in a leakproof container and ask the clinic about amount, refrigeration, and delivery. Report recent antiparasitic treatment and relevant symptoms.

## Interpreting Results

A qualitative report identifies detected parasites or states that none were detected. This differs from a quantitative fecal egg count, which estimates the number of detected stages per gram.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Low numbers or sporadic shedding can prevent detection. Poor storage can allow eggs to hatch or fragile stages to deteriorate. Some parasite stages cannot be reliably identified to species by appearance alone. When clinical suspicion persists, the veterinarian selects repeat samples or a complementary method matched to the suspected organism; a universal parasite panel is not implied.

## Limitations & Confounders

A single negative result cannot exclude all parasites. Organisms can be absent from the collected portion or shed inconsistently. For suspected Giardia, the veterinarian may combine flotation with other tests, such as fecal antigen testing or PCR, a test that looks for the parasite's genetic material.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Collection of naturally passed stool has little physical burden. Feces can contain infectious material, so use the clinic's handling instructions.

## Species & Population Notes

This entry addresses dogs and cats; parasite populations and suitable complementary tests depend on host species and exposure.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Document flotation method and media. A qualitative flotation does not quantify adult worm burden, and its result must be interpreted with clinical findings.

# V005: Equine infectious anemia antibody testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Horse
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and regulatory testing
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft on 2026-11-05; no independent clinical review. Regulatory requirements need jurisdiction-specific confirmation.

**Common Aliases**: EIA test, Coggins test, AGID, EIA ELISA

## Plain-Language Overview

Often called a Coggins test, this blood test looks for the horse's antibody response to a particular virus. The name is sometimes used loosely, so the actual laboratory method should always be recorded.

## Clinical Purpose

Detects antibodies to equine infectious anemia virus. Testing supports infection control and official movement or event requirements, which depend on the jurisdiction.

## How the Test Works

AGID detects a visible reaction as antibody and antigen diffuse through a gel. ELISA uses an enzyme-linked detection system to reveal antibody binding. Both assess immune evidence of infection; they do not directly measure viral quantity in blood.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

Blood is collected for an approved antibody assay. Agar gel immunodiffusion, or AGID, is the original Coggins method; ELISA is another method. Official US testing uses authorized submissions and APHIS-approved laboratories.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Arrange testing early enough for laboratory processing and required documents. Supply accurate animal identification. The veterinarian and laboratory determine acceptable specimen handling and the current official form.

## Interpreting Results

The report states the assay and result. Reactive ELISA findings require the laboratory's official follow-up pathway; AGID is used for confirmation. A positive antibody result is not a measurement of current clinical severity.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

Serology means testing serum for an immune response. Recently infected horses can test negative before antibodies develop, so exposure timing matters. A non-negative laboratory result follows an official confirmation process. Specimen acceptance is assay-specific: serum and plasma are not automatically interchangeable. Interpretation must retain the collection date, not only the date the certificate was issued.

## Limitations & Confounders

Early after infection, antibodies may not yet be detectable. Test validity depends on the assay's approved specimen type and handling. A historical negative test does not cover later exposure.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Blood sampling involves needle discomfort and possible bruising. Official positive results have regulatory consequences managed by animal-health authorities.

## Species & Population Notes

This starter entry covers horses. Donkeys and mules need their own species-specific discussion, including antibody-development timing.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Keep assay name, collection date, identification, and jurisdiction with the record. Check current authority and laboratory requirements rather than assuming a universal validity period. Foals can have maternally acquired antibody, so their results need age and dam-status context; a positive antibody result in a young foal is not interpreted identically to an adult result.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

['Official confirmatory EIA antibody testing', 'Regulator-directed repeat testing after possible recent exposure', 'Clinical assessment of an unwell horse']

# V006: Bovine tuberculosis skin testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Cattle
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and regulatory testing
- **Reference Level**: Starter Clinical Brief
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-05
- **Editorial Status**: Source-checked educational draft on 2026-11-05; no independent clinical review. Official sources differ on some follow-up options; jurisdiction-specific review remains required.

**Common Aliases**: Bovine TB testing, Caudal fold test, CFT, Comparative cervical test, CCT

## Plain-Language Overview

The veterinarian checks whether the animal's skin reacts to a test substance. A reaction is a reason for further official investigation, rather than an automatic finding that tuberculosis is present.

## Clinical Purpose

Assesses an immune response to tuberculin as part of bovine tuberculosis surveillance or investigation. The caudal-fold test is a presumptive screening test, rather than definitive proof of infection.

## How the Test Works

Tuberculin contains mycobacterial proteins. A previously sensitized immune system can generate a delayed local inflammatory response. Comparative cervical testing assesses responses to bovine and avian tuberculins to help interpret a suspect animal within the official program.

## Specimen Collection & Procedure

An authorized veterinarian injects tuberculin into skin and later assesses the site. In the US cattle caudal-fold procedure, reading is around 72 hours after injection. Responders need a regulator-directed follow-up pathway, which may include comparative cervical testing.

## Patient / Animal Preparation

Coordinate animal identification, safe restraint, and availability for the scheduled reading. Tell the veterinarian about illness, medications, and recent vaccinations that could affect an immune-response test.

## Interpreting Results

The official record classifies the response under the applicable program. A screening response does not establish that an animal has bovine tuberculosis; further evaluation is required.

## Detailed Clinical Interpretation

The reading requires both inspection and palpation, meaning feeling the injection site. Response size is not a direct measure of infectiousness. Existing skin abnormalities, dosing or injection errors, and missed reading windows can affect validity. Official classifications such as suspect or reactor have program-defined meanings; owners should retain the original official classification and the results of subsequent investigation separately.

## Limitations & Confounders

Other mycobacterial exposure can cause reactions. Technique, tuberculin handling, and animal immune status affect results. A negative screening result cannot be interpreted outside the herd and exposure context.

## Risks & Pre-Analytical Considerations

Restraint and injection carry handling-related risks; local swelling is the response being assessed. Official findings can trigger movement restrictions or other control measures.

## Species & Population Notes

This entry covers cattle. Procedures and reliability differ in bison, cervids, and other animals; do not apply this entry across species.

## Professional & Technical Notes

Jurisdiction-specific review required. APHIS reference material mentions gamma-interferon follow-up, while Michigan guidance reports approval suspension. Confirm the permitted pathway with the responsible regulator.

## Related Diagnostic Tests

['Regulator-directed comparative cervical testing', 'Postmortem examination when officially indicated', 'Histopathology, PCR, or culture under the regulatory diagnostic pathway']

# V039: Equine fecal egg count

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Horse
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and parasitology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Why can a horse shed no detectable parasite eggs yet still have serious parasitic disease?*

## What the number actually measures

An equine fecal egg count, usually shortened to FEC, estimates parasite eggs per gram of manure, or EPG. It is chiefly a tool for monitoring egg shedding and checking the effect of deworming medicines, called anthelmintics. The 2024 guideline from the American Association of Equine Practitioners, or AAEP, emphasizes that the count does not measure the number of worms inside the horse or diagnose the cause of disease. The distinction is central to safe interpretation. This chapter concerns horses. Results and management rules should not automatically be transferred to donkeys, zebras, ruminants or people. [1]

A long Swedish monitoring programme shows why individual testing can help pasture management. Researchers analyzed 43,330 submitted samples from 26,625 horses on 935 premises. In that selected programme, 26 percent of horses accounted for 80 percent of total strongyle egg output. Egg shedding was therefore unevenly distributed: treating every horse as an identical contributor would miss useful information. Yet an adult horse's category was not permanent. Among 1,034 horses examined in three consecutive years, 62 percent stayed in the same low or high category using the study's 200 EPG division. These findings describe a monitored population and its definitions. They do not establish that a fixed minority always sheds the same proportion on every farm. [13]

## From fresh manure to a microscopic estimate

The sample should belong to an identified horse, with its collection time and recent treatment history recorded. Old manure from a shared paddock introduces uncertainty before the microscope is used. Storage can then change recovery. Crawley and colleagues tested horse manure stored under different conditions: counts remained comparatively stable during a week of refrigeration but declined after longer refrigeration, and tested chemical fixatives also reduced recovery. Their findings do not mean every specimen can wait a week without consequence. Arrange prompt delivery and follow the laboratory's requirements. Freezing or adding a preservative without its advice can make results difficult to compare with the validated method or an earlier sample. [2]

In a flotation method, the laboratory mixes measured manure with a liquid that helps parasite eggs separate from heavier material, then examines a known portion. A multiplication factor converts the eggs actually seen into EPG. As a purely hypothetical arithmetic example, four eggs seen with a factor of 50 gives 200 EPG; four eggs seen is not a claim that only four eggs existed in the whole sample. A 2024 comparison of manual and automated methods emphasized that the factor describes theoretical counting resolution, not guaranteed clinical sensitivity. Egg losses during processing still matter. A method reporting increments of 50 cannot reliably distinguish all small differences within those increments. [3]

McMaster chambers, Mini FLOTAC and centrifugation-based approaches use different sample volumes and processing steps. In a Cornell laboratory comparison, researchers used standardized microscopic beads to study recovery and then examined strongyle eggs in samples from 40 horses. Different methods did not become numerically interchangeable merely because all reported EPG. The bead work was a laboratory model, not proof that every type of parasite egg behaves identically. Accuracy describes closeness to the true amount, whereas precision describes agreement among repeated measurements. A consistently repeated underestimate can look precise while still being inaccurate. When comparing results over time, keep the method and laboratory visible alongside the number. [4]

Thorough mixing, or homogenization, is necessary because the tiny portion examined must represent the larger specimen. An experiment comparing equine strongylid homogenization approaches investigated how preparation affected count magnitude and precision. This is a useful reminder that an EPG report is the end of a chain: sample selection, suspension, egg recovery, subsampling, recognition and multiplication. The result is not generated by photographing every egg in an entire bowel movement. Small changes around a management boundary may reflect measurement variation as well as changing biology. A veterinarian can ask the laboratory about repeatability or repeat an appropriate specimen before interpreting an unexpected difference as treatment failure. [5]

## Changing hosts seasons and parasite stages

Repeated counts in naturally infected horses show real variation within a horse as well as between horses. A longitudinal study reported only moderate overall repeatability, rather than perfect constancy. That does not make monitoring pointless: a pattern across appropriately timed samples can be informative even though each value varies. The practical interpretation is to retain the sequence of dates, not just the largest recorded number. A decrease after treatment should be judged with its starting count, method and sampling interval. Two reports from different dates cannot be compared as though they were duplicate measurements of the same material taken at the same moment. [6]

Longitudinal work in five conservation pony populations examined individual shedding consistency, age effects and seasonal changes. It supports considering the host and environment when interpreting a series. Young horses are not simply small versions of established adult low shedders, and local grazing conditions affect exposure to infectious stages. Seasonal differences in egg output also should not be mistaken for a season when microscopy becomes inherently valid or invalid. A low count in winter does not certify that hidden stages are absent. The veterinarian combines repeated measurements with age, grazing history and local parasite biology to decide what monitoring is useful, rather than assigning a lifelong category from a single test. [7]

Adult egg production is only one part of a parasite's life cycle. Routine egg counts miss migrating larvae and encysted larvae of small strongyles, also called cyathostomins, in the intestinal wall. Pinworm eggs are usually deposited around the anus rather than uniformly in manure; tapeworm detection has additional limitations. AAEP also rejects watery diarrheal specimens for quantitative FEC interpretation, although qualitative examinations may still be useful. A sick horse therefore needs clinical assessment even after a low or zero count. New colic, severe diarrhea, weakness or rapid deterioration warrants prompt veterinary contact rather than waiting for a routine monitoring result. [1]

## Species identification changes the question

Strongyle-type eggs look too similar for a routine count to reliably distinguish small strongyles from the more pathogenic large strongyle Strongylus vulgaris. Larval culture allows eggs to develop and a trained observer to identify later stages. A species-specific PCR instead detects selected DNA. Nielsen and colleagues developed a quantitative PCR for S vulgaris and compared it with traditional approaches on Danish farms. This provides identification beyond a generic egg count, but a fecal test still samples material being shed. It does not directly visualize immature parasites migrating within blood vessels, and a negative result cannot answer every question about past infection or tissue damage. [10]

A German method comparison screened samples from 501 horses by S vulgaris PCR and used larval culture on a subset. In 321 directly comparable samples, including follow-up samples, PCR identified eleven positive samples and culture four. Those are sample counts, not eleven independent affected horses. The findings showed that the methods could disagree and that PCR detected more positives in that investigation. They do not mean a PCR result measures the number of worms or proves that a horse's colic is due to this organism. The question asked, the parasite stage and the clinical findings still limit the conclusion. [11]

Pooling manure from several horses can reduce laboratory workload, but it changes the question from an individual's status to detection in the combined material. A study of pooled S vulgaris testing compared PCR with larval culture and found performance depended on how positive material was represented in the pool. Dilution can matter when one horse contributes a small amount of target among several others. A negative pooled result therefore does not provide the same evidence as suitable individual testing of every horse. Keep the identities and proportions in a pool documented. If an individual clinical or movement decision is required, the veterinarian should choose a sampling plan that actually resolves that individual question. [12]

A Swedish hospital case-control study compared parasite investigations in horses with and without gastrointestinal disease. S vulgaris PCR positivity was 5.5 percent, while antibody evidence of exposure was much more frequent, at 62 percent. These tests were looking at different biological signals, not giving two interchangeable prevalence estimates. Antibodies reflect an immune response and fecal PCR identifies DNA in the sample. Horses with peritonitis had higher antibody levels, but that association did not assign the cause of every illness. The study reinforces why a general strongyle EPG number, a species-specific fecal test and a blood antibody result cannot simply be substituted for one another. [14]

## Tapeworms and complementary tests

Tapeworm segments release eggs unevenly, so an ordinary fecal egg count can miss infection. Antibody tests in saliva or serum provide a different signal. Lightbody and colleagues validated a saliva ELISA, an antibody-binding laboratory assay, using samples matched to tapeworm counts at an abattoir. In that study it detected at least one tapeworm with 83 percent sensitivity and 85 percent specificity. Neither value was perfect. This was a particular assay validated in horses, and the work was conducted by researchers affiliated with its developer. It does not validate every saliva test, prove that a positive animal's current colic is caused by tapeworms or authorize direct transfer to other equids. [9]

A 2023 field investigation reported apparent treatment failure against equine tapeworms. The authors used egg detection before and after treatment while emphasizing the statistical difficulties of variable shedding and small groups. It is a warning against assuming that a familiar product must always work, but apparent failure is not automatically a standardized resistance diagnosis for every parasite. A negative post-treatment tapeworm specimen may arise because no eggs entered the aliquot examined. Conversely, repeated positive results can justify further investigation. Interpret the observations for the studied farm and method, retaining uncertainty rather than translating one publication into a claim that all tapeworm treatment has become ineffective. [24]

## Checking whether a treatment worked

The fecal egg count reduction test, or FECRT, compares appropriately timed counts before and after treatment in the same group. The 2023 guideline from the World Association for the Advancement of Veterinary Parasitology, or WAAVP, emphasizes enough actual eggs counted, suitable group sizes and statistical uncertainty. A hypothetical group mean falling from 500 to 25 EPG represents a 95 percent reduction, calculated as 100 times the difference divided by the starting mean. That arithmetic is not by itself a resistance verdict. Expected efficacy and interpretation depend on drug class, parasite and confidence limits. Wide limits mean the evidence is imprecise. A result near a boundary may remain inconclusive rather than being forced into effective or resistant. [8]

The importance of checking locally is illustrated by a 2024 investigation on Irish farms. The authors found poor efficacy for some commonly used treatments, with reported fenbendazole reductions ranging from zero to 86.2 percent on affected farms and reduced efficacy of macrocyclic lactones, a class of deworming medicines, in some investigations. The outcomes were egg-count reductions, not direct proof of how many horses were clinically ill. Regional reports cannot establish the resistance status of a particular barn without testing. Before interpreting a poor reduction, the veterinary team reviews the product, administration, horse weights, specimens and laboratory method. The result can then guide a revised programme rather than encourage unplanned repeat dosing by the owner. [21]

A separate Swedish study found mean ivermectin egg-count reductions exceeding 99 percent in all sixteen participating establishments. Egg reappearance periods ranged from six to more than eight weeks. Egg reappearance period means how soon shedding resumes after initial suppression; it is different from the early reduction test. Young horses in that study tended to shed again sooner. This provides an instructive contrast with farms showing poor efficacy: drug performance cannot be assumed to be identical everywhere. The selected establishments largely practiced selective treatment, but the observational design cannot prove that this practice alone caused retained efficacy. Preserve both the successful measurement and that limit. [22]

Another Swedish field study documented reduced pyrantel efficacy and found no corresponding change in the broad cyathostomin species composition examined. Identifying which species are present therefore did not, by itself, explain whether the medicine worked. A parasite population can retain a similar species mixture while resistant worms survive within it. FECRT measures the population's response through egg shedding, rather than giving the horse a permanent personal label of resistance. A new horse can also introduce a different parasite population to a property. Treatment records, arrival history and appropriately repeated efficacy checks make this biological distinction useful in a practical farm programme. [23]

## Real outbreaks with low or absent egg counts

Lawson and colleagues reviewed 38 horses admitted to two hospitals with larval cyathostominosis, a disease caused by small strongyle larvae developing in and emerging from the intestinal wall. Twenty-one were nonsurvivors, including three dead on arrival. Among the 21 horses with a fecal egg count, the median was only 50 EPG. This was a selected hospital population, not the risk for a healthy horse with a low count. It nevertheless demonstrates how serious disease from larval stages can coexist with little egg shedding. Blood protein measurements, inflammatory findings, ultrasound, clinical course and fecal or postmortem evidence contributed to investigation. Associations between treatment and nonsurvival in this retrospective series should not be read as proof that supportive treatment caused death; the sickest animals may require the most intervention. [15]

An Irish outbreak affecting a herd of 23 horses provides another outcome record. Fourteen developed overt signs consistent with acute larval cyathostominosis; two were euthanized and twelve recovered. Postmortem tissue from the fatal cases showed severe intestinal inflammation and damage associated with cyathostomin larvae. The investigators also studied altered fecal bacterial communities, but the small descriptive series could not isolate a single cause of each animal's susceptibility. The recoveries show that the syndrome was not uniformly fatal, while the deaths show why a clinical deterioration requires attention. No single egg-count threshold in that study could be adopted as a universal predictor of who would live or die. [16]

A 2025 report described nine young Thoroughbreds with suspected acute larval cyathostominosis on one Irish stud farm. All nine samples were reported as zero EPG, yet fourth-stage larvae were found in six horses. One horse was euthanized and eight recovered. The report describes a well-managed establishment, but also records absent parasite diagnostics and farm treatment records, with no dung removal or mixed grazing. Those reported gaps qualify the description of the farm. Zero detectable eggs did not exclude larval disease. Even larval examination was negative in some suspected cases, so its absence also did not settle every diagnosis. [17]

## Fatal disease does not create a simple neglect test

An Ontario review identified 24 equids with larval cyathostominosis at postmortem between 1991 and 2003. The investigators judged it the primary cause of clinical illness in nineteen, while five had other major diseases despite compatible intestinal signs. Some animals lacked striking gross lesions, and microscopic examination documented the larvae and associated inflammation. This is a useful forensic distinction: finding parasites, attributing the animal's illness and identifying its cause of death are separate judgments. The selected necropsy records could not provide a survival rate among all exposed horses. They also cannot establish neglect merely because larvae were found. Husbandry, treatment access, clinical timeline and competing disease require their own evidence. [18]

Parascaris roundworms can obstruct the small intestine in young horses, a problem that an adult strongyle monitoring category does not describe. Tatz and colleagues reported fifteen horses undergoing surgery for ascarid impaction: twelve survived to discharge and nine survived at least a year. The authors described associations between surgical approach and outcome, but the retrospective series was small and could not separate every treatment effect from disease severity or other changes in care. These were horses with surgically confirmed obstruction, not horses selected by a particular EPG cutoff. A painful, distended or deteriorating foal needs veterinary assessment regardless of whether a previous fecal report looked reassuring. [19]

A later Scandinavian surgical review found fourteen Parascaris impactions among the juvenile horses investigated. Four survived to discharge. Of the three discharged foals with long-term information, one was euthanized about a year later for an uncertain reason, and two were known to survive for years; the fourth outcome was unknown. Several had received anthelmintics shortly before colic, while three had never been treated. Timing alone did not establish that treatment caused the obstruction. The markedly different outcome from the fifteen-horse series also warns against presenting one hospital's survival percentage as a universal prognosis. Preserve the case selection, confirmation method and missing follow-up when using such reports to explain risk. [20]

# V043: Bovine mastitis testing and milk culture

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Cattle
- **Clinical Category**: Udder health and infectious disease
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When milk looks abnormal or its cell count rises, which tests show inflammation, which find a possible cause, and which actually help a cow recover?*

## Inflammation and infection are related but different

Mastitis means inflammation of the mammary gland. An intramammary infection means microorganisms have established infection within it. Infection commonly triggers mastitis, but the inflammatory response can remain after organisms are cleared, and an abnormal milk appearance can have other explanations. Clinical mastitis produces visible changes in milk, the udder or the cow. Subclinical mastitis lacks obvious visible signs but can reduce production and raise inflammatory markers. Merck's veterinary guidance distinguishes these presentations because an otherwise alert cow with local changes poses a different immediate problem from a cow with systemic illness. Testing should serve both the affected animal and the herd. [1]

## Start with the cow the quarter and the history

A cow's udder contains four separate milk producing glands, called quarters. Recording which quarter is affected gives a test its anatomical meaning. A quarter sample, a mixture from all four quarters and a bulk tank sample from many cows answer different questions. Examination also considers appetite, temperature, hydration, pain, milk production, recent calving and previous episodes. Blood in milk can result from teat or udder trauma and bleeding vessels, while severe mastitis can also discolor milk. Merck's description of structural and traumatic disorders therefore provides an important boundary: color or clots justify investigation but do not identify a particular bacterium. A physical injury and an infection can also coexist. [22]

## Somatic cells measure an inflammatory response

Somatic cell count, abbreviated SCC, counts the cow's cells in milk, chiefly white blood cells recruited during inflammation together with cells shed from mammary tissue. Results are expressed as cells per milliliter. A threshold around 200,000 cells per milliliter is commonly used to flag an individual cow for possible infection and further assessment. It is not a bacterial count, an absolute line between health and disease, or a measure of suffering. University of Kentucky guidance emphasizes trends, affected quarters and stage of lactation when reviewing SCC. One high result can prompt a targeted sample; repeated results help distinguish a continuing problem from an isolated change. [3]

The California Mastitis Test, or CMT, is a rapid indirect estimate of milk cells. Its reagent disrupts cell membranes and interacts with released cellular material to produce thickening or a gel. A trained observer grades the reaction, so it is less precise than an automated cell count. Testing quarters separately can reveal where the response is strongest even when the cow's combined milk looks ordinary. The University of Minnesota describes it as a test for subclinical mastitis, rather than a way to identify an organism. A strong gel is evidence of increased cells; it does not specify whether the cause is Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, another infection or a recently resolving episode. [2]

Timing around calving matters when assessing such a screen. Dingwell and colleagues compared CMT with culture of individual milk samples in early lactation. In that study, sensitivity and specificity were highest on day four, at 82.4 and 80.6 percent. Sensitivity describes finding infections identified by the reference method; specificity describes correctly classifying those without that finding. These percentages belong to that study's definitions, day and comparison culture. Culture itself can miss infection, so it is an imperfect reference. The result does not turn day four into a universal appointment or mean an early high CMT requires antibiotics. It shows why a screening result needs its timing and confirmatory evidence. [4]

## Collection can introduce a second source of bacteria

Milk passes through a teat opening surrounded by skin, bedding and environmental organisms. Contamination during collection can therefore produce growth unrelated to infection inside the quarter. The submitting team should use the diagnostic laboratory's collection instructions and suitable sterile containers, label the cow and quarter clearly, and record the collection time and any treatment already given. North Dakota State University's laboratory warns that traces of disinfectant or antibiotics may inhibit growth and that transport problems can reduce viable organisms. Cooling and prompt transport preserve the information, but exact storage rules depend on the test. A sample intended for cell counting or molecular testing should not be assumed suitable for culture after an incompatible preservative is added. [5]

## Culture looks for growth under specified conditions

Culture attempts to grow organisms from milk under controlled laboratory conditions. The laboratory interprets colony appearance, amount and identification together, rather than declaring every colony the cause. Gram positive and Gram negative describe broad bacterial cell wall groups distinguished by a laboratory stain; they are useful first classifications, not complete species names or severity scores. Wisconsin's laboratory guidance explains that a no growth result can follow immune clearance, prior treatment, low or intermittent shedding, handling problems or a test that does not support the organism. Persistent inflammation can therefore coexist with negative culture. A negative result should be read as no organism recovered under these conditions, rather than proof that the cow never had mastitis. [6]

A routine milk culture has a defined purpose. The University of Missouri describes its mastitis service as a basic aerobic culture, meaning organisms grow under oxygen containing conditions, and states that Mycoplasma is excluded unless separately investigated. It also explicitly distinguishes this diagnostic service from food safety testing of milk. Laboratories have reporting rules for mixed growth; the Missouri service reports samples with three or more organisms as contaminated. Other laboratories use more detailed patterns and significance rules. The veterinarian should read the actual laboratory comment and request clarification or a new sample when needed. A contaminated specimen is an uncertain piece of evidence, not a diagnosis of three simultaneous udder infections. [24]

## Mixing milk changes what a negative result means

Combining four quarters is convenient, but milk from an infected quarter can be diluted by the others, and the source quarter is lost. In a Dutch study of 482 cows suspected of subclinical mastitis on 69 farms, composite culture was compared with separate quarter cultures. Sensitivity varied by organism: approximately 58 percent for Staphylococcus aureus and 87 percent for Streptococcus agalactiae, while specificity exceeded 98 percent for the four organisms assessed. These are historical test characteristics under that study's methods, not fixed values for every modern laboratory. They show that a negative composite sample may provide less reassurance than a suitable quarter investigation when a particular gland remains suspicious. [7]

A bulk tank sample mixes milk from much of the herd. It can reveal a herd level warning without identifying which cow is infected, and a negative tank does not exclude a small number of intermittently shedding cows. The University of Minnesota recommends combining tank surveillance with individual testing of fresh cows, clinical cases or cows with high SCC according to the herd's risk. Tank bacterial findings may also reflect organisms on skin or equipment. Bulk tank SCC similarly summarizes the cells contributed by the milk actually entering that tank, weighted by milk volume. Milk withheld from sale is missing from that measurement, so it cannot describe every affected animal on the farm. [8]

## PCR detects DNA without requiring growth

PCR, polymerase chain reaction, extracts genetic material and copies chosen DNA targets until they can be detected. A multiplex assay searches for several specified organisms in one run. It can detect material from dead or growth inhibited bacteria as well as living ones, while organisms outside its target list remain invisible. Koskinen and colleagues compared an 11 target PCR system with culture in 1,000 quarter samples from 922 cows. Among 780 clinical samples, culture identified pathogens in 600 and PCR detected bacteria in 691. Culture also found organisms the panel did not target. The higher molecular yield demonstrates a difference in detection, not proof that every extra target required treatment. The manufacturer's involvement and the selected case population also matter when applying the findings. [9]

Taponen and colleagues examined 79 clinical mastitis samples that had no growth by conventional culture. PCR detected one or two targeted bacterial groups in 34 samples, or 43 percent. Clinical signs and a milk enzyme marker supported inflammation, while DNA detection supplied a possible etiological clue, meaning a clue to the cause. The assay did not establish that those bacteria remained alive at sampling, nor did the study prove that treating every additional molecular detection improved recovery. A molecular positive and culture negative combination is therefore not necessarily a laboratory contradiction. The two tests examine different properties of the sample, and the timing of illness and treatment can explain why their answers diverge. [10]

## An ordinary culture can miss an important herd pathogen

Mycoplasma bovis is particularly important when several quarters are involved, milk falls sharply or cases fail to respond as expected. Cornell's diagnostic guidance describes specialized culture conditions and a possible three to ten days before colonies become visible. Molecular testing can provide another route to detection, depending on the laboratory. Herd screening must account for carriers without obvious mastitis and dilution in tank milk. A negative ordinary bacterial plate therefore does not exclude Mycoplasma. The significance extends beyond the udder because these organisms can also be associated with respiratory, joint and ear disease in cattle. The veterinarian coordinates individual identification, separation and herd biosecurity rather than simply changing antibiotics after each unsuccessful course. [11]

## Sensors can flag change without identifying its cause

Automatic milking systems can monitor milk yield, conductivity and other indicators. Electrical conductivity reflects how readily electrical current moves through milk and changes with its ionic composition. Lactate dehydrogenase, or LDH, is an enzyme whose milk activity can rise with tissue injury and inflammation. A 2019 diagnostic study examined SCC, conductivity and LDH in different milk fractions before and after milk ejection. The work illustrates that the fraction sampled and the organism associated with inflammation can affect a marker's usefulness. An alert should prompt inspection and appropriate diagnostic sampling. It is neither a bacterial identification nor evidence that the animal is safe to ignore when the software produces no alarm. [23]

## Susceptibility results are one part of treatment selection

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing examines whether an isolate's growth is inhibited under standardized laboratory conditions. Interpretation requires a suitable clinical breakpoint, the rule relating a laboratory measurement to an expected drug response for a defined organism and setting. It does not reproduce drug delivery, damaged tissue or the cow's immunity. A 2026 Brazilian observational study enrolled 340 cows with mild or moderate clinical mastitis from 14 herds. Overall susceptibility classifications did not consistently predict bacteriological cure, although oxacillin resistance in the staphylococcal group was associated with failure. Treatment protocols were not standardized, and some breakpoints were borrowed where specific ones were unavailable. Those limitations restrict prediction; they do not make resistance surveillance or a properly interpreted resistant result irrelevant. [16]

An older treatment study by Owens and colleagues provides another boundary. Even though all tested isolates were considered susceptible to the studied drug combination, bacteriological cure in newly acquired Staphylococcus aureus infections was 70 percent, compared with 35 percent in chronic infections. Bacteriological cure means the organism was no longer recovered by the study's follow up sampling; clinical cure means visible signs resolved. Those are different outcomes. The historical percentages should not be used to choose a current product or promise an individual cow's result. They demonstrate why infection duration and the host can alter recovery even when the organism appears susceptible in the laboratory. [17]

## Culture guided treatment has been tested in selected cows

Lago and colleagues randomized 422 cows with mild or moderate mastitis affecting 449 quarters across eight farms. One program treated immediately; the other used culture results to select treatment after a short delay. Intramammary therapy means administering medicine into the mammary gland through the teat. Antibiotics were given through the assigned protocol to 100 percent of control quarter cases and 44 percent of culture guided cases. Including secondary therapy, the proportions were 100 and 51 percent. The investigators found no statistically significant differences in time to clinical cure or in their short term bacteriological and failure outcomes. Failure rates were not zero, and the numerically different outcomes do not prove perfect equivalence. The trial supports supervised selection for nonsevere cases, not withholding care from a systemically ill cow while waiting for a plate. [12]

The companion report followed that same cohort through lactation, rather than providing an independent second trial. It found no statistically significant differences between treatment programs in mastitis recurrence, SCC, milk production or cow survival. Longer follow up matters because normal looking milk after several days does not settle whether infection persists or another episode will occur. These results concern a defined protocol, trained participating farms and enrolled cows. They do not establish that every homemade interpretation of a culture system is equally reliable, or that all herds have the same pathogen mix. A farm's veterinary protocol needs criteria for reassessment and escalation as well as a decision about initial antibiotic use. [13]

A separate randomized study by Vasquez and colleagues at a 3,500 cow New York farm analyzed 489 eligible mild or moderate mastitis events: 246 assigned a pathogen based approach and 243 blanket treatment. Severe cases were excluded. Intramammary antibiotics were used in 32 percent of pathogen based cases and all blanket cases. Mean times to clinical cure, 4.5 and 4.8 days respectively, were not significantly different; milk production and survival outcomes also showed no significant differences. These results concern a defined selection strategy, rather than untreated mastitis of every severity. Applying it requires an initial severity assessment, dependable culture interpretation and monitoring of the cow's course, with veterinary reassessment when recovery does not proceed as expected. [14]

## A culture program also needs quality control

Field implementation can be less tidy than a controlled comparison. McDougall and colleagues studied selective treatment on seven dairy farms and compared on farm results with laboratory culture. Overall agreement was 188 of 331 samples, or 56.9 percent, and ranged from 44.7 to 88.2 percent between farms. Agreement is not the same as sensitivity for one pathogen, and a percentage combining growth categories cannot describe every treatment decision. The variation shows why training, sample handling, interpretation and periodic laboratory comparison matter. A veterinarian may need species level identification or repeat sampling when a broad culture category does not fit the cow's course. Reducing antibiotic use is useful only alongside acceptable animal outcomes. [15]

## An algal outbreak changed the diagnosis and herd response

Hodges and colleagues reported Prototheca zopfii mastitis in 17 of 120 cows in a New Zealand herd. Prototheca is an alga, rather than an ordinary bacterium. Nine affected cows had visible flakes or clots; some others had elevated SCC without obvious clinical mastitis. Examination of udder tissue showed granulomatous inflammation, a pattern containing organized inflammatory cells, associated with the organism. Repeated milk examinations identified affected animals, and removal of infected cows was reported to control the problem. The paper used the organism names available in 1985. It did not demonstrate an antibiotic cure. Although environmental water also yielded the organism, the suggested route of entry remained a hypothesis. Identifying a possible source is different from proving an individual management failure. [18]

## Milk changes can reveal a virus beyond routine bacteriology

In the 2024 United States H5N1 investigation, Burrough and colleagues described affected dairy cows with illness, reduced feed intake and abrupt loss of milk production. Influenza A screening PCR, subtype testing and national reference confirmation identified highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1. In examined mammary tissue, destructive lesions with suppuration, meaning pus forming inflammation, were accompanied by viral antigen in mammary epithelial cells, the cells lining milk secreting structures. This combination of molecular testing and pathology supplied stronger causal evidence than an abnormal milk appearance alone. The series also documented fatal infection in cats exposed to raw milk from affected cows. It was a diagnostic case series, not a bovine treatment trial or an estimate of every infected cow's recovery. A routine bacterial milk culture would not answer the viral question. [19]

The current United States Department of Agriculture, or USDA, National Milk Testing Strategy uses influenza testing in milk for surveillance and follow up investigation. Its Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, or APHIS, explains that a positive processing silo sample triggers investigation; herd level testing determines which premises are affected. This is a different scale of evidence from diagnosing one quarter. Pooling many milk sources is efficient for surveillance but requires trace back to locate an affected herd. Suspected unusual or clustered disease should be discussed promptly with the veterinarian and relevant animal health officials, using current sampling instructions. Neither a positive milk PCR nor a farm exposure alone proves that a particular person's illness was acquired there. [20]

## Recovery food safety and welfare need separate records

A cow can have improved looking milk while still being within a medicine's milk discard period. Conversely, a negative culture is not a drug residue test. The United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, explains that withdrawal and milk discard periods allow drug residues to fall to acceptable levels and that treatment records must identify the animal, medicines and relevant dates. The veterinarian determines the applicable instructions, including any lawful extra label use. Mastitis culture, inflammatory markers, residue testing and human food safety investigations have different purposes. Keeping them separate prevents a reassuring result in one category from being used as permission in another. Recovery should also be assessed through comfort, appetite, milk production and recurrence. [21]

Fever, loss of appetite, dehydration or shock make suspected mastitis urgent. These signs require prompt veterinary assessment and supportive care decisions without waiting for culture. A high cell count alone cannot establish neglect or legal responsibility. An animal welfare investigation needs clinical findings, chronology, treatment and husbandry records alongside the laboratory evidence. [1]

# V044: Bovine paratuberculosis testing

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Cattle
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease testing
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *A healthy-looking cow can carry Johne’s infection for years. Testing makes the hidden risk more visible, but a negative result never supplies a universal guarantee.*

## An infection that can remain hidden for years

Bovine paratuberculosis, usually called Johne’s disease, is an infection of cattle caused by Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, abbreviated MAP. The bacterium is swallowed, commonly in material contaminated by manure, and can establish a long-lasting infection in the intestine. Young cattle are particularly susceptible. Milk and colostrum, the first milk after calving, can also contribute to exposure. An infected animal may look healthy for years while the herd’s infection risk is already changing. Testing therefore has several different jobs: investigating a sick cow, finding animals releasing bacteria in manure, monitoring control, and estimating the risk of buying replacements. Those jobs require different combinations of evidence; one laboratory result cannot answer all of them. [1]

## When the cow needs attention before the final result

In clinical Johne’s disease, persistent intestinal inflammation interferes with absorption and allows protein to escape into the bowel. A cow may continue eating while losing weight, producing less milk and developing persistent or intermittent diarrhea. Loss of blood proteins can cause swelling beneath the jaw, often called bottle jaw. These signs are not unique to MAP, so the veterinarian also considers other intestinal, nutritional and systemic diseases. Progressive weakness, dehydration, marked wasting or inability to maintain intake warrants prompt assessment and a welfare plan while testing proceeds. Waiting weeks for a culture result is inappropriate if the animal is deteriorating. There is no established treatment that reliably cures bovine Johne’s disease; prognosis and humane management need individual clinical judgment. [2]

## Antibodies and shedding answer different questions

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, or ELISA, detects antibodies: proteins made by the animal’s immune system. Laboratories can test serum, the liquid separated from clotted blood, or an appropriately validated milk sample. The result is indirect evidence rather than a count of bacteria. Antibody responses are often weak or absent early in infection and become easier to detect as disease progresses. Cornell’s diagnostic guidance emphasizes that an antibody-negative cow can still shed MAP. Conversely, a positive screen often needs fecal testing and herd context before an individual management decision. The same guidance generally discourages routine bovine testing below 18 months because detection is unreliable at that stage, rather than because infection in a younger calf is impossible. [3]

## Culture, PCR and the material being measured

Fecal culture asks whether viable MAP can be recovered by a specialist laboratory; the organism’s slow growth means that final results can take many weeks. Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, detects selected bacterial DNA sequences much faster. DNA detection does not by itself establish that the detected organisms remain alive. Both methods depend on whether the submitted portion of manure contains detectable material, and laboratory processing can affect recovery. The World Organisation for Animal Health’s diagnostic manual treats antibody tests, organism detection and tissue examination as complementary tools. It also describes interference associated with whole-cell vaccination, including antibody responses and complications for tuberculosis testing. Vaccination history and local programme rules should therefore accompany a submission; a vaccine is not a substitute for interpreting the assay. [4]

## Why an accuracy percentage needs its reference standard

In a 2005 comparison, researchers evaluated five ELISAs using cattle from seven infection-free and seven infected dairy herds. Three laboratories together identified 417 fecal-culture-positive cows. Four assays detected only about 28–29% of those culture-positive animals. When the investigators instead used one laboratory’s culture results as the reference, estimated ELISA sensitivity rose to roughly 46–50%. Sensitivity means the proportion of reference-positive animals detected; specificity means the proportion of reference-negative animals correctly called negative. Changing the reference changed the apparent accuracy even though the antibody tests had not changed. These historical product-specific results explain why an attractive percentage from a brochure cannot be transferred automatically to every herd, disease stage or modern assay. [5]

## Different PCR services may produce different answers

PCR is a family of laboratory methods, not a single interchangeable test. Extraction must release DNA from difficult bacterial cells while dealing with substances in manure that can inhibit amplification. A 2024 ring trial sent the same bovine and ovine fecal material through different laboratories and workflows; detection varied between methods and laboratories. That comparison does not establish one universal ranking for all cattle populations. It does support asking which assay is validated for the intended species and specimen, whether the laboratory checks for inhibition, and how borderline results are handled. When monitoring one herd over time, changing the method can change the apparent trend. The laboratory should help distinguish a biological change from a change in measurement. [6]

## Reading a positive, a negative or a disagreement

A quantitative PCR can provide an estimate related to the amount of MAP DNA. A cycle-threshold value describes how much amplification was needed to detect the signal; within the same validated system, a lower threshold generally accompanies more starting DNA. Values from different assays are not a universal bacterial scale. The American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine consensus also emphasizes that organisms can pass through an animal after ingestion from a contaminated environment. Thus a weak fecal result, an antibody-positive result with negative fecal testing, and a repeatedly high fecal result are different situations. The veterinarian weighs clinical signs, repeat measurements, previous results and programme definitions. None of these patterns can safely be reduced to “one negative proves freedom” or “every positive predicts immediate clinical decline.” [7]

## A decade of observation revealed different trajectories

A longitudinal study followed three northeastern United States dairy herds for approximately ten years, collecting about 13,000 serum, 6,500 fecal and 2,000 tissue samples. The investigators observed progressors, with increasing and sustained shedding accompanied by increasing antibodies, and non-progressors, with intermittent low shedding and little detectable antibody response. Positive proportions differed markedly among serum, feces and tissues. These were sample results accumulated over time, not three interchangeable estimates of the proportion of cows infected on one day. The practical lesson is that a quiet year or a single negative sample can belong to a longer infection history. Repeated identified-animal records can reveal that history far more clearly than a folder of unrelated positive and negative reports. [8]

## The immune response can lag behind fecal shedding

Repeated sampling of 14 infected cows in two low-prevalence dairy herds allowed a 2017 study to compare milk ELISA, fecal culture and fecal quantitative PCR within the same animals over time. Higher shedding and stronger antibody signals were associated, but they did not always peak together. In some analyses, a rise in shedding predicted a higher milk-antibody result up to two months later. This helps explain a real-world disagreement: an animal can be releasing detectable bacterial material before its antibody result becomes strongly positive. The small, selected infected cohort cannot supply an all-herd accuracy estimate. It does show why the sampling date belongs beside the result and why a sequence of results can be more informative than simultaneous agreement alone. [9]

## Pass-through is possible, but it is not an automatic explanation

A closed Limousin herd was monitored for 53 months in a study of possible passive passage of MAP. Initially, 26 of 41 animals were PCR-positive, whereas two were culture-positive. After the two high-shedding cows were removed, the proportion PCR-positive fell over subsequent examinations, eventually reaching 6.7%. The authors interpreted the pattern as evidence that some low-level detections could reflect ingested environmental organisms passing through rather than established infection. This finding does not make all weak positives harmless or false. It identifies a competing explanation that needs appropriate follow-up, especially in a contaminated herd. A repeat sample can clarify the pattern, but a negative repeat does not retrospectively prove that the first result represented contamination. [10]

## Pooling saves resources and changes sensitivity

Pooling combines portions from several identified animals for one laboratory test. A negative pool is an economical screening result for that group; a positive pool usually requires individual follow-up to locate detectable shedders. In an Australian beef-cattle experiment, investigators created 160 pools using archived positive and negative fecal material. Pools of ten performed best in that particular direct-PCR system, but differences among the tested pool sizes were not statistically significant, and low bacterial quantities remained harder to detect. Larger pools also increased inhibition problems. This is not a recommendation to make ten-animal pools on every farm. The validated laboratory and control programme determine the permissible pool size, handling and follow-up, because dilution can hide a low-level positive animal. [11]

## A bulk sample estimates herd risk rather than naming a cow

A Normandy study collected individual serum from 15,372 cattle in 192 herds, alongside bulk-tank milk and composite fecal samples. Bulk-milk antibody testing alone missed many herds containing seropositive cattle; performance improved when more tested cattle in a herd were antibody-positive. Composite fecal PCR and milk ELISA supplied partly different information, so combining them improved detection under the study’s definition. That reference was individual antibody status, itself imperfect, rather than a flawless infection census. A bulk-tank result also represents the milk entering that tank, not every bull, dry cow or replacement heifer. These distinctions matter when a herd screen is used to plan further testing: a herd-level positive does not identify a particular animal, and a negative does not cover unsampled groups automatically. [12]

## Environmental testing locates contamination without identifying its source

Researchers examined 72 environmental samples from 19 infected Czech dairy herds, comparing PCR with culture in milking-parlour holding areas and alleyways. Results showed that a shared environment can provide useful evidence of MAP contamination, but the methods did not agree perfectly and no sampled location was established as universally superior. An environmental positive can justify looking more closely at adult shedding and the movement of manure around susceptible youngstock. It cannot identify which cow deposited the material or establish when an individual became infected. A negative sample covers the place, material and date tested; it does not certify every pen or pasture. Environmental screening is strongest when linked to a defined herd investigation rather than treated as an individual-animal diagnosis. [13]

## Certification is a documented level of risk

Cattle Health Certification Standards, known as CHeCS, the organisation setting standards for participating cattle health schemes in the United Kingdom and Ireland, updated its technical document in June 2025. Its Johne’s risk-level programme uses adult-herd testing together with management requirements and a health plan. Risk Level 1 follows three consecutive annual herd-test passes. The additional R1* category requires no seropositive animals at the most recent herd test or during the preceding twelve months; follow-up fecal negatives do not erase that additional condition. These are programme-specific definitions, not a claim that every animal is uninfected. A purchaser needs the current certificate, the population and method tested, and the herd’s subsequent history. A negative pre-purchase test on a young individual provides much less reassurance than its appearance on a sale sheet may suggest. [14]

## Documented herd outcome: ten years of sustained control

A 2024 German report described a closed commercial dairy herd of about 450 cattle followed with annual fecal culture and antibody testing from 2012 through 2022. The authors’ estimated prevalence of shedders fell from 24.2% in 2012 to 0.4% in 2019; no shedder was identified during 2020–2022. Culture tended to identify positive animals earlier than ELISA, and agreement between the tests was generally low. Removal of shedders operated alongside calf hygiene and colostrum management. This was a substantial, documented herd improvement over a decade, not a rapid cure of individual cattle. Its success depended on continued management and surveillance; the absence of detected shedders cannot be exported as a guarantee for another herd with a different testing history. [15]

## Documented programme outcome: fourteen Italian dairy farms

Musolino and colleagues examined annual serology and herd records from fourteen northern Italian dairy farms during 2014–2021. Antibody-positive animals were more numerous in 2014–2016 than in 2017–2021, and nine farms improved their programme health ranking. No clinical Johne’s cases were reported during the reviewed period. MAP shedding was detected in 9.4% of the seropositive animals, emphasizing that an antibody result and a fecal-shedding result are not equivalent. The farms were few and were not randomly selected, so their outcomes cannot establish the result every national programme will achieve. Standardized testing and management changes accompanied the improvement, but this observational design cannot isolate the effect of each measure or prove that the remaining cattle were all infection-free. [16]

## Documented PCR programme outcome: control without complete disappearance

On a Victorian dairy farm, an Australian study evaluated annual high-throughput Johne’s fecal PCR from 2015 to 2023. Investigators performed 7,153 fecal tests on 1,955 individual Jersey cows, using pooled screening followed by individual testing. The proportion of detected fecal shedders decreased from 9.6% to 1.2%, shedding levels fell, and clinical cases were eliminated during the programme. The endpoint still included PCR-positive cows: disease control was not the same as disappearance of every detectable infection. Some low or moderate shedders subsequently tested negative, illustrating fluctuating detectability. The report supports sustained testing linked to action and protection of susceptible calves. It does not show that a single annual PCR, or testing without changes in exposure, produces the same outcome elsewhere. [17]

## Experimental calves show why age rules are not biological absolutes

In a longitudinal experimental study, five calves were exposed to MAP at two weeks of age and followed with diagnostic testing. Two developed clinical Johne’s disease at twelve and sixteen months. Both had repeatedly positive fecal cultures before the clinical illness, with antibody detection appearing later. These are real, unusually early outcomes under a deliberate experimental exposure, not a prediction for ordinary calves on a farm. They demonstrate that disease and detectable infection can occur before the ages commonly chosen for routine herd screening. Screening-age rules are designed around the expected performance and efficiency of programmes. A sick young animal with a credible exposure history still needs a clinical investigation rather than dismissal solely because it is “too young for Johne’s.” [18]

## Older youngstock are not completely protected by age

A field investigation exposed yearling dairy cattle to pasture previously grazed by MAP-infected animals. Infection was subsequently demonstrated, showing that susceptibility decreases with age without vanishing. The study’s outcome matters when interpreting a management history: separating newborn calves from adult manure is valuable, but it does not make later heavily contaminated grazing irrelevant. It also cautions against assigning an exact infection date from the animal’s age at diagnosis. Long incubation, variable exposure and imperfect detection leave substantial uncertainty. A positive adult test cannot by itself reveal whether infection occurred around birth, during rearing or later. The herd investigation should reconstruct plausible exposure opportunities and protective practices while keeping those possibilities distinct from events actually demonstrated by testing. [19]

## Necropsy example: an Angus cow with progressive wasting

The Joint Pathology Center’s 2017 conference documented a 3.5-year-old Aberdeen Angus cow with profuse chronic diarrhea and progressive emaciation. Fecal examination found acid-fast rods, bacteria retaining a special laboratory stain, and was negative for Salmonella. The cow was euthanized and examined after death. Its small intestine was thickened and folded; histopathology, microscopic examination of tissue, showed severe chronic inflammation dominated by macrophages, immune cells that can contain organisms, with numerous acid-fast rods in intestinal and lymph-node tissue. The reported diagnosis was paratuberculosis. No additional bacterial identification was reported in that case account. This provides a necropsy-supported explanation for wasting, rather than permission to attribute every thin cow to underfeeding or every acid-fast organism to MAP without evaluating the full pathological evidence. [20]

## Forensic interpretation requires more than matching a bacterium

Whole-genome sequencing compares much of an organism’s genetic material and can help investigate related infections. In a Canadian study, investigators examined MAP isolates from 67 cows in twenty herds. Detailed genetic differences supported multiple infection events, and some transmission links required animal-movement information to make the genomic evidence meaningful. Less detailed typing could not adequately resolve between-herd transmission. The relevant forensic lesson is evidential restraint: a positive test, a similar strain or severe disease does not independently prove a seller’s knowledge, the date of acquisition, a particular transmission route or neglect. Attribution needs verified animal identities, laboratory methods, movement and management records, and consideration of missing sampling. Biological relatedness and responsibility are different questions. [21]

## MAP and human Crohn’s disease: an unresolved relationship

Johne’s disease is established in cattle; the proposed relationship between MAP and Crohn’s disease in people remains uncertain. British Columbia’s official information describes the possible association without treating it as proven causation. Finding MAP in some human studies, or observing similarities between intestinal diseases, does not show that a particular cow caused a person’s illness. Nor can a bovine antibody or fecal result diagnose Crohn’s disease in an owner. Animal-health control, food hygiene and public-health assessment have their own purposes and evidence standards. The uncertainty deserves accurate explanation rather than either a declaration that MAP is a confirmed cause of Crohn’s disease or an assurance that the research question has been definitively closed. [22]

## Turning the report into a defensible next decision

The United States Department of Agriculture’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service emphasizes appropriate licensed tests, approved laboratories and herd risk assessment within its accredited-veterinarian guidance. A useful record identifies the animal or defined group, specimen, collection date, assay, previous results and reason for testing. The veterinarian then states what the result establishes, what remains uncertain and what follow-up or herd action is justified under the applicable programme. Clinically affected cattle need an immediate welfare decision alongside the diagnostic plan; apparently healthy animals need a proportionate interpretation of transmission risk. Protecting calves and purchasing with herd-history information continue between testing rounds. The most useful endpoint is a documented reduction in disease and exposure risk, supported by repeat evidence, rather than an unsupported promise of permanent freedom. [23]

# V046: Small ruminant fecal egg count

- **Domain**: Veterinary
- **Species Scope**: Sheep, Goat
- **Clinical Category**: Parasitology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *A worm egg count measures shedding, while a pale or collapsing sheep or goat may need care before eggs tell the full story.*

## What an egg count can tell a sheep or goat keeper

A fecal egg count, abbreviated FEC, estimates the number of parasite eggs in a measured amount of manure. Its usual unit is eggs per gram, or EPG. This helps monitor grazing groups, identify substantial egg shedding and investigate whether a worm treatment worked. It does not directly count every worm inside the animal or diagnose every cause of diarrhea, poor growth or death. Sustainable Control of Parasites in Sheep, known as SCOPS, emphasizes that clinically ill animals need veterinary investigation rather than interpretation of a count alone. Marked pallor, weakness, collapse, rapidly worsening diarrhea or sudden deaths require prompt attention. A laboratory result from an apparently well group must not postpone assessment of the sick animal beside it. [1]

## Begin with a defined group and a useful history

Australia’s WormBoss guidance uses the term mob for a group managed together. A meaningful group result starts by defining that group: species, ages, grazing history, recent treatment and whether samples represent healthy animals, poor performers or both. Mixing lambs with adult ewes, or goat kids with lactating does, can conceal the population actually at risk. Fresh, correctly identified material and the receiving laboratory’s collection instructions matter more than an improvised universal sampling recipe. For an individual clinical question, preserve that animal’s identity rather than substituting an anonymous flock average. For monitoring, use representative sampling agreed with the adviser. Record which animals and management groups were excluded, so a reassuring result is not mistakenly applied to animals it never represented. [2]

## Egg output is not a direct internal head count

Adult female worms produce eggs, but different worm types produce different numbers and host immunity can suppress egg production. The amount of water in manure also changes the concentration measured per gram. WormBoss therefore interprets counts alongside species, season, age, condition and production stage. Two animals with the same EPG need not carry the same number of worms or suffer the same damage. A high-producing parasite can dominate egg output without being the only clinically important worm present. Conversely, immature or temporarily arrested worms may contribute little to the count. The test is most useful as one measurement in a biological story, with its method and detection limit attached, rather than as a standalone numerical instruction to treat or leave untreated. [3]

## Storage can change both the number and the apparent mixture

A sheep-and-goat storage study by Drimtzia and Papadopoulos compared fresh material with refrigerated samples over time. Egg counts declined during prolonged storage, while the proportion of Haemonchus larvae developing in laboratory cultures changed earlier than the overall egg count. Thus a sample might retain an apparently usable total while no longer preserving the original worm mixture equally well. The study does not justify leaving routine samples for weeks before submission. It shows why prompt delivery, a recorded collection date and the laboratory’s requirements are essential. Material intended for a simple count, larval identification or molecular testing may need different handling. Adding preservatives or applying a storage rule designed for another test can make later interpretation difficult. [4]

## Precision begins with the small portion actually examined

McMaster and Mini-FLOTAC are named methods that concentrate and count eggs in a small, defined portion of a processed fecal sample. Flotation uses a dense liquid to lift eggs away from much of the debris. A counting factor converts observed eggs to EPG, accounting for dilution and the volume examined. With few observed eggs, one extra or missed egg can substantially change the estimate; a low-resolution method can also report zero when a more sensitive examination detects eggs. In a 2021 comparison using West African dwarf sheep and goats, Mini-FLOTAC produced more precise results under the study’s conditions but required more analysis time. Neither method turns a tiny subsample into perfect knowledge of an animal. Comparing results is most defensible when the method, detection limit, sample quality and laboratory consistency are known. [5]

## Automated counting also needs validation

McEvoy and colleagues compared five methods on fecal samples from 41 naturally infected lambs in 2024, including conventional microscopy and three image-analysis devices. Results were positively correlated, but some devices returned different EPG values and some were less repeatable than the manual McMaster comparator. Numbers of less common egg types were too small for a robust comparison across every method. Agreement on which samples tend to be higher is not the same as interchangeable counts. A device trained to recognize strongyle eggs, a group of similar-looking roundworm eggs, may perform differently for another parasite or a difficult sample. Automation can improve workflow, but its validation, quality checks and parasite coverage still belong in the interpretation, especially if a farm changes its testing service. [6]

## A pooled average can hide the animal that needs help

A 2023 small-ruminant study compared 801 individual samples with 134 pooled samples. Pooled and individual counts were often strongly correlated, yet agreement was frequently poor. Treatment-efficacy interpretations matched in all sheep trials, but differed in four of ten goat trials. Pooling therefore has a legitimate cost-saving role without replacing every individual question. An average cannot identify the few animals contributing most of the eggs or show which one is anemic. It also loses information needed to estimate uncertainty from variation among animals. The veterinarian and laboratory should decide whether the question is group monitoring, individual welfare or a formal reduction test before choosing pooled analysis, and should avoid treating a group mean as every animal’s result. [7]

## Sheep and goats need species-specific interpretation

In a controlled 2024 experiment, seven Arsi-Bale sheep and seven Arsi-Bale goats were infected with Haemonchus contortus, alongside uninfected control groups. Egg counts began increasing around day 21 after infection, whereas packed cell volume, the proportion of blood occupied by red cells, had already begun falling earlier. Infected goats developed higher egg counts and lower packed cell volumes than the infected sheep. Examination at the study endpoint also found greater worm establishment in the goats. These are outcomes from particular breeds and experimental conditions, not a universal ranking of every sheep and goat. They show why a sheep-derived numerical rule cannot automatically be transferred to goats and why blood loss can precede a reassuring egg-count stage. [8]

## Pregnancy, lactation and season change the baseline

A study of naturally infected Jamunapari and Sirohi goats analyzed 1,399 fecal samples. Counts varied with breed, season, year and reproductive state, with higher counts in late pregnancy and pronounced changes around early lactation. Those were repeated samples, not 1,399 independent goats. Such changes can reflect shifting immunity and exposure as well as the worms themselves. A rising count around kidding therefore needs its production context; it is not automatically proof that a recent product failed. Equally, a low count in one season does not permanently characterize that doe. Recording kidding dates, grazing changes and previous counts makes the result useful for planning while avoiding a misleading comparison between animals measured under very different biological conditions. [9]

## Danger can precede egg production: Nematodirus in lambs

Nematodirus battus provides a crucial exception to simple count-based reassurance. SCOPS explains that young lambs can become severely ill from developing larvae before those worms are mature enough to produce detectable eggs. A low or negative count therefore does not exclude an early outbreak of nematodirosis. Risk assessment also uses lamb age, previous grazing, weather and hatch forecasts, together with the clinical picture. Dehydration and rapidly progressing diarrhea require veterinary attention without waiting for egg production to catch up. The parasite’s distinctive eggs can help later, but the absence of eggs at one time point does not establish absence of damaging larvae. This timing problem is different from a laboratory error and cannot be corrected merely by applying a lower treatment threshold. [10]

## A blood-feeding worm changes the urgency

Haemonchus contortus, the barber’s pole worm, feeds on blood in the abomasum, the ruminant’s fourth stomach compartment. Substantial infection can cause anemia, weakness, swelling under the jaw and death; diarrhea need not be prominent. SCOPS notes that this worm’s high egg production can rapidly increase contamination of pasture when conditions allow development. A count helps assess shedding, but the animal’s circulation and oxygen-carrying capacity determine clinical urgency. A pale, weak sheep or goat needs prompt assessment even if a recent group count was low. Other conditions can also cause anemia or swelling, so identifying Haemonchus and assessing blood loss are complementary tasks. A normal-looking fecal pellet does not rule out a serious blood-feeding infection. [11]

## FAMACHA assesses anemia rather than counting worms

FAMACHA is a trained visual scoring system comparing the color of the lower eyelid’s inner surface with a standardized card. It was developed to recognize anemia associated with Haemonchus, not to measure eggs or diagnose every intestinal parasite. A United States validation included 847 sheep and 537 goats from 39 farms and compared scores with packed cell volume. How the investigators defined an abnormal score changed the balance between missing anemia and flagging animals that were not anemic. This is why a photograph, memory of a color or an egg count cannot substitute for proper training and clinical context. Diarrhea-producing worms may cause substantial disease without pale eyelids, while anemia can have causes other than Haemonchus. [12]

## Good field tools still depend on their users

A later study assessed FAMACHA use by trained producers in 552 sheep and 676 goats across southeastern United States and Puerto Rican farms. It supported the tool’s practical value but also showed that accuracy depended on the species and the definition used for anemia. Some anemic animals were not identified by the chosen eye-score rule. An animal with concerning behavior, poor condition or weakness therefore needs reassessment even when its recorded score appears reassuring. Training, an appropriate original chart, repeated observation and veterinary review help maintain performance. The study’s result is evidence for a useful field aid, not proof that every owner score can safely exclude anemia or replace blood testing when the clinical findings disagree. [13]

## Finding out which worms produced the eggs

Many gastrointestinal strongyle eggs look alike under routine microscopy. Beef + Lamb New Zealand’s Wormwise guidance distinguishes the total egg count from tests identifying the worm mixture. A laboratory larval culture allows eggs to develop so the resulting larvae can be classified, usually to genus rather than exact species. Different organisms develop and survive processing differently, so reported percentages should not be treated as exact internal worm proportions. Parasite-specific PCR detects selected DNA targets and can distinguish important species when the assay is validated for them. These methods are particularly helpful after treatment, when the question becomes which parasites survived. They add identification to the count; they do not automatically establish how much tissue damage those parasites caused in an individual animal. [14]

## Sequencing can describe a community, with qualifications

Redman and colleagues validated a DNA-sequencing method for the ovine nemabiome, meaning the community of roundworm species. They tested known mixtures of eight important species and used correction factors to reduce species-representation bias. Applied to archived material from 99 United Kingdom sheep farms, the method revealed substantial differences between farms and between ewe and lamb groups. This is more informative than labeling every similar-looking egg “strongyle,” but it still measures relative representation in the processed material. Sample preparation, the genetic target and analytical corrections affect that representation. A result stating that one species comprises most detected sequences is not a direct count of adult worms and cannot, by itself, identify which individual transmitted infection to another. [15]

## What a modern fecal egg count reduction test asks

Anthelmintic resistance is the inherited ability of worms to survive an otherwise effective medicine. A fecal egg count reduction test, or FECRT, compares identified animals’ egg shedding before and after treatment to assess efficacy. The World Association for the Advancement of Veterinary Parasitology’s 2023 guideline uses expected efficacy appropriate to the host, parasite and drug, with statistical uncertainty around the reduction. Research testing targets smaller efficacy losses than the more practical clinical protocol. The protocols use different grey zones, preserving uncertainty rather than forcing a yes-or-no label. For most ruminant strongyles the stated target is 99%, but interpreting a result also requires enough eggs actually counted and an appropriate design. Sampling time depends on the treatment and biology. A percentage calculated from two group means alone cannot establish resistance without considering those conditions and alternative causes of reduced efficacy. [16]

## A quick treatment check and a resistance investigation differ

SCOPS separates a practical drench check from a more structured efficacy investigation and advises discussing testing with the veterinarian or adviser. A post-treatment count can flag continuing egg shedding, but interpreting failure also requires the pretreatment situation and correct timing. Poor administration, an inappropriate product, storage problems, inaccurate weight estimation and resistant parasites can produce superficially similar concerns. A full investigation documents what was given and how, then uses a suitable laboratory method and follow-up plan. Neither persistent diarrhea nor a nonzero count alone tells which explanation applies. Repeatedly giving another treatment without understanding the first result can delay diagnosis and increase selection pressure. A sick animal’s immediate care and the flock’s resistance investigation should proceed together. [17, 26]

## Documented resistance outcome: twelve German sheep farms

A 2024 investigation applied the revised FECRT framework on twelve farms in northeastern Germany and combined it with nemabiome analysis. Using one statistical package, resistance was detected on seven of eight farms tested with fenbendazole, eight of eight tested with ivermectin and eight of twelve tested with moxidectin. The parasites surviving treatment differed among farms and included several strongyle species. Classifications did not agree perfectly when different analytical frameworks were compared. These findings document a substantial problem in the investigated farms, rather than a prevalence estimate for every German flock. They show why a familiar brand name or a previous success elsewhere cannot guarantee current efficacy, and why identifying the surviving species can clarify what a reduction test means. [18]

## A larger field survey found failures across treatment classes

A separate German field study evaluated 253 treatments in 223 small-ruminant flocks using pooled reduction testing. Reduced effectiveness occurred in every examined treatment class, although the study could not assess some less frequently represented drugs reliably. Haemonchus frequently survived unsuccessful treatments, but non-Haemonchus worms also contributed. Treatments had been chosen by farmers with their local veterinarians, and the investigators applied inclusion criteria intended to reduce avoidable confounding. Even so, an observational survey of submitted treatments does not identify a single best product for every sheep or goat enterprise. Its practical outcome was evidence that efficacy needed checking on the actual farm. A general recommendation based only on the species name, country or historical treatment calendar would have missed that variation. [19]

## Real outbreak: an anemic Scottish ewe and her flock

A 2026 report described a 2024 outbreak in a sixteen-animal pedigree sheep flock in southeastern Scotland. One thin ewe developed severe pallor, swelling beneath the jaw and marked anemia; its count exceeded 10,000 EPG, with specialized staining identifying 98% of eggs as Haemonchus. Other ewes were also affected. Clinical assessment, targeted treatment and follow-up testing were combined with sequencing to investigate the groups’ worm communities. Seven weeks later, all ewes had body-condition scores of 2–3, and the initially sick ewe’s eyelid score had improved from 5 to 2; six flockmates still scored 3. No further health issues were reported for the remainder of 2024 or the 2025 grazing season. The documented recovery followed an integrated investigation; neither a high count alone nor a single proposed source explained every aspect of the outbreak. [20]

## Five-year outcome: reducing treatments without ignoring performance

In Scotland, Kenyon and colleagues compared four lamb-treatment strategies over five consecutive grazing seasons. By 2010, estimated ivermectin efficacy in the group treated every four weeks had fallen to 62%, compared with 86% in the individually performance-targeted group and 86% in the strategic-treatment group. Lamb body weight in those latter two groups remained similar to the frequent-treatment group. In contrast, waiting for flock clinical signs was associated with lighter lambs in the first three years. These outcomes support monitored selective approaches while showing the cost of waiting too long. They do not justify withholding care from an ill lamb. The trial measured the combined strategy in its own setting; its percentages are not guaranteed outcomes for another climate, parasite community or product. [21]

## Refugia preserve susceptible worms without abandoning sick animals

Refugia are the parts of a parasite population not exposed to a particular treatment, including worms in appropriately untreated animals and stages on pasture. SCOPS explains that retaining susceptible parasites can slow the increase of resistant ones, because survivors of treatment do not become the only parents of the next generation. This is an ecological management principle, not an instruction to leave a weak or anemic animal untreated. Selective treatment requires reliable monitoring, adequate nutrition, attention to growth and a plan for rapid reassessment. Egg counts, clinical observations and farm-specific efficacy evidence help identify where treatment is justified. The aim is to preserve effective medicines while protecting welfare; pursuing a zero-egg result in every animal at every sampling can undermine that longer-term objective. [22]

## Coccidia are a separate diagnostic question

Coccidia such as Eimeria are single-celled parasites, not gastrointestinal worms. They release oocysts, which may be reported as oocysts per gram rather than worm EPG. Merck’s goat guidance emphasizes the animal’s age, clinical findings, species of Eimeria and, when needed, intestinal pathology. Some species are much more damaging than others, and substantial shedding can occur without matching clinical severity. A routine strongyle count should not be silently relabeled a coccidiosis test, and a general worm treatment does not automatically address coccidia. Goat kids with severe weakness or diarrhea need a broader investigation, potentially including bacterial, nutritional and other parasitic causes. Sheep and goats also have different host-associated Eimeria species, so an organism’s name and host matter. [23]

## Necropsy example: fatal coccidiosis without classic diarrhea

An Argentinian report described an Angora-goat outbreak in a herd containing fourteen adult goats and seventeen kids. Three approximately one-month-old kids died within 24–36 hours of losing appetite, becoming weak and lying down. A fourth, moribund kid was euthanized for necropsy. Intestinal lesions and microscopic parasite stages supported mixed Eimeria arloingi and Eimeria ninakohlyakimovae infection; feces from the thirteen remaining asymptomatic kids were also examined. Diarrhea had not been a conspicuous clinical sign. The publication does not establish a subsequent recovery rate for those survivors. The case illustrates why sudden losses need pathology as well as fecal counting. In a welfare or forensic investigation, the parasite result must be considered with lesions, timing and documented conditions; it cannot alone establish neglect or responsibility. [24]

## Use local evidence and record what changed

A 2026 Australian dairy-goat survey examined 1,028 samples from 68 herds and used sequencing on a subset. Strongylid egg shedding was highly uneven: 30% of sampled goats contributed 80% of the total egg output. Age, climate and management history were associated with counts, and several important worm species were identified. This was a cross-sectional survey, not proof that every positive goat was clinically ill or that any one intervention caused an outcome. It reinforces the need for local, species-specific interpretation. A useful record includes animal or group identity, collection date, EPG and method, clinical observations, treatment details and planned reassessment. Follow-up asks whether health, performance and efficacy improved, rather than whether a number crossed a universal threshold. [25]

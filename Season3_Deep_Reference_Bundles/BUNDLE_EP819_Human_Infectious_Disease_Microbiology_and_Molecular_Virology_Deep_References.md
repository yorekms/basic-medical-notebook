# H017: Urine bacterial culture

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Clinical microbiology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 5)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When bacteria grow from a urine sample, how do we decide whether they explain the illness?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Acute illness can take priority over culture completion

Systemic illness or deterioration needs clinical assessment while laboratory work proceeds.

## A culture asks a different question from a urine dipstick

A urine bacterial culture asks which bacteria can grow from a urine specimen under the laboratory's conditions. It can help investigate symptoms and guide treatment when bacterial infection is suspected. Urinalysis answers related but different questions, such as whether white blood cells or blood are present. A clinician combines the history, examination and laboratory findings rather than diagnosing every urinary complaint from one result. The location of illness also matters: infection confined to the bladder differs from infection involving the kidneys or bloodstream. A culture can identify an organism without locating the infection by itself. This chapter concerns human testing, predominantly in adults. Children, pregnancy, urinary catheters and altered urinary anatomy have particular assessment pathways, and their findings should not simply be interpreted using assumptions developed for a healthy adult with straightforward bladder symptoms. [1]

## The specimen must represent the urinary tract

Most people provide a clean catch specimen in a sterile container, following the collection instructions supplied by the clinic or laboratory. Some specimens are obtained by a clinician through a catheter or another medically indicated route. The collection method belongs on the request because it changes the likelihood and meaning of contamination. The container's label, collection time and any recent antibiotics are also important. Routine cultures often need about 24 to 48 hours for initial results, although identification and further testing may extend the process. That biological growth time is different from how urgently the person needs assessment. The patient should ask how to return and store a home collected sample and how results will be communicated. A poorly labeled or incorrectly handled specimen can produce an answer that is difficult to connect reliably to the original clinical question. [2]

## Colonies turn growth into a concentration

A laboratory places a measured amount of urine onto growth medium and incubates it under defined conditions. A colony is visible growth arising from a viable microbial unit. Reporting colony forming units per milliliter, abbreviated CFU/mL, relates the number of colonies to the sampled volume. The UK microbiology standard gives the example that one colony from a one microliter inoculum corresponds to 1,000 CFU/mL. A hypothetical plate with 50 colonies from that volume therefore represents 50,000 CFU/mL. These are teaching numbers, not a diagnosis. The calculation also exposes a detection limit: sampling a tiny volume can miss organisms present at low concentrations. The medium, incubation conditions and amount examined all influence what can be found. No growth means no growth detected by that method, rather than proof that every possible microorganism is absent. [3]

## Identification and susceptibility testing follow growth

Once an organism has grown, the laboratory decides which isolates need identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. An isolate is a particular organism separated from the original specimen. Mayo's urine culture service describes susceptibility testing appropriate to the organism and specimen source under its laboratory procedures. A report is therefore not necessarily a test of every available antibiotic against every colony. Some findings may need clarification, additional identification or a new specimen before a clinically useful susceptibility result can be produced. Preliminary growth information and a completed identification report can arrive at different times. The reader should distinguish the laboratory's statement about an isolate from the clinician's judgment about whether that isolate caused the symptoms. A technically reliable susceptibility result can still be irrelevant if the sample grew a contaminant or if bacteria are present without symptomatic infection. [4]

## Susceptible does not mean guaranteed clinical cure

Antimicrobial susceptibility categories translate laboratory measurements into predictions under specified conditions. EUCAST defines S as susceptible with a standard regimen and I as susceptible with increased exposure; R indicates a high likelihood of failure even with increased exposure. Under this system, I is not simply another spelling of resistant. Exposure includes how the drug reaches the infection site as well as its administration. Laboratories using another standard may use category labels differently, so the reporting system matters. These are professional interpretation rules, not instructions for a patient to increase a dose. A susceptible result does not guarantee recovery, because the diagnosis, infection site, treatment delivery and any unresolved obstruction or other focus still matter. The useful report connects the organism and its susceptibility with the patient's actual problem and the clinician's treatment plan. [5]

## Contamination is a measurable laboratory problem

Bekeris and colleagues examined 14,739 urine specimens across 127 laboratories. Using a study definition based on more than two isolates at specified concentrations, the median institution had a contamination rate of 15 percent, with substantial variation between laboratories. Refrigeration after collection was associated with lower contamination rates. These are historical observations under that study's definition, not a present day failure rate for every laboratory. They show why the pathway between the patient and the incubator matters. Organisms introduced during collection can multiply and create a misleading impression of urinary infection. Clear instructions and appropriate transport are part of the test's quality. If contamination is suspected, a repeat sample may be more useful than trying to choose an antibiotic from a confusing collection of organisms that may never have been present in the bladder. [6]

## Mixed growth and white cells need context

UK Health Security Agency guidance explains that mixed growth often reflects contamination, but the interpretation depends on the patient, collection method and pattern of growth. It should not automatically be labeled either a definite infection or a useless specimen. Pyuria means white blood cells in urine. Sterile pyuria describes persistent white cells without growth on routine culture, a pattern with several possible explanations including recent treatment or organisms not detected by standard methods. A request form should therefore communicate relevant symptoms, pregnancy, catheter use, urinary abnormalities and antimicrobial exposure. The clinician may ask for another specimen, a different test or microbiology advice when the pattern and the illness disagree. Repeatedly treating a laboratory phrase without revisiting the clinical question can expose the patient to treatment while leaving the actual cause of symptoms unaddressed. [7]

## One colony count threshold cannot answer every case

Hooton and colleagues compared voided midstream specimens with catheter obtained bladder urine in healthy premenopausal women with acute uncomplicated cystitis. Escherichia coli in the midstream sample predicted bladder bacteriuria even at 100 CFU/mL, with a positive predictive value of 93 percent at that threshold. Enterococci and group B streptococci in voided samples did not behave in the same way. The organism and population therefore changed the interpretation of the count. These results should not be transferred unchanged to an asymptomatic person, a child or someone with a long term catheter. They explain why the familiar figure of 100,000 CFU/mL cannot be used as an absolute dividing line for all symptomatic infection. A lower count can matter in the appropriate setting, while a higher count still requires a clinical explanation. [8]

## An antibiotic can rapidly change what the culture detects

John and colleagues prospectively studied adults receiving a first antibiotic dose for upper or febrile urinary infection. Cultures were positive before treatment in 86 of 87 patients, compared with 26 of 75 first urine samples obtained afterwards. Later specimens had still lower positivity. Because fewer patients supplied each successive specimen, the changing denominators must remain visible. The study demonstrates that a negative culture after treatment cannot be read as equivalent to a negative culture before treatment. It does not justify delaying urgent care to obtain an ideal specimen, or stopping antibiotics without advice to make a later test positive. Instead, the collection time and treatment history should be supplied so that the clinician and laboratory can interpret what the method had a reasonable chance of detecting. [9]

## Bacteria without symptoms can be colonization

Asymptomatic bacteriuria means bacteria detected in urine at defined counts without symptoms attributable to urinary infection. The IDSA guideline stresses that this can occur whether or not pyuria is present. In many groups, including healthy nonpregnant adults, people with diabetes and those with long term catheters, routinely treating this finding offers no established benefit and can cause harm through antimicrobial exposure and resistance. Important exceptions include pregnancy and selected endoscopic urological procedures involving mucosal injury, where screening and targeted treatment may be appropriate. These exceptions require their own care pathways. A positive culture is consequently a finding to interpret, not an automatic prescription. In an older person with confusion but no local urinary symptoms or systemic signs, other explanations need evaluation rather than assuming that commonly present bacteriuria must be the cause. [10]

## A randomized trial separated clearing bacteria from preventing illness

Harding and colleagues randomized women with diabetes and asymptomatic bacteriuria to antimicrobial treatment or placebo. During an average follow up of 27 months, at least one symptomatic urinary infection occurred in 23 of 55 treated women and 20 of 50 placebo recipients, approximately 42 and 40 percent respectively. Treatment did not demonstrate the expected prevention of symptomatic complications, despite targeting the bacteria found in urine. This distinction is central to understanding a laboratory endpoint: eliminating detectable growth is not automatically the same achievement as improving the patient's health. The study concerned women with diabetes who had no urinary symptoms at enrollment, not patients with a painful or febrile infection. Its results support avoiding treatment simply to make an asymptomatic culture negative, while leaving symptomatic illness and the guideline's specific exceptions to appropriate clinical assessment. [11]

## Hospital observations also found no clinical advantage from treating the result alone

Petty and colleagues reviewed 2,733 hospitalized patients with asymptomatic bacteriuria across 46 hospitals. Most received antibiotics. After adjustment, treatment was associated with a longer period in hospital after urine testing, without an improvement in the measured clinical outcomes. The reported increase in length of stay was 37 percent. Because this was an observational study, it cannot prove that antibiotics themselves caused every additional hospital day; unmeasured differences between patients may contribute. Nevertheless, it provides no support for assuming that treatment of an isolated positive culture is harmless or automatically beneficial. The outcome of interest is the patient's recovery, complications and treatment burden. A culture report that triggers unnecessary medication can change care even when no urinary disease requiring that medication has been established. [12]

## Repeat testing should answer an unresolved question

The AUA CUA SUFU guideline for recurrent uncomplicated urinary infection in women advises against routine post treatment test of cure cultures when symptoms have resolved. It recommends repeat culture when symptoms persist after antimicrobial treatment, and assessment for alternative causes when symptoms remain despite microbiological cure. Those are different situations. Repeatedly finding bacteria in someone who feels well can encourage unnecessary treatment, while persistent pain or urgency deserves an explanation even if the culture becomes negative. The guideline applies to its defined population and does not replace pregnancy or complicated infection pathways. A useful follow up plan states what improvement is expected, how results will be reviewed and which persistent or new symptoms require reassessment. Testing is most informative when the answer can change that plan. [13]

## Kidney infection changes the clinical priority

NICE guidance for acute pyelonephritis, meaning kidney infection, recommends obtaining urine for culture and susceptibility testing before antibiotics when feasible, then reviewing treatment using the results. A person with possible sepsis or another serious complication may need hospital assessment. The culture is intended to guide treatment; it is not a reason to leave an acutely ill person untreated until bacteria have grown. Vomiting, dehydration, pregnancy or increased risk of complications can change the care setting and investigation. Clinicians also assess whether obstruction or another problem requires attention beyond antimicrobial selection. This explains why the same organism on a report may lead to different management in a stable person with bladder symptoms and someone with systemic illness. The laboratory result contributes to the decision but cannot grade clinical stability from a distance. [14]

## A published case needed a different specimen and growth conditions

A 2024 report described a young adult with a ureteric stone and pyelonephritis, including pus in the kidney's collecting system. Routine urine culture remained negative. Clinicians relieved the obstruction, and material drained from the infected system was cultured using additional conditions. Blood and pus cultures identified Actinotignum schaalii, a slow growing bacterium that can be missed by routine aerobic urine culture. Identification used laboratory systems including MALDI TOF mass spectrometry, which recognizes a characteristic protein pattern. The patient improved clinically, inflammatory markers fell and he was discharged on continued treatment. The reported discharge improvement should not be expanded into an unreported long term cure. The case shows how a negative routine specimen can coexist with a genuine infection and obstruction. It also shows why finding the organism and addressing the obstructed system were separate contributions to care. [15]

## A urinary isolate can connect to a larger infection

A separate 2024 report described a man whose urine and blood cultures both grew Aerococcus urinae. Frequent urination was present, but painful urination was not. A heart murmur and other findings led the team to investigate endocarditis, infection involving a heart valve, which was supported by echocardiography. Treatment was changed according to the susceptibility result, and after two weeks of antibiotics the mitral valve was replaced. The report states that he recovered from the operation. That outcome belongs to combined clinical assessment, cultures, imaging, antimicrobial care and surgery; urine culture alone neither diagnosed the valve infection nor produced recovery. The case does not mean that everyone with this urinary organism has endocarditis. It demonstrates why a patient's wider signs and a matching bloodstream isolate can change the importance assigned to what initially looks like a urinary result. [16]

## More sensitive growth methods create new interpretation questions

Price and colleagues compared standard culture with expanded quantitative urine culture in catheter obtained specimens from 150 adult women attending a urogynecology service. The expanded approach used different conditions to detect organisms missed by the routine method. Standard culture missed 67 percent of the organisms classified as uropathogens in the comparison. This was a selected population and a comparison between laboratory protocols, not proof that two thirds of all ordinary cultures are clinically wrong. Greater detection does not automatically establish that every additional organism causes symptoms or should be eradicated. The study is useful because it exposes the boundary between what a method can grow and what explains a person's illness. New techniques need evidence about clinical outcomes and interpretation as well as evidence that they detect more microbial life. [17]

## Forensic interpretation requires more than growth after death

Forensic microbiology can contribute to an investigation of death from infection, but a postmortem specimen has additional limitations. The ESGFOR consensus explains that microbial growth may represent genuine infection, contamination during collection or movement of organisms through tissues after death. Interpretation therefore needs the clinical record, the sampling site and timing, and the anatomical and microscopic findings. Applied to a urine culture, this means that growth after death cannot independently establish that a urinary infection caused death, when it began or whether it was preventable. A suspected urinary source should be assessed alongside kidney and urinary tract findings and any evidence from other specimens. This is a limit on inference, not a reason to discard microbiology. Preserving the original report, collection details and uncertainty makes the result more useful in reconstructing what the available evidence actually supports. [18]

## Symptoms can demand attention before a culture report is complete

NHS guidance advises urgent assessment for urinary symptoms accompanied by features such as high or low temperature, shivering, pain in the back beneath the ribs, rapid deterioration or important vulnerability, including pregnancy. Confusion, marked drowsiness or difficulty speaking can require emergency help. A dark color or strong smell alone can have other explanations, including insufficient fluid intake, so the symptom pattern matters. The practical task is to report the actual change and relevant risk factors rather than wait for the laboratory to attach a positive or negative label. For a stable investigation, keep the collection method, date, recent antibiotics, organism, count and susceptibility report together. The result becomes most useful when someone explains whether it supports infection, colonization, contamination or an unresolved question, and how that interpretation changes the plan. [19]

# H018: Blood cultures

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Clinical microbiology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 1)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-06
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational treatise. Independent clinical review pending. Adult and pediatric collection requirements must remain distinct.

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When a culture bottle grows an organism, did it come from an infection in the bloodstream, from the skin during collection, or from an analytical complication that needs a different explanation?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Emergency when sepsis is suspected

Suspected sepsis needs emergency assessment now rather than waiting for culture growth.

## Look for an organism without reducing sepsis to a bottle

Blood cultures investigate whether recoverable microorganisms are present in a blood sample. They can help identify the cause of a bloodstream infection and guide later antimicrobial decisions. Sepsis is a different concept: a life-threatening response to infection with systemic consequences. CDC describes sepsis as a clinical assessment involving symptoms, physical findings, and tests for infection or organ damage. Its causes include bacterial, fungal, and viral infections. A culture is therefore one contributor to that assessment, not a detector for every cause of sepsis. The clinical question should explain why sampling is useful, rather than treating every fever as the same indication or a negative bottle as proof that serious infection is absent. [1]

## Why collection is part of the test

The amount of blood cultured strongly influences recovery because the organisms may be sparse in the sampled circulation. The IDSA and ASM 2024 guide emphasizes blood volume, appropriate bottles, and more than one culture set in adults being evaluated for bloodstream infection. A set means bottles arising from one collection event, not every bottle counted as an independent event. Children require age- and weight-appropriate sampling rather than an adult volume copied down a ward. Peripheral venipuncture is generally preferred because catheter-drawn samples have a greater contamination risk. Prompt transport and correct incubation handling complete the collection pathway. These are tasks for trained clinical staff using the local validated procedure, not a home sampling protocol. [2]

## Two opposite errors can start at the same needle

Too little blood can create a false negative by reducing the chance that the sample contains a recoverable organism. Introducing skin microorganisms can create a false positive for bloodstream infection even though the bottle genuinely grows something. CDC's adult blood culture quality tool treats both errors as patient safety problems: missed organisms can delay correct treatment, while contamination can lead to unnecessary antibiotics and longer hospitalization. Thus a collection described only as successful because a bottle reached the laboratory is not the whole story. Programs monitor adequate collection and contamination to improve the diagnostic process. The quality of the sample is evidence about how much confidence a later positive or negative result deserves. [3]

## How a bottle announces growth

A culture bottle supplies a growth environment and an instrument watches for a signal of microbial activity. In the FDA-reviewed BacT/ALERT PF Plus system, microorganisms metabolize nutrients and generate carbon dioxide. Dissolved carbon dioxide changes a colorimetric sensor in the bottle; reflected light is monitored to detect the change. The signal is an indication to investigate growth, not an organism's full name and not an antibiotic prescription. The cited bottle formulation also uses adsorbent resins, materials that bind some antimicrobial drugs, intended to improve antimicrobial neutralization. That feature does not guarantee recovery after treatment. Bottle formulation, organism growth requirements, instrument algorithms, and specimen quality still matter. Other platforms can use different detection designs, so this mechanism should remain attached to its named system. [4]

## A positive bottle opens another investigation

The laboratory examines positive material and continues identification and susceptibility work. A rapid molecular panel can accelerate some of this journey. BIOFIRE BCID2, for example, tests positive blood culture material for specified organisms and resistance genes, using nucleic acid amplification. Its instructions require interpretation with the Gram stain, the microscopic staining pattern and appearance of organisms. The panel is limited to its targets and can miss organisms in mixed cultures. A resistance gene not detected does not establish antibiotic susceptibility: resistance can arise through other mechanisms. Conversely, nucleic acid from nonviable organisms in the medium can produce a misleading detection. Subculture and standard susceptibility testing remain necessary. Rapid identification adds evidence; it does not erase the need to reconcile the whole microbiological picture. [5]

## The name of a skin organism is not a verdict

Commensals are organisms that commonly live on the body without causing disease in that location. Recovering one from a culture can indicate contamination, but the ASM Laboratory Practices Subcommittee cautions that familiar contaminants can also cause genuine infection, including infections involving vascular lines, prosthetic valves, and implanted devices. Patient vulnerability and the pattern across collected sets therefore matter. Laboratory contamination metrics use predefined rules for quality reporting, and those rules do not replace individual clinical interpretation. A label useful for monitoring a department's collection practice may be insufficient to settle one person's diagnosis. The laboratory and treating team must distinguish a probable collection contaminant from a clinically meaningful isolate instead of discarding a result solely because its name looks familiar. [6]

## An antibiotic can change what remains recoverable

Collection before antimicrobial treatment is desirable when feasible. Cheng and colleagues studied paired cultures in patients with severe manifestations of sepsis and found that beginning empirical antimicrobial therapy reduced the sensitivity of cultures collected soon afterward. This original diagnostic study supports a practical point without supplying a universal performance percentage for all patients: a negative culture after treatment is less reassuring than the same negative label might appear. The result depends on what was present and recoverable at collection. Documenting the timing of antibiotics is therefore part of interpretation. The study does not justify withholding needed treatment in a deteriorating patient merely to obtain an ideal specimen or wait for a laboratory opportunity. [7]

## Do not make treatment wait for microbial growth

The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends collecting blood cultures as soon as possible and ideally before antimicrobials in adults with possible, probable, or definite sepsis or septic shock. It also states that sepsis is a clinical diagnosis that cannot be established or excluded by one diagnostic test. Sepsis and septic shock require immediate treatment and resuscitation. The guideline distinguishes degrees of infection likelihood and shock rather than assigning every febrile presentation one identical timetable. Once microbiology and susceptibility information become available, they can help the clinical team reassess and narrow treatment appropriately. A culture's role is to support that evolving decision process; it is not permission to delay urgent care until a bottle becomes positive. [8]

## When to seek help and what to ask next

An infection accompanied by new confusion, severe discomfort, clammy skin, a weak pulse, or shortness of breath can signal sepsis. Seek emergency assessment now when sepsis is suspected; do not wait at home for culture results. Tell the team about worsening infection and recent treatment. [1]

Useful questions afterward include whether appropriate sets and volume were obtained, whether antibiotics preceded collection, and how the isolate fits other findings. Some causes of culture-negative endocarditis, an infection involving the heart's inner surface, require targeted serology, meaning antibody testing, or molecular investigation rather than simply repeating the same routine culture indefinitely. [2]

# H019: Stool pathogen testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and parasitology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can a stool test establish about diarrhea when finding a germ and proving the cause are different questions?*

## The question comes before the container

Stool pathogen testing investigates whether bacteria, viruses or parasites may explain an intestinal illness. A pathogen is an organism capable of causing disease, but detecting one does not automatically prove it caused every symptom. The 2017 guideline from the Infectious Diseases Society of America, or IDSA, recommends targeted investigation for features such as bloody diarrhea, fever, severe abdominal tenderness or sepsis, and broader testing during suspected outbreaks. Many short, uncomplicated diarrheal illnesses need no extensive laboratory search. This chapter concerns human testing, mainly in United States practice. The purpose is to explain how a result becomes useful evidence alongside the person's illness and exposures, rather than to provide a treatment recipe. [1]

The phrase stool test hides several different examinations. A test for hidden blood, a marker of intestinal inflammation and a pathogen panel do not answer the same question. Even among infection tests, a routine molecular panel has a defined list of targets. UC Davis separates routine protozoal testing from investigations for worm eggs and larvae in people with appropriate risks. A person who has lived in an endemic area or has impaired immunity may need an investigation that an ordinary panel does not include. A negative report therefore means that the targets sought were not detected under that method's conditions. It does not establish that every infectious or noninfectious cause of diarrhea has been excluded. [23]

## Following the specimen into the laboratory

The collection process can change what reaches the laboratory. The supplied clean, leakproof container helps prevent contamination by toilet water, urine or soil. Collection time, patient identity and the requested examination belong with the sample. CDC's parasitology guidance emphasizes that preservatives are selected for a purpose: material preserved well for microscopic appearance may be unsuitable for a molecular assay. Follow the collecting laboratory's kit instructions rather than adding household chemicals or choosing a preservative independently. Tell the ordering team about recent antimicrobials and other medicines. This information may explain reduced recovery or guide selection of a different test, and should never become a reason to postpone urgent care. [2]

Culture means attempting to grow living bacteria on selected laboratory media. The organisms sought, atmosphere, incubation and follow-up identification depend on the laboratory's service. Texas public health laboratory guidance, for example, lists several enteric bacteria and notes that outbreak investigations can alter the screening approach. Its catalog also explains that improper transport and storage reduce recovery. A culture result may take longer than a molecular panel because growth takes time. Its particular value is a living isolate, an identified bacterial population that can undergo further characterization or antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The label routine culture is not a promise that all organisms causing diarrhea were sought or would grow. [3]

A multiplex molecular panel searches for several genetic targets in one run. PCR, polymerase chain reaction, copies selected DNA sequences until an instrument can detect them; viral RNA targets require conversion into DNA first. A prospective FilmArray study examined 1,556 specimens and detected at least one potential pathogen in 832. Among those positive specimens, 262 contained more than one target. That result illustrates why a long positive list needs interpretation: it does not rank the organisms by responsibility for the illness. The study examined a particular platform, target list and clinical population. Its detection frequency is neither the prevalence of infection in the general public nor the probability that every detected organism caused disease. [4]

## Antigens microscopy and organisms beyond the panel

An antigen is a recognizable component of an organism. Antigen assays use antibodies as molecular recognition tools, sometimes creating a colored signal in a rapid cartridge or a measurable signal in a laboratory plate. Direct fluorescent antibody microscopy attaches a glowing label to selected parasite structures, helping the observer recognize them. CDC describes these approaches for Giardia and Cryptosporidium, while warning that preparation and preservation must match the kit. These methods differ from simply looking for anything parasite-like on a routine slide. Antigen detection does not usually produce a living organism for later culture or a detailed genetic fingerprint. Its clinical usefulness depends on the organism, specimen and validated assay. [5]

Norovirus illustrates why the organism matters when comparing methods. Its genome is RNA. CDC prefers reverse transcription quantitative PCR, usually shortened to RT qPCR, for laboratory detection. Reverse transcription makes DNA from the viral RNA; quantitative PCR then measures the accumulating signal. Some stool panels include common norovirus groups. Rapid antigen enzyme immunoassays exist, but CDC notes their lower sensitivity and advises against replacing molecular testing with them in outbreak investigations. A negative rapid test can therefore leave a different degree of uncertainty than a suitable molecular test. The speed of a visible result alone does not describe how reliably an assay detects its intended target. [6]

Cryptosporidium is a tiny intestinal parasite whose oocysts, its environmentally resistant stages, can be identified by specialized staining, fluorescent antibody testing, antigen methods or PCR. CDC's 2025 guidance explains that shedding is intermittent, so collection over three days may improve detection. A laboratory's general microscopy request may not automatically include every specialized method. Routine diagnostic detection also differs from the additional genetic work needed to distinguish species or subtypes during an epidemiological investigation. This matters when people ask whether an infection came from a swimming pool, another person or an animal. A positive diagnostic test alone rarely supplies that complete chain of evidence. [7]

Giardia also may be absent from the particular bowel movement sampled despite ongoing infection. CDC recommends three stool specimens collected over several days for accurate investigation and recommends retesting after treatment only if symptoms continue. Available methods include fluorescent antibody microscopy, antigen assays and molecular testing. The word cyst describes one transmissible stage, rather than a cyst in the person's tissues. Finding Giardia in stool confirms infection, while the clinician still considers the full explanation for continuing symptoms. Repeated collection in this setting addresses intermittent shedding; it should not be confused with automatically repeating a broad panel every day or testing indefinitely until every result becomes negative. [8]

Cyclospora requires another specific question. CDC's September 2026 guidance notes that not all gastrointestinal panels include it and routine parasite examinations may not detect it unless requested. The oocysts can be sparse and shed intermittently. Molecular methods search for its DNA; special staining and ultraviolet fluorescence provide other recognition methods. Its structures naturally fluoresce under appropriate illumination, unlike the common Cryptosporidium species. Prolonged diarrhea, travel and a suspected food-associated cluster can prompt the clinician to request this additional investigation. A report saying parasite testing negative is incomplete without knowing which organisms and methods it covered. The history can reveal that the right target was never examined. [9]

## C difficile requires a clinical diagnosis

Clostridioides difficile, often shortened to C difficile or C diff, can be carried without causing the current illness. A PCR assay typically detects a gene associated with toxin production; it does not directly measure toxin already present in the stool. A glutamate dehydrogenase, or GDH, antigen test detects a bacterial component that is not limited to toxin-producing strains. A toxin enzyme immunoassay looks for toxin proteins but is less sensitive than molecular detection. CDC also warns that toxin can deteriorate during delayed, unrefrigerated handling. These differences explain why laboratories use combinations of tests, and why a PCR positive but toxin negative report requires assessment rather than an automatic diagnosis or dismissal. [11]

The diagnostic guideline from the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America, or SHEA, and IDSA identifies unexplained, newly occurring diarrhea with at least three unformed stools in 24 hours as the preferred testing population. Laboratories may use a multistep algorithm incorporating toxin, or an appropriate molecular approach when agreed submission criteria select likely cases. Age changes interpretation: routine testing of infants is discouraged because carriage is common. For children aged one to two years, alternative explanations should first be excluded. These boundaries concern who should be tested and how a result should be interpreted; they are not rules for a parent to diagnose or treat a child independently. [12]

Polage and colleagues followed 1,416 hospitalized adults tested at one academic center. Of 293 PCR positive patients, 162 had no toxin detected by the clinical immunoassay. None of those 162 developed the study's C difficile related complications, compared with ten of 131 toxin positive patients. One toxin negative patient had recurrent infection considered a contributor to death; the group was not risk free. Outcomes overall resembled those of test negative patients despite limited treatment. This observational study supports caution about overdiagnosis, but does not prove that every toxin negative patient can safely remain untreated. Disease severity, test limitations and the particular patient's course remain necessary to the decision. [10]

## Multiple positives and repeat results

Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli, or EPEC, is another result whose meaning varies. A 2022 retrospective study of children and adults associated atypical EPEC with presentations ranging from no symptoms to severe or prolonged diarrhea. Some people remained positive for months to years or experienced repeated infection. Higher stool bacterial loads were associated with symptoms, but did not reliably predict the disease or its severity. The word atypical describes a bacterial subgroup, not an unusual patient. A positive EPEC result should therefore be interpreted with symptoms, other detected organisms and competing causes. It should not become an explanation for every subsequent abdominal complaint merely because its name remains in the medical record. [13]

During a 2013 Rhode Island shigellosis outbreak, Prakash and colleagues detected ten Shigella or enteroinvasive E coli targets by panel testing, whereas culture identified six Shigella sonnei cases. Independent PCR and sequencing supported the additional molecular detections. The panel itself could not distinguish Shigella from enteroinvasive E coli, a closely related target group. The investigation demonstrates that culture negative does not always mean the molecular result was false. It also shows why species resolution and recoverable isolates mattered for the public health definitions then in use. Individual recovery outcomes were not the focus of this report, so it cannot show that the extra detections improved each patient's eventual health. [14]

A Stanford retrospective study examined follow-up panel testing within four weeks. Of 80 follow-up tests after an initial positive result, 43 still detected the same target. That is a count of tests, not 43 newly infected people. Most follow-up tests after a negative result remained negative. The authors discouraged routine use of this panel as a test of cure. Persisting genetic detection need not mean continuing viable infection, while new symptoms or a specific concern about poor initial sampling can justify a different decision. This study addressed one platform and practice setting. It does not prohibit organism-specific follow-up requested for public health or a carefully reassessed clinical problem. [15]

For C difficile, the guideline advises against routine repeat testing within seven days during the same episode and against testing asymptomatic people as a test of cure. Persistent positivity can outlast clinical improvement. A new deterioration still needs reassessment of dehydration, complications, medicines or another diagnosis. The question is whether another specimen will resolve a new uncertainty, rather than prolong treatment because a sensitive assay remains positive. [12]

## When delay can change the outcome

Shiga toxin producing E coli, or STEC, changes the immediate clinical question. CDC recommends stool culture for E coli O157 together with testing that detects Shiga toxins or their genes to cover non O157 STEC. The result can affect management because antimicrobial treatment may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome, or HUS, and drugs that slow bowel movement should be avoided in STEC infection. HUS involves injury to small blood vessels, breakdown of red blood cells, low platelets and kidney injury. Clinicians assess hydration and blood tests alongside stool findings. A person with bloody diarrhea should seek medical assessment rather than start leftover antibiotics or wait for a panel result before addressing serious illness. [16]

HUS can develop in someone who has had diarrhea even as attention shifts away from the bowel symptoms. CDC identifies warning signs including little or no urine, marked pallor, unexplained bruising, blood in the urine, extreme tiredness and reduced alertness. These warrant immediate medical care. A stool test identifies evidence of an intestinal organism; blood counts and chemistry assess complications affecting the kidneys and circulation. Neither test replaces the other. This is an appropriate situation for urgency in an educational account: a changing person's condition matters more than a reassuring laboratory result obtained earlier. It does not mean that every episode of loose stool represents an emergency. [17]

The spring 2018 United States outbreak linked to romaine lettuce gives these complications a documented scale. CDC's final notice reported 210 infections across 36 states. Among 201 people with information available, 96 were hospitalized, including 27 who developed HUS; five deaths were reported. Whole genome sequencing showed closely related bacterial isolates, while interviews and traceback evidence implicated romaine from the Yuma growing region. These are the reported outcomes of that particular outbreak, not the risk for every person with STEC. Keeping the denominator visible also matters: the hospitalization proportion used the 201 people with available information, rather than assuming complete follow-up for all 210. [18]

Madden and colleagues reported severe colitis with lower gastrointestinal bleeding in which a multiplex stool PCR detected Entamoeba histolytica. Two conventional stool microscopy examinations were negative. The molecular result was repeated because the finding was unusual locally and remained positive; treatment was followed by a rapid clinical response. At a year of follow-up the patient had no features suggesting inflammatory bowel disease and had not redeveloped anemia. The authors considered the PCR useful in reaching the diagnosis promptly. This is a successful individual outcome, not proof that every positive amoebic target is definitive or that molecular testing alone explains all colitis. Symptoms and response supported the laboratory evidence. [21]

## Forensic evidence and the limits of a microbial match

A forensic report described a previously healthy 32-year-old man who died overnight after profuse vomiting. Postmortem culture of gastric contents yielded abundant pure Salmonella Typhimurium variant Copenhagen. The investigators integrated this with histology, toxicology and scene findings to support fatal infectious gastroenteritis. They proposed severe dehydration, electrolyte disturbance and arrhythmia as the most plausible mechanism, but vitreous was not collected and electrolytes were not measured. The food source remained unidentified. This was gastric sampling after death, not a routine living patient's stool test. It demonstrates both the value of microbiology and the need to distinguish an identified organism, an attributed disease and a proposed final physiological mechanism. [20]

The investigation of the 1984 Oregon salmonellosis outbreak eventually combined clinical illness, stool cultures, exposure comparisons and criminal evidence. Torok and colleagues reported 751 people meeting clinical or culture-based case criteria; not every one was culture confirmed. A bacterial strain recovered in the subsequent investigation was indistinguishable from the outbreak strain. The forensic conclusion required the broader investigation, rather than arising from a patient's stool culture alone. Likewise, today's positive Salmonella report cannot identify who contaminated food, establish intent or assign responsibility to a restaurant. A laboratory match can support a link while timing, exposure records, alternative sources and independent evidence determine what that link means. [19]

Whole genome sequencing reads bacterial DNA in far more detail than a routine diagnostic panel. PulseNet uses these comparisons to recognize clusters that may share a source and to investigate antimicrobial resistance. Think of the routine result as identifying the kind of organism sought, while sequencing compares much more of its genetic identity. Even close genetic relatedness is interpreted alongside epidemiology. Specimen identity, collection dates, retained isolates and documented transfers allow investigators to assess whether laboratory findings belong together. A clinical test is useful for care; a forensic or regulatory conclusion may require additional evidence and handling records. Those purposes should remain clear when discussing a result with public health investigators. [24]

## What better testing can and cannot deliver

Faster detection is useful only if it changes an appropriate decision. A randomized trial of 60 children with bloody diarrhea compared rapid multiplex testing with standard investigation. Although the technology produced faster, broader information, the investigators did not find that the advances necessarily improved the measured clinical outcomes. The study was small, so it cannot rule out benefits in every subgroup. Its lesson is that an instrument's speed, diagnostic yield and effect on patient health are separate measurements. When reviewing a result, ask what was sought, whether the specimen was suitable, how the finding fits the illness and what action follows. Those questions keep the test connected to the person it is meant to help. [22]

# H020: Tuberculosis immunologic testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and microbiology
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 5)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07
- **Editorial Status**: Source checked educational draft — independent clinical review pending

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a tuberculosis test remember about infection, and what must clinicians establish before calling that infection active disease?*

### Clinical Urgency & Triage Boundary: Clinical symptoms require assessment

A negative screening test must not delay assessment of a compatible illness.

## What an immune test can establish

Tuberculosis immune testing looks for evidence that a person's immune system recognizes tuberculosis related proteins. The two familiar approaches are the tuberculin skin test, or TST, and blood tests called interferon gamma release assays, or IGRAs. They help investigate infection even when someone feels well. Latent tuberculosis infection means infection without evidence of active tuberculosis disease after an appropriate medical evaluation. People with latent infection do not spread TB to others, although some later develop disease. An immune result alone cannot make that distinction. It does not show where bacteria are located, count them, measure lung damage or establish whether the person is contagious. This chapter concerns human testing, with practical interpretation mainly drawn from United States guidance; veterinary TB tests and other national pathways require their own standards. [1]

Testing works best when it answers a reasoned question about exposure and risk. CDC recommends targeting people with increased likelihood of infection or progression, rather than indiscriminately screening everyone. A hypothetical calculation explains why: among 10,000 people, suppose 100 are infected and a test has 95 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity. It would produce about 95 true positives and 99 false positives. Only about half the positive results would represent infection. These are invented teaching assumptions, not performance claims for a particular IGRA. Sensitivity describes detection among infected people; specificity describes correct exclusion among uninfected people. In a low risk group, even uncommon false positives can be important. The history therefore changes the meaning of the result before any laboratory number is considered. [2]

## How the two tests detect immune memory

An IGRA exposes collected blood cells to selected antigens, meaning recognizable protein fragments, and measures their response. Memory T cells are white blood cells that can respond after previous sensitization. When they recognize the supplied antigens, they may release interferon gamma, an immune signaling protein. QuantiFERON TB Gold Plus measures the amount released, while T SPOT TB counts cells producing a detectable response. Neither method searches the blood for intact TB bacteria. The selected antigens improve discrimination from BCG vaccination and most other mycobacteria, but the test remains an indirect measure. A result also requires responsive cells and appropriate controls. A negative response can reflect absence of infection, recent exposure before immune recognition develops, impaired immunity or a problem affecting the specimen. [3]

The skin test measures a delayed immune reaction to purified protein derivative placed in the skin by a trained professional. The reader measures induration, the firm swelling, rather than the surrounding redness, 48 to 72 hours after placement. Interpretation combines that measurement with risk. Under CDC criteria, at least 5 mm is positive in selected groups such as recent contacts and people with HIV or substantial immune suppression. A 10 mm threshold applies to other specified risk groups, and 15 mm to people without known risk factors. These are United States criteria, not a universal self-reading chart. A person should attend the planned reading even if the site looks unremarkable. Failure to react does not exclude infection or active disease in someone with impaired immunity or serious illness. Correct placement, trained reading and the clinical context all contribute to the result. [4]

## Vaccination timing and specimen handling

BCG is a tuberculosis vaccine commonly given in countries where TB is more prevalent. It can cause a positive conventional skin test, and the appearance of that reaction does not reliably distinguish vaccination from infection. CDC therefore advises risk based evaluation of a positive TST even when BCG was received. An IGRA is generally preferred for a BCG vaccinated person because BCG itself does not induce a positive IGRA. This distinction concerns which antigens the tests use, rather than a blanket statement that one method is always correct. Keep vaccination history with the test record. It is relevant to choosing and interpreting the test, but it should not be used to dismiss symptoms or automatically explain away a positive result without assessment. [5]

After a recent exposure, the immune system may not yet produce a detectable response. A negative result obtained during that window can precede a later positive result. CDC contact investigation guidance calls for repeat testing when the first test was performed before eight to ten weeks had elapsed since the last exposure. Last exposure matters because contact may continue for some time. This planned repeat is not a reason to delay evaluation of symptoms. Young children and people with substantial immune suppression may need additional assessment and preventive management during the window under specialist care. The test date, exposure dates and current clinical findings belong together. Without that timeline, a laboratory report can appear reassuring while answering the question too early. [6]

Blood collection is only the beginning of an IGRA. The sample contains living immune cells, so collection materials, transport and timely processing affect whether those cells can provide an interpretable response. Mayo's QFT4 catalog specifies special handling and processing requirements and identifies situations that can compromise interpretation. These instructions are laboratory and platform specific; the patient should use the collection service arranged by the ordering team. This is different from a chemistry test in which a stable dissolved substance is simply measured later. An indeterminate result may lead the clinician and laboratory to review the sample pathway as well as the person's immune condition. Repeating the same collection problem would not resolve the underlying uncertainty, even if a fresh report appeared in the medical record. [7]

## Understanding controls and uncertain results

QuantiFERON uses a Nil control to measure background interferon gamma and a Mitogen control to test whether the cells can produce an adequate response to a general stimulus. The antigen response is assessed against that background. The manufacturer's interpretation resource explains that high background or an inadequate control response can prevent a useful positive or negative classification. The report may then say indeterminate. It means the test has not resolved the likelihood of infection; it does not establish either that infection is absent or that the person has severe TB. The clinician considers immune suppression, illness and specimen handling before deciding what to do next. The numerical interferon value is a measure of immune response, not a bacterial count or a direct scale of contagiousness. [8]

A second test can be useful for a specific reason. ATS, CDC and IDSA diagnostic guidance suggests confirmatory testing when someone aged five or older who is unlikely to be infected nevertheless has a positive screening test. In that low risk setting, both tests should be positive before the person is considered infected under the recommended approach. This is not a rule that every positive result must be ignored until duplicated, particularly after a significant exposure or in a symptomatic patient. The same guideline emphasizes that neither TST nor IGRA separates latent infection from active disease. The purpose of confirmation is to reduce unnecessary diagnoses and treatment in a population where false positives can outweigh true positives. Clinical evaluation remains necessary when the likelihood of disease is high. [9]

## Repeated results and laboratory investigations

Metcalfe and colleagues studied repeat QuantiFERON Gold In Tube testing on the same patient samples. Among 366 patients with valid paired results, 28, or 8 percent, changed classification between the first and second measurement. Results near the decision boundary were particularly vulnerable to a small numerical change altering the label. Because the same sample was retested, this was not evidence that those people acquired or cleared infection between two blood draws. The study examined an earlier assay generation in a particular screening setting; its percentage cannot be transferred to every current platform. It demonstrates why the exact assay, numerical response and clinical risk are useful when interpreting a change. A conversion or reversion is a description of test results, not a complete biological explanation. [10]

A separate 2012 laboratory investigation reported a sudden increase in positive QuantiFERON Gold In Tube results from about 10 to 31 percent. The team associated the cluster with a particular batch of antigen tubes and compared results using another batch. Among 81 employees retested after initial positive results in the suspect period, 91 percent tested negative with the new batch. The exact manufacturing or chemical cause remained unresolved despite investigation. This was a real quality problem with consequences for staff and follow-up services, rather than proof that all positive tests from all batches are unreliable. Its useful lesson is organizational: an unexpected cluster can justify coordinated laboratory review. An individual should discuss an unexpected result with the clinician, rather than independently decide that it must be a defective test. [11]

## Published cases showing different explanations

Patel and colleagues reported a 61 year old man taking adalimumab, an immune modifying medicine, who developed an ulcerated forearm lesion. A previously negative IGRA became positive, but culture and DNA identification of the skin specimen identified Mycobacterium marinum, a related environmental organism. The authors interpreted the immune result as cross-reactivity, in which shared antigens provoke a response. Treatment had to be adjusted for antimicrobial susceptibility and adverse effects, including diarrhea and blurred vision. The lesion resolved during the reported course, and the ocular symptoms resolved after five months of treatment. This was not an uncomplicated response to the first antibiotic. The case shows why identifying an organism at the diseased site can change the meaning of an immune test. It does not make IGRA a recommended diagnostic test for M. marinum or prove that every positive result in someone with a skin lesion is cross-reactive. [12]

Goire and colleagues described a 52 year old man with chest pain and fluid around the lung. He had a positive QuantiFERON result in 2018 but a negative result during his 2022 illness. Testing of respiratory and pleural samples, including a molecular assay on pleural biopsy tissue, initially failed to identify TB. Mycobacterial culture of pleural tissue eventually grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis. He completed six months of treatment with resolution of symptoms and pleural fluid and remained well at twelve months. A later QuantiFERON result was still negative. The observed outcome was recovery from confirmed pleural disease despite a negative immune result. It does not establish the exact biological cause of reversion or date the infection. The lesson is to continue investigating a plausible clinical problem when indirect immune testing and early direct tests do not settle it. [13]

## A forensic investigation needs more than immune testing

Templeton and colleagues investigated exposure to a patient whose tuberculosis had not been recognized before death. None of 40 previously skin test negative workers who had provided ward care showed a conversion. Among five previously negative personnel present during the autopsy, all five became skin test positive. Two subsequently had positive sputum cultures eight weeks later. The organisms from those two people and the deceased patient had matching DNA fingerprints. Thus immune testing identified a change requiring investigation, while cultures and organism comparison strengthened the evidence linking the illnesses. This small historical event cannot quantify the risk at every modern autopsy or prove that ordinary ward contact is safe. It demonstrates why records from before and after exposure matter and why an immune result alone cannot identify who transmitted an infection. The report's culture outcomes should not be expanded into an unreported long term recovery or legal finding. [14]

Auld and colleagues examined United States surveillance data from people with culture confirmed TB, including 11,393 with recorded IGRA results. Thirteen percent of those IGRAs were negative and 6 percent indeterminate. Negative or indeterminate immune results were associated with worse outcomes than positive results, and an indeterminate result was associated with disseminated disease and death. Disseminated means disease involving multiple body sites. These were observational associations in patients already known to have TB, not the predictive value of screening a healthy person. The data could not determine whether the pattern reflected impaired immune responses, delayed recognition, other illness or several factors together. The study reinforces a practical boundary: in someone seriously ill with a compatible presentation, an unhelpful immune result does not justify closing the investigation. [15]

## Outcomes depend on what follows the result

Finding infection can create an opportunity to prevent disease, but benefit and harm depend on the treatment actually delivered. In Sterling and colleagues' randomized prevention trial, completion was 82.1 percent with a shorter rifapentine plus isoniazid regimen and 69.0 percent with a longer isoniazid regimen. Investigator assessed drug related liver toxicity occurred in 0.4 and 2.7 percent, respectively. The trial supported the shorter regimen's effectiveness for preventing TB in the studied high risk population. Those findings are historical trial evidence, not a prescription for an individual reader. Drug interactions, age, pregnancy, resistance considerations and other clinical factors affect regimen selection. The example connects testing to real outcomes: a positive immune result is useful when it leads to the correct evaluation, an appropriate treatment decision and follow-up that includes adverse effects. [16]

Children require a separate assessment because progression after exposure can be rapid and severe, particularly in the very young. Current CDC guidance generally favors TST below age five while acknowledging that some experts use IGRAs in younger children. Choice also depends on the exposure, vaccination history, the ability to obtain blood and local expertise. A young child's negative test soon after contact does not automatically settle the management decision. CDC describes window preventive treatment for selected exposed children after TB disease has been excluded, with repeat testing and specialist guidance. This is a professional prevention pathway, not permission to start or stop medication at home. Families should keep the planned follow-up and tell the care team about new symptoms even if the first test was negative. [17]

## Evaluating disease and preserving a useful record

Evaluation for active TB joins the history, examination, chest imaging and appropriate microbiology. Culture attempts to recover the organism, while nucleic acid amplification tests look for its genetic material. A positive immune test can support evidence of infection but cannot replace those investigations. Conversely, some patients with active disease have negative immune tests. Specimen choice follows the suspected site: a respiratory sample and tissue from another affected organ do not answer exactly the same question. Before treating latent infection, clinicians must assess for active disease because the treatment approaches differ. The resulting explanation should state what has been demonstrated, what remains suspected and what evidence is still awaited. A report marked positive should not be allowed to compress all of that work into a single unexplained label. [18]

Persistent cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue or coughing blood can prompt assessment for TB, although other illnesses can produce similar symptoms. CDC describes a cough lasting three weeks or longer among common pulmonary features; that description is not a requirement to wait three weeks before seeking help when symptoms are concerning. Tell the service about possible TB exposure when arranging assessment so that it can plan the visit appropriately. Someone becoming acutely unwell needs prompt clinical care rather than waiting for a routine screening result. A prior negative test cannot override a changing clinical picture. Symptoms outside the lungs can also occur, so the location and course of the illness matter when the team selects further investigations. [19]

Keep the original test name, date, skin measurement or blood interpretation, exposure history and follow-up plan. CDC's occupational guidance distinguishes a two-step baseline skin testing process, which can reveal a boosted response from older immune memory, from repeat testing after a new exposure. Those timelines answer different questions. Written documentation of an earlier positive result or completed treatment is also valuable because immune tests may remain positive and repeated testing often adds little. A positive result alone cannot establish treatment failure, a new infection date or current infectiousness. Occupational requirements may vary with local regulations and the person's duties. A clear record allows the next clinician to interpret the result in context instead of repeating tests simply because the earlier explanation has been lost. [20]

# H021: Human immunodeficiency virus testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can HIV testing detect infection early and resolve results that initially seem contradictory?*

## What the testing pathway is for

Human immunodeficiency virus, abbreviated HIV, can be present for years before a person recognizes symptoms. Testing can identify infection while treatment can still prevent substantial immune damage. A diagnosis of HIV does not itself mean that someone has AIDS, the advanced stage of the disease. Current United States CDC guidance recommends at least one routine test for people aged 13 to 64, with repeat screening for ongoing exposure risk and testing during pregnancy. These population recommendations do not prohibit testing outside that age range when clinically appropriate. This chapter explains the United States laboratory pathway; other countries use validated national algorithms. Testing should include an agreed way to receive results and reach follow-up care, because an unanswered report does not complete the diagnostic task. [1]

## Three biological signals, three different questions

An antigen is a component of the virus; an antibody is a protein the immune system makes in response. Laboratory combination assays look for HIV-1 p24 antigen together with antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2. In Mayo's diagnostic serum assay, selective binding reactions are converted into an electrically triggered light signal, called electrochemiluminescence. The instrument compares that signal with an assay boundary. It is not looking at intact viruses under a microscope. A reactive combination result does not necessarily identify which component produced the reaction, and its signal is not a viral count. The laboratory must continue the appropriate sequence. This particular catalog also distinguishes diagnostic patient testing from blood-donor screening, which has its own requirements. [2]

A nucleic acid test, or NAT, looks for viral genetic material. In a reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction assay, RNA is converted into DNA, and selected target sequences are amplified until they can be detected. Mayo's qualitative HIV-1/HIV-2 RNA assay reports detection rather than a numerical viral load. Internal controls help identify failure or inhibition of the reaction. An inconclusive result means the test could not reliably settle presence or absence; it is not equivalent to negative. Specimen handling also matters: unsuitable storage or processing can impair RNA detection. The patient usually experiences a routine blood draw, while the collection service and laboratory manage the platform-specific requirements and determine whether another sample is needed. [3]

## The window period belongs to the exact test

After an exposure, HIV markers take time to become detectable. CDC gives usual detection ranges of 10 to 33 days for NAT, 18 to 45 days for a laboratory antigen/antibody test using venous blood, 18 to 90 days for a rapid fingerstick antigen/antibody test, and 23 to 90 days for antibody tests. These ranges describe test categories, not a promise that every infection is detectable on the first day listed. A test on the day of an exposure cannot exclude infection acquired that day. The clinician relates the assay and collection date to the last possible exposure and arranges repeat testing when indicated. Antiretroviral prevention or treatment requires additional interpretation, discussed below. [4]

## Why a reactive screen starts a sequence

The CDC and Association of Public Health Laboratories algorithm begins with a laboratory HIV-1/HIV-2 antigen/antibody immunoassay. A reactive result proceeds to an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay, which determines whether the antibody pattern supports HIV-1 or HIV-2. When that supplemental assay is negative or indeterminate, diagnostic nucleic acid testing resolves whether the first signal represents early HIV infection or a false reaction. Early infection can produce detectable virus before the antibody pattern has developed fully. Repeating only the original screening test does not answer that biological question. The tests complement one another because they examine different markers; the sequence is more informative than collecting several copies of the same preliminary result. [5]

A good report states each assay result and the interpretation of the completed laboratory algorithm. The Association's October 2025 reporting guidance emphasizes the distinction between a final result from one instrument and the overall diagnostic interpretation. A portal may display the reactive screen before supplemental results appear. Pending testing should be communicated as pending, with a clinician responsible for completing it. The updated document also recognizes newer diagnostic NATs and supplemental assays, so older advice that every positive screen requires a Western blot is outdated for this pathway. Its standard reporting framework is not intended to manage people taking pre-exposure prophylaxis; those patients need the specific clinical testing approach described later. [6]

## Indeterminate does not mean partly infected

Indeterminate describes an assay pattern that does not meet its established positive or negative criteria. It is not a measurement of a fraction of an infection. New York's current testing guideline calls for prompt RNA testing after a reactive screen with a negative or indeterminate differentiation result when this has not already occurred automatically. Reflex testing means the laboratory adds the next indicated test without requiring another appointment or order. A newly collected specimen may be needed when the pattern remains unresolved. The ordering team should establish what has actually been performed, rather than assume that a laboratory comment is a completed test. Recent symptoms, exposures and medicines help determine the next step and the urgency of review. [7]

HIV-2 makes assay identity particularly important. It is concentrated in West Africa and occurs elsewhere through migration and transmission networks. Standard HIV-1 RNA assays do not reliably detect or quantify HIV-2. Therefore, an undetectable HIV-1 viral load cannot exclude HIV-2 when serology and exposure history suggest it. Even an HIV-2-specific RNA test may be undetectable in an untreated person with HIV-2, and immune deterioration can still occur. Specialist laboratories can help resolve atypical patterns using appropriate HIV-2 testing. Distinguishing the types also changes treatment choices because their drug susceptibility differs. Geography informs the assessment; it should neither replace laboratory confirmation nor become a reason to overlook HIV-2 in someone without an obvious travel history. [8]

## False positives and the denominator

Specificity is the ability to classify uninfected people correctly. Even a highly specific screen can produce false reactions, particularly when testing many people in a population where infection is uncommon. Consider a hypothetical example: among 10,000 people, assume 10 have HIV, sensitivity is 100 percent, and specificity is 99.9 percent. There would be about 10 true reactive results and 10 false reactive results. This is invented arithmetic, not a performance estimate for a named assay or an actual screening program. It explains why a reactive screen needs confirmation. Biological cross-reactions, specimen errors and technical problems are different possible causes; a person cannot determine which applies by examining the screening number alone. [9]

## What a self-test can and cannot finish

An oral-fluid self-test can make testing private and accessible, but the specimen and method matter. The FDA-authorized OraQuick test detects antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2; it does not provide the early RNA information of a NAT. FDA describes an approximately three-month window for this product. Its clinical study sensitivity was approximately 92 percent, a figure specific to the studied home-use setting rather than all HIV assays. A reactive self-test needs medical laboratory follow-up. A negative result obtained too soon after exposure cannot provide the reassurance of a correctly timed clinical pathway. An invalid result means the device did not give an interpretable answer. Follow the current product instructions and contact a testing service when the result or timing is uncertain. [10]

## Symptoms can change the testing request

Acute HIV can resemble influenza or another viral illness, with fever, sore throat, enlarged lymph nodes or rash; some people notice little or nothing. Symptoms alone cannot diagnose it. CDC advises HIV RNA testing when acute infection is suspected despite a negative or indeterminate initial pathway. Telling the clinician about a recent exposure can therefore change which test is ordered. A generic negative antibody result is insufficient when the clinical question is whether infection is too recent for antibodies to have formed. Conversely, a rash after an exposure is not proof of HIV. The useful response is clinical assessment with appropriately timed testing, together with investigation of other possible causes of the illness. [11]

## Prevention medicines can change marker development

Pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, is medicine used to prevent HIV in people without infection. If infection occurs despite prevention, antiretroviral activity can suppress viral RNA and delay antibody development, making results difficult to interpret. CDC's current PrEP guidance bases testing on recent medication history, including oral and long-acting agents, and advises a new blood specimen when results are ambiguous. Oral rapid tests are less suitable for this setting because they may miss recent infection. Decisions about continuing or changing prevention belong to the treating team while confirmation is arranged. A reader should report the exact product and dates, including injections received elsewhere; the laboratory cannot infer those exposures from the specimen. [12]

The HPTN 083 investigation documented the consequences. It examined HIV infections among cisgender men and transgender women who have sex with men in a prevention trial. Cabotegravir exposure sometimes suppressed virus and delayed antibody expression, so study-site testing recognized infection later than retrospective testing. Five cabotegravir-arm cases had mutations associated with resistance to integrase inhibitors, a class of HIV drugs. In these five, site detection occurred a median of 47 days after the first HIV-positive visit identified retrospectively, and participants received additional injections before diagnosis. This was a specific diagnostic and resistance outcome, not proof that every person using injectable prevention will have delayed detection. The findings support carefully designed follow-up rather than abandonment of effective prevention. [13]

## Recent exposure needs assessment before a test becomes positive

Post-exposure prophylaxis, or PEP, is emergency prevention after a potentially significant exposure. CDC's 2025 guidance recommends starting indicated nonoccupational PEP as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after exposure; laboratory results should not delay the first treatment decision. That is a prevention deadline for this circumstance, not a rule that symptoms must develop within 72 hours. A baseline negative test cannot exclude acquisition from the event that prompted the visit. Follow-up generally combines laboratory antigen/antibody testing and diagnostic NAT at four to six weeks and at the final twelve-week visit, with specified exceptions for interim testing. Antiretroviral exposure can delay detection. The clinician must connect timing to the actual exposure and treatment course. [14]

## Pregnancy and infancy require their own pathway

Pregnancy testing aims to protect both the pregnant person and infant. NIH guidance recommends expedited testing during labor when HIV status is undocumented, or when renewed risk was not followed by appropriate repeat testing. A reactive result triggers immediate supplemental maternal testing and infant assessment while the delivery team makes time-sensitive prevention decisions. An initial reactive result still requires confirmation; urgency and diagnostic accuracy must proceed together. Maternal infection acquired after an earlier negative prenatal test is one reason that old records cannot always settle the present question. Decisions about infant feeding and preventive treatment should be made with the obstetric and pediatric teams while results are clarified, rather than improvised from a preliminary portal notification. [15]

In an infant with perinatal exposure, antibodies transferred across the placenta can make an antibody test positive without infection of the infant. NIH therefore recommends virologic RNA or DNA testing for diagnosis below 18 months in this population. A positive virologic result is confirmed on a separate blood sample. Testing follows an age- and risk-specific schedule because a birth specimen cannot detect every infection acquired around delivery, and preventive medicines may reduce assay sensitivity. Breastfeeding creates an additional exposure timeline requiring its own follow-up. Occasionally maternal antibodies persist beyond 18 months, so a positive antibody result in an exposed older infant may still need NAT clarification. Adult window-period charts cannot substitute for this pediatric pathway. [16]

## A published false-reactive screen during autoimmune liver disease

Robinson and colleagues reported a 51-year-old woman with abdominal pain, jaundice and severe liver-enzyme elevation. Her HIV combination screen remained reactive on repeat, but HIV-1 NAT was negative. Liver biopsy and other findings supported autoimmune hepatitis, an immune attack on the liver. She improved with corticosteroid treatment and was discharged for gastroenterology follow-up. The report did not document a completed HIV differentiation assay because it was unavailable locally, and long-term follow-up was not supplied. The authors considered assay interference associated with the autoimmune illness plausible; the precise mechanism was not established. This case illustrates a misleading preliminary signal and the limits of the documented workup, not a license to dismiss reactive HIV screens in everyone with liver disease. [17]

## A molecular result altered by cell therapy

Hauser and colleagues described a 16-year-old girl treated with engineered immune cells, called CAR T cells, for leukemia. Before bone marrow transplantation, HIV serology was nonreactive while one RNA platform repeatedly reported low positive results. A different platform targeting another viral region found no RNA, and the team attributed the original results to interference from the lentiviral vector, a modified viral delivery system carrying genetic instructions, used to engineer the cells. Shared target sequences could trigger the assay without actual HIV infection. She proceeded to transplantation and achieved remission, but leukemia relapsed four months later. The interference was specific to therapy and assay targets; repeating an affected method alone would have reinforced the wrong impression. [18]

## Undetectable RNA can coexist with established infection

Fadl and colleagues reported a 62-year-old woman whose hospital screening showed confirmed HIV seropositivity, preserved CD4 cells and undetectable RNA without documented antiretroviral treatment. Earlier records contained positive HIV results dating to 2015, but the authors found no documented disclosure, counseling or treatment. They interpreted her course as elite control, an uncommon capacity to suppress replication naturally. Reported laboratory follow-up through August 2025 continued to show undetectable RNA; a subsequent treatment outcome was not provided. The case cannot establish why each earlier encounter failed. It illustrates two distinct problems: undetectable RNA does not automatically invalidate established serologic evidence, and storing a correct result in disconnected records does not ensure that the person understands or receives care for the diagnosis. [19]

## A transmission investigation requires more than a positive test

CDC's New Mexico investigation identified five linked HIV diagnoses during 2018–2023: four former clients of an unlicensed spa and one client's sexual partner. Viral sequences were closely related, and investigators found serious infection-control deficiencies. Evidence supported probable transmission through platelet-rich plasma microneedling for three clients; the couple's established infections likely preceded those services. Three people already had advanced disease at diagnosis. A specific contamination event was not confirmed, and the report did not provide individual treatment outcomes. This matters for forensic interpretation: a positive test establishes a person's diagnostic status, whereas attributing acquisition to a particular procedure requires timing, records, laboratory comparison and competing explanations. The report should not be simplified into five proven procedure-acquired infections. [20]

A separate forensic investigation used viral gene sequences from a man and two women to explore an alleged transmission relationship. The published abstract describes analyses of different genome regions and emphasizes that unsampled infections and the method's inherent limits prevented unambiguous proof of direct person-to-person transmission. The full report was not available for this chapter, so no court verdict or patient outcome is inferred. A phylogenetic tree groups related viral sequences; it is not a human DNA fingerprint identifying a single perpetrator. Close relatedness can support an epidemiological hypothesis, but an intermediary person may not have been sampled. Clinical diagnosis, public health linkage and legal attribution are different conclusions with different evidence requirements. [21]

## After diagnosis, testing measures different outcomes

Once HIV is confirmed, viral load and CD4 count answer different questions. Plasma RNA estimates current viral replication, while the CD4 count assesses a key part of immune function. NIH's current initial-assessment guidance also includes tests for coinfections and conditions relevant to safe treatment. A high CD4 count does not negate HIV, and a falling viral load does not mean antibodies should disappear. Follow-up schedules depend on treatment stage, clinical stability and other findings. The useful record includes assay, units, date and medication history. Comparing an antibody screening index with RNA copies per milliliter would compare different measurements, not two estimates of the same quantity. A clinician explains which result is being followed and why. [22]

Resistance testing examines viral genetic changes that can reduce the effectiveness of particular medicines. NIH recommends it at entry into care to guide treatment, including appropriate integrase testing when prior prevention or treatment makes that resistance relevant. Genotyping is not the test that initially proves everyone has HIV, and obtaining it should not unnecessarily delay indicated treatment. The team can adjust a regimen when results arrive. A negative resistance report does not guarantee that every possible resistant variant has been excluded, especially when viral RNA is too low for a successful assay. This distinction helps readers understand why diagnosis, selection of treatment and measurement of treatment response may require several related laboratory orders. [23]

Successful treatment can reduce HIV RNA below a laboratory's detection limit. This means the assay cannot detect circulating RNA at its stated sensitivity, not that HIV has been eradicated from every cell. CDC affirms that people taking treatment who achieve and maintain viral suppression do not transmit HIV through sex. That prevention evidence should be communicated clearly rather than undermined by fear about the word positive on an antibody report. It does not turn an old undetectable result into a guarantee about current suppression, prevent other sexually transmitted infections, or supply an equivalent zero-risk conclusion for breastfeeding or shared injection equipment. Regular care connects the diagnosis to sustained health and to the particular outcome each test can actually establish. [24]

# H022: Viral hepatitis testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 6)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-07

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *Does a positive hepatitis test show current infection, past exposure, immunity, or a misleading reaction?*

## The word hepatitis does not identify the cause

Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver. Viral hepatitis testing investigates specific infections, while other blood tests assess liver injury and function. The familiar hepatitis A, B and C viruses are different organisms with different testing pathways. Hepatitis A does not become chronic; hepatitis B and C can persist and lead to cirrhosis, meaning substantial liver scarring, or liver cancer. Many infected people initially have no symptoms. A and B are vaccine-preventable, while C can usually be cured with antiviral treatment. A positive result therefore needs its full name and context before its meaning can be explained. This chapter explains human testing, mainly United States pathways, including the often-omitted hepatitis D and E viruses. [1]

## What is actually included in the sample request

Most routine investigations use a blood sample. Serology measures antibodies or viral proteins through selective binding reactions; molecular assays detect viral genetic material. Mayo's acute hepatitis profile illustrates why the order name matters: it includes hepatitis A IgM, hepatitis B surface antigen and core IgM, and hepatitis C antibody with RNA reflex testing. The serologic components use electrochemiluminescence, converting binding reactions into measurable light. This acute profile is not the same as screening for hepatitis B immunity, and it does not automatically test for D or E. A negative panel answers only the questions included in that panel. The clinician should review the component results, exposure timing and whether indicated follow-up tests were completed. [25]

## Hepatitis A: recent illness versus immunity

For suspected hepatitis A, the relevant antibody is immunoglobulin M against hepatitis A virus, abbreviated IgM anti-HAV. IgG is a different antibody class associated with past infection or vaccination. Total anti-HAV combines classes, so a positive total result alone cannot establish acute hepatitis A. CDC recommends IgM testing when symptoms and clinical assessment suggest this infection, rather than routine screening of well people. IgM commonly persists for months and can occasionally react falsely, so positive does not supply an exact acquisition date. An unexpected result may require reassessment. Confirmed cases prompt public health review because exposed contacts may benefit from time-sensitive prevention; readers should contact a clinician promptly after a recognized exposure. [2]

A 2021 report described a 56-year-old woman initially diagnosed with hepatitis A after a positive IgM result despite lacking typical symptoms. Abnormal liver tests fluctuated over the following eighteen months. Further investigation, including biopsy, supported autoimmune hepatitis. HAV RNA was negative in current and stored specimens, and the authors concluded that the IgM result had been misleading. After two months of corticosteroid treatment, her liver tests normalized and HAV IgM became negative. The earlier diagnosis had resulted in isolation, distress and delayed recognition of the immune disease. The report does not quantify how often this occurs. It shows why persistent or clinically inconsistent IgM reactivity warrants investigation, rather than repeated acceptance of the same explanation. [3]

## Hepatitis B starts with three complementary markers

CDC recommends at least one hepatitis B screen for all adults aged 18 or older using the triple panel. Hepatitis B surface antigen, HBsAg, is a viral protein; anti-HBs is antibody against that surface protein; total anti-HBc is antibody against the viral core. Together they help distinguish current infection, previous infection, vaccine response and susceptibility. Vaccination alone does not produce core antibody. Periodic testing remains appropriate for susceptible people with ongoing exposures. Screening is useful even without symptoms or disclosed risk. Additional hepatitis B e antigen and its antibody, HBeAg and anti-HBe, help characterize viral activity after infection is identified. They do not replace the triple panel or measure liver scarring. Persistence of HBsAg for at least six months supports chronic infection. [4]

The common patterns are understandable when read as a set. HBsAg and core antibody together support current infection; core IgM helps investigate recent infection, although it can also appear during a flare of longstanding disease. Negative HBsAg with positive core and surface antibodies usually indicates resolved infection. Surface antibody alone can reflect vaccine immunity when a completed series is documented. All three negative results commonly indicate susceptibility in someone never vaccinated. These patterns require clinical interpretation: recent vaccination can transiently produce HBsAg, and antibodies can wane. An anti-HBs concentration of at least 10 mIU/mL is interpreted in relation to completion of the vaccine series and the reason for testing. [5]

## An isolated core antibody needs explanation

Isolated core antibody means total anti-HBc is positive while HBsAg and anti-HBs are negative. CDC's dedicated interpretation guidance lists several possibilities, including previous infection with waning surface antibody, a false-positive core result, occult infection and a viral surface variant missed by an assay. Occult infection means viral genetic material is present despite negative surface antigen testing. The person's immune condition, prior results and exposure background help determine which further tests are needed; HBV DNA can be important. A person about to receive immune-suppressing treatment needs particular attention because previously controlled infection may reactivate. This pattern should not automatically be translated into either active contagious hepatitis or complete reassurance. A clear follow-up plan is more useful than an unexplained positive label. [6]

## Viral DNA measures replication, not the whole liver

HBV DNA testing amplifies selected viral DNA sequences using polymerase chain reaction, or PCR. A quantitative result estimates the concentration, commonly reported in international units per milliliter, whereas a qualitative result describes detection. Mayo's serum assay explains that a concentration below its quantification range can be detected without being measured accurately as a number. That differs from an undetected result. Both depend on the assay and specimen. Viral DNA helps assess activity and treatment response, but it is not a direct percentage of liver damage. Interpretation combines viral markers with liver chemistry, examination and assessment of fibrosis, the build-up of scar tissue. A low treated viral load should not lead someone to stop prescribed therapy independently. [7]

## Pregnancy and infant follow-up are connected but different

CDC recommends HBsAg screening during each pregnancy. When a pregnant person has hepatitis B, the care team coordinates maternal evaluation and the infant's prevention plan. The infant later needs post-vaccination serologic testing that contains HBsAg and anti-HBs only, rather than the adult triple panel. Recommended timing is age nine to twelve months, or one to two months after completion when the vaccine series is delayed. These tests assess both infection status and the immune response to vaccination. Testing too early can mislead, and a record of vaccine administration does not itself establish the outcome. Families should receive a scheduled pediatric follow-up and an explanation of who will review the results. [8]

## Vaccine antigen can be detected without infection

Khoo and colleagues described five people with advanced kidney disease whose HBsAg became positive after hepatitis B vaccination. Three were receiving hemodialysis and two had not started dialysis. All had negative HBV DNA testing, and repeat surface antigen results became negative. The median first positive test was four days after vaccination; the median documented negative repeat was thirteen days, with variable testing intervals. HBsAg remained negative during reported follow-up extending up to two years. Dialysis equipment was temporarily isolated while results were clarified. This selected series is not a population frequency estimate or a universal clearance timetable. It illustrates the importance of telling the testing team about recent vaccination while still investigating a possible genuine infection. [9]

## Transferred antibodies can resemble a past infection

Parker and colleagues reported a 50-year-old man whose hepatitis B core and surface antibodies became positive after intravenous immunoglobulin, a treatment prepared from pooled donor antibodies. HBsAg and HBV DNA were negative. The apparent past infection caused substantial marital distress and nearly led to unnecessary antiviral treatment before further immune treatment. A remaining vial from the administered batch contained the same antibodies, and the patient's antibody level fell on repeat testing, supporting passive transfer rather than his own response to HBV infection. Clarification occurred before the antiviral was started; the authors reported that the brief delay in arthritis treatment had no consequential effect because first-line treatment was working well. Antibodies were genuinely present, but their origin changed the interpretation. [10]

## Reactivation can have serious outcomes

Controlled or resolved hepatitis B can become active again in particular treatment settings. In a primary FDA adverse-event investigation, Bersoff-Matcha and colleagues examined twenty-nine reports of HBV reactivation during direct-acting antiviral treatment for hepatitis C. Two patients died and one required liver transplantation. Diagnosis and treatment of reactivation were delayed in seven reports and possibly seven others. These voluntarily submitted reports had incomplete information and no denominator of everyone treated, so their counts cannot provide an individual risk percentage. The indexed abstract was available for this chapter, rather than the complete report. The clinical lesson is to establish HBV status and an appropriate monitoring plan before HCV treatment, while recognizing that effective hepatitis C treatment remains beneficial. [11]

## Hepatitis C: antibody exposure and RNA infection

A reactive hepatitis C antibody test, anti-HCV, can represent current infection, a previous infection that cleared, or a false antibody reaction. CDC's standard laboratory pathway automatically follows a reactive antibody result with HCV RNA testing. Detectable RNA establishes current infection; reactive antibody with undetectable RNA usually means no current infection. Recent exposure, immune suppression or concern about specimen integrity can justify further investigation. RNA usually becomes detectable within one to two weeks after exposure, whereas antibodies often take eight to eleven weeks. For possible exposure within six months, clinicians should consider RNA even if antibody is negative. These are biological detection intervals, not instructions to delay seeking care until a particular week. [12]

Completing the second step is a measurable patient-care outcome. CDC's 2023 operational guidance describes Veterans Health Administration facilities where only 64 percent of people completed the testing sequence when RNA required a separate visit, compared with 98 percent in facilities using single-visit collection strategies. These are facility observations, not a randomized comparison proving that one change alone caused the difference. They nevertheless show the practical cost of an unfinished antibody result. Reflex testing means that the laboratory performs RNA testing automatically when the antibody reaction calls for it. Collecting all required samples at the initial visit reduces the chance that transportation, missed contact or a second appointment prevents someone from learning whether current infection is present. [13]

## Reading a molecular result below the measuring range

HCV RNA assays commonly use reverse transcription PCR: RNA is copied into DNA, selected sequences are amplified, and a fluorescent signal is compared with standards. Mayo's quantitative assay distinguishes an undetected result from RNA detected below the range that can be accurately quantified. For that assay, a result reported below 15 IU/mL can mean detectable RNA, not an interchangeable negative. An inconclusive reaction may require another specimen. The laboratory manages collection and transport requirements because RNA integrity affects the answer. Keep the actual wording, units and assay with the report. A screening antibody index, an RNA concentration and a liver-enzyme value measure different things; their numbers should not be compared as if they were one scale. [14]

## Recent infection can fluctuate

The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) emphasize that HCV RNA alone cannot distinguish acute infection from longstanding infection. A documented negative baseline followed by detectable RNA, or antibody conversion, can help establish a recent acquisition. During acute infection, RNA may fluctuate or briefly become undetectable, so one negative RNA result does not always establish spontaneous clearance. Alanine aminotransferase, abbreviated ALT, can be normal or fluctuate as well, and other liver insults can raise it. Clinicians combine the timeline, repeated tests when indicated and competing diagnoses. Current guidance favors treating diagnosed acute infection without waiting for a hoped-for spontaneous clearance. [15]

## Point-of-care testing can shorten the path to care

In June 2024, FDA authorized the Xpert HCV test for adults with symptoms or exposure risk in appropriately certified point-of-care services. It detects HCV RNA from fingerstick blood and can provide results in about an hour, potentially allowing diagnosis and linkage to treatment during one encounter. This is a molecular test, so calling every rapid hepatitis test an antibody test would be incorrect. It is also different from a home self-test. The benefit depends on having a service ready to explain the result, evaluate the patient and arrange treatment. A fast instrument cannot by itself complete care when referral or follow-up is missing. The service should confirm availability and suitability. [16]

## Cure is checked with RNA after treatment

For chronic hepatitis C, undetectable or nonquantifiable HCV RNA twelve weeks or longer after treatment completion defines sustained virologic response, often abbreviated SVR12, and is consistent with cure. An end-of-treatment result alone is a different time point. Antibody usually remains positive after cure, so repeating antibody does not measure treatment success or diagnose reinfection. When ongoing exposure warrants reassessment, clinicians use RNA. Cure does not erase established cirrhosis or every future liver risk: people with cirrhosis still need appropriate surveillance for liver cancer. The follow-up therefore separates several outcomes—viral clearance, residual scar-related risk and new exposure—rather than treating one successful result as the end of every form of care. [17]

## An exposed infant needs an age-specific test

Maternal hepatitis C antibodies can persist in an infant without proving infant infection. CDC's 2023 recommendations use NAT for HCV RNA at age two to six months for perinatally exposed infants. An undetectable result at or after two months does not require further follow-up unless clinically warranted; detectable RNA leads to pediatric specialist consultation. Previously untested children aged seven to seventeen months should receive RNA testing, while those aged eighteen months or older begin with antibody and RNA reflex testing if reactive. The purpose of earlier testing is also practical: more exposed children can be identified and linked to care before they disappear from follow-up. [18]

## An occupational exposure starts a separate timeline

After a healthcare worker's blood exposure, CDC recommends prompt baseline assessment of both the source patient and worker, with HCV RNA preferred for source testing in relevant circumstances. When follow-up is indicated, RNA is checked at three to six weeks and a final antibody assessment at four to six months, with appropriate RNA follow-up or substitution when baseline antibody was already positive. These dates are specific to this occupational pathway. A baseline result cannot identify infection acquired moments earlier. CDC does not recommend routine hepatitis C post-exposure prophylaxis; the strategy is to detect and treat infection if it occurs. Prompt exposure assessment still matters because hepatitis B and HIV require their own prevention decisions. [19]

## Hepatitis D requires the hepatitis B context

Hepatitis D virus depends on hepatitis B surface proteins, so it is investigated within the care of someone with HBV. WHO's 2024 guideline conditionally favors offering total anti-HDV testing to all HBsAg-positive people; where resources prevent that, specified higher-risk groups are prioritized. A positive antibody test is followed by NAT for HDV RNA to establish active infection. Antibody exposure and active viral replication are separate findings here too. The recommendations have limited-certainty evidence, and RNA assay access and standardization remain challenges. An unexpectedly severe course or substantial liver inflammation despite low HBV DNA should prompt consideration of another cause, including HDV. Low HBV DNA does not exclude clinically important disease driven by a second virus. [20]

## Hepatitis E is not confined to overseas travel

Hepatitis E can follow contaminated water or, for certain genotypes, animal-to-human exposure through undercooked meat. WHO describes diagnosis using specific IgM antibodies and, where needed, HEV RNA in blood or stool. RNA testing is particularly important in uncommon or chronic presentations. Immune-suppressed people, especially organ transplant recipients, can develop persistent infection. The geographic and exposure pattern matters, but absence of travel does not exclude locally acquired disease. Symptomatic hepatitis during pregnancy deserves prompt assessment because HEV can cause severe maternal and fetal complications in relevant settings. The different genotypes and populations prevent one mortality estimate or testing timetable from being applied indiscriminately to every person with a positive result. [21]

Davern and colleagues tested stored samples from 318 United States patients thought to have drug-induced liver injury. Nine had HEV IgM and four also had detectable HEV RNA. Reassessment favored hepatitis E over medication injury in seven of the nine, while two remained more likely to have drug-related injury. Six-month specimens were available for only four people: all four had undetectable RNA, including two whose initial RNA was positive, with falling IgM and rising IgG supporting recent infection. These findings do not show that every suspected medication reaction is hepatitis E or that all nine had equivalent certainty. Testing changed causal attribution, with follow-up limited to the specimens actually available. [22]

## Forensic attribution needs clinical and independent evidence

The New Hampshire healthcare outbreak investigation identified the outbreak HCV strain in thirty-two of 1,074 cardiac catheterization laboratory patients tested. Three carried more than one strain. Molecular comparison was combined with interviews, medication discrepancies and records linking a technician to exposure days. Limiting testing to patients with documented direct care by that worker would have missed fifteen of the thirty-two confirmed cases. The report later recorded the technician's guilty plea and thirty-nine-year prison sentence; individual patients' treatment outcomes were not its focus. This is a relevant forensic lesson: antibody or RNA establishes diagnostic evidence, while reconstructing transmission requires additional records and molecular interpretation. A newly discovered positive test, by itself, cannot prove where, when or from whom infection was acquired. [23]

## Clinical deterioration can be urgent before the viral label is settled

Severe acute liver illness is assessed while the cause is still being investigated. AASLD stresses prompt viral and autoimmune testing when acute liver failure is suspected, with particular attention to encephalopathy, meaning altered brain function associated with liver failure. New confusion, unusual drowsiness or rapid deterioration in someone with jaundice warrants urgent medical assessment rather than waiting for a routine hepatitis panel. Clinicians also examine clotting function, other laboratory findings, medicines and possible nonviral causes. The urgency comes from the person's condition, not a universal antigen value or antibody deadline. Conversely, an asymptomatic screening result still deserves reliable follow-up even when it does not call for emergency care. The goal is both an accurate cause and an appropriate response to current illness. [24]

# H023: Respiratory pathogen nucleic acid testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Infectious disease and molecular diagnostics
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What does a positive respiratory PCR establish when the same signal might represent a new infection, lingering genetic material or an unexpected laboratory finding?*

## The clinical question defines the test

Respiratory nucleic acid testing searches for selected genetic material from organisms in an airway specimen. Nucleic acids are DNA and RNA, the molecules carrying genetic instructions. Finding a target can identify an infection that changes treatment, precautions or an outbreak investigation. It does not by itself explain every cough or measure lung damage. CDC describes multiplex testing for influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, abbreviated RSV, and SARS CoV 2, the virus causing COVID 19, together because their illnesses overlap. The clinician starts with the person's condition, exposure history and the decision a result could change. [25]

The name respiratory panel is not a universal inventory. Mayo's nasopharyngeal panel includes common viruses and selected bacteria, but its ordering guidance recommends more targeted tests for otherwise healthy people likely to have a mild, self limited illness. An extensive panel can be useful in a seriously ill or vulnerable patient without being necessary for every cold. Its target list and specimen approval should be read literally. A report combining rhinovirus and enterovirus does not identify which of those related viruses is present. A broad panel also does not replace investigations for organisms outside its list, or establish a noninfectious explanation such as asthma. [2]

## The specimen has an address and a date

An anterior nasal swab samples the front of the nose; a nasopharyngeal swab reaches the region behind it. Saliva, throat material, sputum and specimens from deeper airways are different materials. A test validated for one is not automatically validated for all the others. CDC emphasizes matching collection to the manufacturer's instructions and the laboratory's acceptance rules. Correct identity, collection site and time belong with the specimen. Poor sampling can leave too little material for a reliable answer even when a sophisticated instrument works perfectly. Nasopharyngeal collection and invasive lower airway sampling are professional procedures, rather than home alternatives to an approved self collection kit. [4]

Timing changes how much target is available. Influenza specimens generally give the best yield when obtained early in illness, while lower respiratory material can remain informative in severe lung disease after upper airway detection declines. CDC also stresses approved collection materials, transport and storage. Excessive delay or unsuitable handling can reduce detection; the laboratory's instructions should govern refrigeration or other storage rather than a rule borrowed from another assay. Antigen tests recognize viral proteins, while molecular tests recognize nucleic acid and are generally more sensitive. A rapid result can therefore come from either method. Speed alone does not identify the technology or its limitations. [5]

## How a small genetic signal becomes detectable

PCR means polymerase chain reaction. After extraction releases and purifies genetic material, short DNA guides called primers select a target sequence for repeated copying. RNA viruses require reverse transcription, conversion of RNA into DNA, giving the name RT PCR. Multiplex testing follows several targets within one system. Some platforms use successive amplification stages and identify products through their melting characteristics. The BIOFIRE RP2.1 instructions describe a particular closed pouch system; its operation should not be assumed for every molecular test. Its usual output is a qualitative finding, detected or not detected, rather than a count of infectious particles. [3]

That system includes an RNA process control carried through extraction and amplification, plus a separate amplification control. Failed controls can invalidate the run and require laboratory investigation. A passing control supports the analytical process; it cannot prove the original swab reached the right place or contained the patient's pathogen. An invalid report therefore is not a negative report. [3]

## Performance numbers belong to a study population

Leber and colleagues evaluated an earlier panel, RP2, using 1,612 prospective nasopharyngeal specimens. At least one target was detected in 1,020. Most specimens came from children, and many came from emergency or inpatient care. This is a detection frequency in that selected population, not the chance that an average person has a respiratory infection. Performance was assessed target by target against comparison methods, with additional investigation of disagreements. Rare targets provided less clinical evidence than common ones. A high overall agreement can conceal uncertainty for a seldom encountered organism; the report's exact target and validation matter more than a single advertised percentage for the whole panel. [8]

## A negative result does not examine the whole lung

When symptoms persist despite a negative result, the question is why. The target may be absent, below the detection limit, outside the panel, or missed because of site or timing. Coinfection also occurs: a positive influenza test does not exclude COVID 19. Current CDC guidance specifically recommends lower respiratory nucleic acid testing for critically ill, mechanically ventilated patients suspected of influenza or COVID 19 whose diagnosis remains unconfirmed, including after negative upper specimens. Clinicians can use material obtained through the patient's existing airway care when appropriate. This does not mean everyone with a negative nose swab needs bronchoscopy. The potential information must justify the procedure and fit the laboratory's validated specimen types. For hospitalized patients with suspected influenza, CDC also advises starting indicated antiviral treatment promptly without waiting for test results. [1]

A Massachusetts General Hospital study illustrates the difference. After an initial negative result, 13 of 669 repeated lower respiratory tests were positive, compared with 67 of 6,484 repeated upper respiratory tests. These denominators are tests, not independent patients or universal sensitivity estimates. The 13 people diagnosed through lower specimens had compatible symptoms and abnormal chest radiographs. Testing was selected by clinical suspicion, so the groups were not interchangeable randomized samples. The finding supports reconsidering the anatomical site when pneumonia remains plausible. It does not show that a routine negative nasal test commonly misses infection in everyone, or provide individual treatment outcomes for all those detected. [20]

## Cycle threshold is not a portable infectiousness score

Some real time PCR instruments record a cycle threshold, abbreviated Ct: the amplification cycle when a signal crosses the assay's decision threshold. In the same method under comparable conditions, a lower value usually reflects more starting target. Collection quality, specimen volume, extraction, target design and instrument settings also affect it. A Ct of 30 on one platform cannot simply be equated with 30 on another. The Infectious Diseases Society of America, or IDSA, concludes that nonstandardized signal values such as Ct are insufficient alone to establish SARS CoV 2 infectiousness. Its guideline also discourages automatic repeat molecular testing after every initial negative result, while allowing repeat investigation when timing, collection or evolving illness warrants it. [7]

## A positive signal can outlast the infectious episode

CDC notes that SARS CoV 2 RNA can remain detectable for up to 90 days after a positive test and advises against using NAATs to retest people who tested positive during that interval. NAAT means nucleic acid amplification test, the broader family that includes PCR and other amplification methods. Reinfection and persistent detection must be considered through the clinical testing pathway rather than by declaring every later positive a new episode. Antibody testing answers a different question about the immune response and is not a substitute for acute respiratory detection. A repeatedly positive genetic test should not become an unsupervised requirement to keep testing until every signal disappears. [6]

In an early German study, Wolfel and colleagues followed nine hospitalized patients with generally mild COVID 19. Respiratory RNA sometimes persisted after symptoms resolved, but their attempts to recover infectious virus were unsuccessful in specimens obtained after day eight of symptoms. Growing virus in suitable cells supplies evidence of viable virus under those laboratory conditions; PCR supplies evidence of its genetic material. The divergence is the useful observation. Nine patients infected early in the pandemic cannot define a safe day or Ct threshold for every variant, illness severity or immune condition. Failure to grow virus also depends on specimen quality and culture conditions, rather than measuring every possible transmission event. [9]

## Impaired immunity can change the interpretation

Avanzato and colleagues described a woman with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and reduced antibody levels who remained without COVID 19 symptoms despite prolonged detection. Infectious virus was recovered from nasopharyngeal specimens collected 49 and 70 days after her initial positive test; viral RNA was detected up to day 105. After a second convalescent plasma treatment, RNA eventually became undetectable, but one case cannot establish that the treatment caused clearance. The important outcome was documented prolonged viable virus shedding in an immunocompromised person, not RNA persistence alone. Such patients may need specialist infection control assessment. Conversely, this exceptional course should not be projected onto every otherwise healthy person with a late positive PCR. [10]

## RSV detection depends on age and specimen

RSV can cause bronchiolitis in infants, an illness affecting small airways, and serious lower respiratory illness in older adults or people with vulnerable hearts, lungs or immunity. CDC describes molecular assays as highly sensitive and antigen assays as generally less sensitive. The assay instructions determine acceptable specimens and whether the result distinguishes RSV types. A positive result can support precautions and clinical assessment, but it does not directly measure breathing effort, oxygenation or the need for hospital care. These require examination and monitoring. A negative antigen result and a negative well collected molecular specimen should not be treated as equivalent pieces of evidence. [14]

The Multispecimen Study's final analysis included 3,669 hospitalized adults with a valid study nasopharyngeal specimen and at least one additional study specimen. Adding sputum, saliva or paired blood antibody testing detected RSV infections missed by the nasopharyngeal specimen alone. Paired serology compares an acute blood sample with a later sample for a changing immune response; it can improve research ascertainment while arriving too late for an immediate bedside decision. Not every participant supplied every specimen, and the study population was largely older with substantial underlying illness. Its results support awareness of underdetection in adults, rather than automatically ordering every specimen for every infant or outpatient. [15]

## Finding an organism and assigning symptoms are separate tasks

Rhinovirus is a frequent cause of colds, but a later detection needs a timeline. Loeffelholz and colleagues followed infants through their first year and used viral genetic typing to distinguish repeated detection of the same strain from a new strain. Among 179 evaluable infections, eight showed the same strain for more than 30 days. This was uncommon in that otherwise healthy infant population. The observation explains why two positive rhinovirus results are not enough to decide whether one infection persisted: a new infection can intervene. It also shows why symptoms, interval and immune status belong beside the molecular result. Genetic typing in a research study is more detailed than a routine combined rhinovirus enterovirus report. [16]

Spuesens and colleagues enrolled 405 asymptomatic and 321 symptomatic children in a Dutch observational study. Mycoplasma pneumoniae DNA was found in 21.2 percent of tested asymptomatic children and 16.2 percent of tested symptomatic children; the difference was not statistically significant. Culture, quantitative PCR and antibody tests did not reliably separate carriage from symptomatic infection in this population. Carriage means an organism is present without establishing it as the cause of the current illness. This does not make Mycoplasma harmless or PCR useless. It means a detected upper airway target cannot alone prove that a child's pneumonia, wheezing or fever is attributable to that bacterium, and that these older local percentages are not universal current prevalence estimates. [13]

## A target can be shared or introduced by contamination

Pertussis testing demonstrates why sequence choice matters. Bordetella pertussis causes whooping cough, but a commonly used repeated DNA target, IS481, also occurs in some related Bordetella species. Multiple targets can improve specificity. CDC advises testing people with compatible symptoms and emphasizes the first three weeks of cough as the period of best PCR sensitivity. Inappropriate swabs and late sampling can reduce detection. DNA from environmental contamination can also produce a misleading signal, including residual bacterial DNA associated with vaccine preparation areas. That is a specimen integrity issue, not evidence that vaccination caused pertussis. Laboratories interpret weak or equivocal signals alongside clinical and epidemiological information. [17]

In a New Hampshire hospital investigation in 2006, 134 personnel met a suspected pertussis definition: 98 through positive or equivocal PCR and 36 through symptoms alone. None of 192 specimens submitted for culture yielded B pertussis. Retesting 111 available DNA extracts at CDC found only one positive for both confirmatory targets. The combined laboratory, clinical and community findings argued against pertussis as the main cause of the apparent outbreak. Nevertheless, 978 ill personnel had been tested, treated and furloughed pending negative results, and 1,311 contacts received preventive antibiotics. This was a costly diagnostic and public health outcome. The investigation does not show all PCR platforms are unreliable; it shows the need to verify a surprising cluster through independent evidence before attributing every illness to one target. [18]

## Lower respiratory panels and bacterial culture have different jobs

A pneumonia panel is a separate assay for lower airway material, with its own bacterial targets and reporting rules. The original United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, assessment of the FilmArray Pneumonia Panel describes approximate bacterial DNA quantity categories. These are genetic copies per milliliter, not colony forming units, which count organisms capable of producing colonies in culture. Detection may reflect colonizing airway bacteria rather than the cause of pneumonia. A resistance gene can inform investigation, but its absence does not prove susceptibility to a drug, and its presence may not identify which organism carries it in a mixed sample. Culture remains important for isolates, additional identification and susceptibility testing. [19]

The FDA assessment also emphasizes prevalence, how common the target is in the tested population. Even a highly specific assay can produce a false positive, and an unexpected positive during low circulation deserves careful interpretation. This changes the probability that a result reflects a true finding; it does not mean all positives should be dismissed whenever an organism is uncommon. [19]

Legionella often needs a specific request. CDC recommends a urinary antigen test paired with culture or molecular testing of a lower respiratory specimen. The usual urine assay detects Legionella pneumophila serogroup one, whereas suitable respiratory methods can detect a wider range. Culture can produce isolates for comparison with environmental samples during an outbreak. PCR may still detect the target after antibiotics have reduced growth. Collecting useful specimens should not delay necessary treatment. A negative ordinary nasal viral panel therefore cannot exclude Legionnaires' disease, and a positive diagnostic test alone cannot prove which building's water system was responsible. Exposure history, environmental findings and strain comparison may be needed. [22]

## Changing viruses can change assay performance

A mutation is a change in genetic sequence. If it occurs where an assay's primers or detection probes recognize the virus, performance can decline. FDA notes that tests targeting several regions are generally more resilient than tests targeting one region, although this is not a guarantee against every problem. When suspicion remains high, the laboratory may consider a different assay with different genetic targets. Routine SARS CoV 2 detection usually does not identify a variant by name. An unusual pattern can prompt further investigation, while sequencing, reading a longer genetic sequence, provides a different level of characterization. Clinical detection, variant surveillance and proof of transmission should remain distinct conclusions. [21]

## Faster answers help only when care responds

The ResPOC randomized trial enrolled 720 adults presenting to a UK hospital with acute respiratory illness. Its main analysis included 360 assigned rapid molecular testing and 354 assigned usual care. Antibiotics were given to 301 of 360 and 294 of 354, respectively, so testing did not reduce the proportion receiving them. Among antibiotic recipients, however, single doses or courses shorter than 48 hours occurred in 50 of 301 versus 26 of 294. Appropriate influenza antiviral use was also higher with rapid testing. The trial distinguishes a useful change in some treatment decisions from a claim that viral detection automatically eliminates antibiotics. Clinicians still considered bacterial disease and many treatment decisions preceded results. [11]

FluPOC studied 613 hospitalized adults during two influenza seasons, allocating 307 to rapid molecular testing and 306 to routine care. Influenza was identified by study assessment in 100 and 102 participants, respectively. All 100 influenza cases in the rapid group were recognized during care, compared with 60 of 102 in routine care. Antivirals reached 99 of 100 versus 63 of 102, and median time to treatment was one versus six hours. These are documented diagnosis and treatment outcomes from a test and care strategy. They do not establish a mortality benefit for every panel or a universal six hour treatment deadline. The practical lesson is to connect a result promptly to a clinical response. [12]

## Forensic diagnosis requires more than a positive swab

Edler and colleagues examined 80 consecutive deceased people with SARS CoV 2 infection in Hamburg. Most deaths were classified within categories involving COVID 19, with differing certainty or competing causes; four were considered unrelated to the infection. The latter included cardiac deaths and sepsis from necrotizing fasciitis, a destructive deep soft tissue infection; three of those four infections were first detected postmortem. Autopsy findings, imaging and medical history supplied the causal assessment that PCR alone could not. This selected group of deaths cannot estimate the fatality risk among infected people. Nor can its classification be transferred unchanged to another death. It provides a direct forensic example of why detecting viral RNA, establishing tissue injury and deciding the cause of death are related but different evidentiary steps. [24]

## Urgency comes from the person as well as the pathogen

Difficulty breathing, persistent chest pressure, new confusion or inability to wake normally warrant emergency assessment regardless of a pending or negative panel. In children, fast or labored breathing, bluish lips, poor alertness or dehydration are warning signs. CDC advises prompt medical attention for these changes. People at higher risk who develop a respiratory illness should also contact their clinical team early because treatment opportunities can be time sensitive. There is no single testing deadline that covers every organism or every patient. The useful result records the test, target, collection site and date, and is interpreted alongside current illness. A reassuring laboratory word must never override obvious deterioration. [23]

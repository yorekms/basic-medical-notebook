# H024: Allergy testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When does a positive allergy test identify a dangerous trigger, and when does it identify an immune response that causes no illness?*

## Start with the reaction and the exposure

Allergy testing answers a clinical question about a particular exposure, rather than searching for a hidden explanation for every symptom. Immunoglobulin E, abbreviated IgE, is an antibody involved in many immediate allergic reactions. Sensitization means that testing detects an IgE response to an allergen, a substance capable of triggering allergy. Clinical allergy requires that exposure actually produces compatible symptoms. The European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology recommends starting food-allergy assessment with a detailed history, then selecting tests for the suspected foods. The account should connect what was eaten, the time symptoms began, the organs affected, previous tolerance and subsequent exposures. A positive result without that connection may create an unnecessary restriction; a convincing reaction deserves investigation even when an initial test is negative. [1]

A rapidly developing reaction with breathing difficulty, throat involvement, collapse or circulatory failure requires emergency assessment. Anaphylaxis is a severe systemic allergic reaction; it can occur without hives. The World Allergy Organization recognizes sudden bronchospasm, airway involvement or hypotension after a likely allergen as potentially diagnostic even when skin findings are absent. Treatment follows the clinical condition, with epinephrine as first-line therapy, rather than waiting for blood results. Someone with a prescribed emergency plan should follow it and summon emergency help. Testing becomes valuable after immediate care has begun, when the team preserves the exposure timeline and investigates the cause. A routine outpatient allergy panel is not a way to decide whether an actively deteriorating person needs help. [19]

## What skin and blood testing actually measure

Skin prick testing measures an immediate skin response to selected allergen extracts. A wheal is the raised, itchy swelling; the surrounding redness is the flare. The Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy emphasizes trained selection, interpretation and the ability to manage systemic reactions. Antihistamines and some other medicines can suppress the skin response, while extensive eczema can make testing difficult. The service should review medicines beforehand and give individualized preparation advice; stopping a necessary medicine without discussion can introduce another problem. Blood testing may be useful when skin testing is unsuitable. [2]

A specific-IgE blood assay measures antibody binding to a selected allergen. In Mayo Clinic Laboratories' peanut assay, peanut material is attached to a solid support. Antibodies in the serum, the liquid remaining after blood clots, can bind that material. An enzyme-linked detection antibody then produces a fluorescent signal proportional to the amount of bound specific IgE. The laboratory converts the signal into its reporting units. The instrument is detecting an immune recognition pattern, not peanuts circulating in the body or a direct measurement of airway swelling. Specimen identity, the exact allergen tested and the assay platform belong on the report. A numerical result is useful only with the test's interpretation and the reason it was ordered. [3]

A larger skin response or higher food-specific IgE may increase the probability of clinical allergy in an appropriate population, but neither reliably predicts the severity of a future reaction. Total IgE combines responses to many targets and does not identify the culprit food. The United States National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases expert panel also distinguishes IgE-mediated reactions from other food disorders. A negative specific-IgE test does not settle conditions driven by different immune mechanisms, including food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome, which can cause delayed vomiting and systemic illness. Likewise, digestive intolerance is not automatically an allergy. The history determines which biological pathway needs assessment, rather than making every meal-related complaint fit one blood test. [4]

## Components can clarify a positive extract result

A whole peanut extract contains several proteins. Component testing asks which individual proteins the antibodies recognize. Mayo's reflex panel distinguishes storage proteins, including Ara h 2, from Ara h 8, which shares features with a birch-pollen protein. Cross-reactivity means antibodies recognize similar structures in different sources. That can explain a positive peanut extract result in someone whose main sensitization is to pollen. Component results refine the clinical assessment; they do not provide an absolute guarantee about reaction severity or replace the exposure history. A negative result for one component also leaves other components untested or negative separately. The practical question is which uncertainty the additional result resolves before avoidance, supervised challenge or another clinical decision is chosen. [5]

Kansen and colleagues prospectively assessed 150 children aged 3.5 to 18 years in Dutch secondary and tertiary care; 113 completed a conclusive reference assessment. At Ara h 2 values of 0.1 allergen-specific kilounits per liter or less, written kUA/L, 26 of 26 evaluated children were peanut-tolerant; at 5.0 kUA/L or higher, 34 of 35 were allergic. The intermediate group needed further evaluation. These findings support probability ranges in that pathway, rather than a universal rule for every child, adult or laboratory. The investigators did not incorporate pretest probability into their accuracy analysis. A reassuring component value therefore belongs within a clinician-selected pathway, not an unsupervised attempt to eat a previously suspected trigger. [6]

## A functional blood test and a supervised challenge answer different questions

The basophil activation test examines living blood cells rather than antibody binding alone. Basophils are immune cells that can respond when their surface-bound IgE encounters an allergen. In Santos and colleagues' peanut study, flow cytometry, a method that measures individual cells, detected activation markers after laboratory exposure to peanut. The primary analysis included 104 children, with independent validation in 65 others. The test helped distinguish allergy from sensitization with tolerance and could reduce the need for challenges. Some participants' cells did not respond adequately to the test controls, limiting interpretation. Fresh-cell handling and specialist laboratory expertise matter. Published accuracy for this selected pediatric peanut population should not be transferred automatically to every allergen or to all commercial implementations. [7]

An oral food challenge observes whether eating a suspected food produces a clinical reaction under medical supervision. The 2024 joint statement from the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology and its European counterpart addresses safety, observation and explicit criteria for continuing, pausing or stopping. Those criteria matter because anxiety, subjective sensations and genuine early reactions can overlap. An open challenge is often sufficient; blinded challenges conceal the exposure to reduce expectation effects when uncertainty persists. The team decides whether the patient's health and prior history make a challenge appropriate and provides rescue capability. A negative result can support reintroduction through an agreed plan. It does not prove lifelong tolerance under every circumstance or identify the cause of unrelated symptoms. [8]

## The harm of testing without a sound question

Food-specific immunoglobulin G, or IgG, is a different antibody class from IgE. Commercial panels sometimes present IgG responses to many foods as evidence of sensitivity or intolerance. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology advises against that use: these responses can reflect ordinary dietary exposure, and food-specific IgG4 may accompany tolerance. A long list of positive foods is therefore not a validated explanation for fatigue, eczema, bowel symptoms or generalized ill health. This does not make those symptoms imaginary. It means the proposed measurement cannot establish the advertised cause. Broad restriction can make eating difficult and reduce nutritional variety while delaying evaluation of the actual problem. The test's clinical validity matters more than the number of foods it reports. [9]

A published infant case shows a concrete consequence of unnecessary restriction. An infant with eczema was placed on an increasingly restricted diet and eventually received only rice milk. He developed generalized swelling, poor growth, profound anemia and very low albumin, a blood protein that helps retain fluid in the circulation. Low positive milk and egg IgE results were reported, but cow's milk was tolerated during hospital care, and later skin tests were negative. Nutrition support resolved the edema; protein and clotting tests normalized by day ten. Tooth demineralization persisted at follow-up through age two. The report concerns an inappropriate diet, not harm from medically necessary allergen avoidance. Its diagnostic lesson is that sensitization and eczema do not establish a need to remove essential foods without nutritional oversight. [10]

## Delayed reactions can require a different target

Alpha-gal is a sugar structure found in non-primate mammalian products. Alpha-gal syndrome can produce delayed reactions after mammalian meat, so asking only about foods eaten immediately before symptoms may miss the pattern. Current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance combines symptoms, timing and tick or outdoor exposure with alpha-gal-specific IgE testing. A positive antibody result alone does not establish the syndrome, particularly where tick exposure and sensitization are common. Testing needs to target alpha-gal specifically when that history suggests it; a conventional food panel cannot be assumed to include every relevant target. The report should preserve the distinction between detectable antibodies and the clinical syndrome, rather than automatically converting a positive result into lifelong dietary instructions. [11]

Commins and colleagues tested the delayed-reaction explanation in a small supervised meat-challenge study. Ten of twelve participants with alpha-gal IgE and a history of delayed severe reactions developed clinical reactions, while controls did not. Reactions began three to seven hours after ingestion, unlike the shorter interval familiar from many other food allergies. Laboratory basophil activation also developed later in the challenge period. The experiment supports a biological reason for the misleading time gap. It does not mean every sensitized person reacts, every episode will have the same delay, or that waiting several hours makes a home exposure safe. The observed outcome was a challenge reaction in a selected group, not a long-term estimate of the chances of a fatal event. [12]

## A penicillin label deserves a precise history

A record saying penicillin allergy can represent immediate allergy, a delayed immune reaction, a viral rash during treatment or a nonimmune adverse effect such as diarrhea. Current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance uses the original symptoms, timing, management and later tolerated exposures to select a validated risk assessment and, when appropriate, skin testing or direct oral challenge. Severe delayed reactions with blistering, widespread skin injury, organ damage or blood-cell injury require specialist assessment and do not belong in a routine challenge pathway. Removing an inaccurate label can restore antibiotic choices; it does not justify ignoring a credible severe reaction. The aim is an accurate, usable record that explains both the suspected reaction and the evaluation actually completed. [13]

The PALACE trial studied selected adults with low-risk penicillin histories at six specialist centers. Of 382 randomized participants, 377 were analyzed: 187 received direct oral challenge and 190 the skin-testing pathway followed by challenge. One person in each group had the immediate immune-mediated primary outcome, about 0.5 percent per group. Nine versus ten immune-mediated adverse events were recorded over the following five days, with no serious adverse events reported. Direct challenge met the prespecified statistical margin for noninferiority: it was not unacceptably worse within the trial's defined comparison. The result did not establish identical risk or test unsupervised exposure, severe delayed reactions or every person with a penicillin label. Delabeling follows a completed risk-based assessment. [14]

Drug testing is specific to the medicine and mechanism. The 2022 practice parameter places greater emphasis on history-based selection and challenge for suitable patients, while recognizing that many drug skin tests have limited validation. A challenge asks whether hypersensitivity occurs; desensitization temporarily modifies responsiveness so an essential medicine can be given through a specialist plan. Successful desensitization does not erase the underlying allergy. A negative test to one antibiotic also cannot clear unrelated medicines automatically. The final record should name the drug, the tested formulation when relevant, the methods used and what future prescribing advice follows. That distinction helps prevent both unnecessary avoidance and a misleading statement that all drug allergy has been excluded. [15]

## A hidden antiseptic illustrates why the exposure record matters

In a 2017 report, a twenty-year-old man developed a rash after fracture surgery, then severe bronchospasm and cardiac arrest during another operation two months later. He recovered after resuscitation and intensive care and was discharged well. Initial skin testing was negative, but chlorhexidine-specific IgE was elevated; repeat skin testing later became positive. Chlorhexidine is an antiseptic and can be overlooked when attention centers on anesthetic drugs. The report supports reviewing disinfectants and other perioperative exposures alongside medicines. It does not establish that every early postoperative rash predicts anaphylaxis, or that one negative skin test excludes the culprit. No subsequent long-term surgical outcome was reported, so discharge recovery is the documented clinical endpoint. [16]

## Patch testing investigates delayed skin allergy

Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed immune reaction in skin, often to substances such as metals, preservatives or workplace chemicals. Patch testing is different from skin prick testing and blood IgE measurement. The published 2026 European Society of Contact Dermatitis guideline connects a positive patch result to the location and course of dermatitis and to actual exposure. A positive response establishes contact sensitization; current clinical relevance still has to be assessed. Irritation can resemble allergy, and immunosuppressive treatment can reduce test responses. Occupational interpretation also requires the work-material history and symptoms away from and back at work. A positive patch test alone cannot identify the responsible employer or prove that every rash has one occupational cause. [17]

## Respiratory allergy testing should change a practical decision

For hay fever and allergic asthma, positive pollen, mite or animal-dander tests need to match where and when symptoms occur. The 2024 inhalant-immunotherapy guideline applies to people aged five and older and recommends selecting clinically relevant allergens that correlate with history and are confirmed by testing. Immunotherapy is treatment intended to modify the allergic response, so treating every positive result can add burden without clarifying the cause of symptoms. The guideline also advises against repeat allergy testing simply to judge whether ongoing immunotherapy works, unless exposure changes or symptom control is lost. Clinical improvement and safety assessment are the relevant outcomes. A falling test number is not, by itself, a certificate that treatment succeeded or that a patient can abandon follow-up. [18]

## Tryptase supports anaphylaxis assessment without replacing it

Tryptase is an enzyme associated with mast cells, immune cells that release mediators during many allergic reactions. The 2023 anaphylaxis practice parameter suggests obtaining an acute sample as early as feasible, ideally within two hours of symptom onset, and a later baseline for comparison. This is a clinical sampling opportunity, not a deadline for seeking help. One widely used comparison is an acute value above 1.2 times the baseline plus 2 nanograms per milliliter. A meaningful rise supports mast-cell activation but does not identify the allergen. A normal result cannot exclude anaphylaxis. Baseline elevation may prompt assessment for a mast-cell disorder or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, an inherited cause of increased baseline tryptase; it does not independently prove a recent reaction. [20]

Dua and colleagues measured tryptase during 160 reactive peanut challenges and 45 placebo challenges in fifty adults. These were repeated challenges, not 160 separate patients, and only nine percent of reactive challenges met the study's anaphylaxis category. Tryptase rose above the laboratory normal range in just four reactions, while comparison with each participant's baseline identified a rise in one hundred. No rise occurred during the placebo challenges. The study shows why a single normal-range value can be falsely reassuring and why the person's baseline adds information. Its proposed study-specific percentage threshold should not replace clinical assessment or be applied automatically to children, injected-drug reactions or postmortem specimens. The laboratory measurement records part of the reaction, rather than every mechanism producing symptoms. [21]

## Venom testing and a forensic case show the limits of one result

After a systemic bee or wasp reaction, testing aims to identify relevant venom sensitization and assess future management needs. The German and Austrian specialist-society guideline recommends connecting venom IgE, component results and, where needed, skin or cellular testing with the sting history. Positive responses to more than one venom can reflect cross-reactivity rather than multiple clinically important allergies. Tests obtained soon after a sting can be negative and require planned reassessment. Baseline tryptase and evaluation for mast-cell disease are particularly relevant after reactions extending beyond skin symptoms. The purpose is to select an appropriate prevention and follow-up plan. A positive venom result in someone without a compatible reaction should not automatically be treated as evidence of a previous dangerous event. [22]

A 2025 forensic report described a forty-one-year-old vineyard worker with systemic mastocytosis who collapsed after a sting and died twelve hours later despite intensive resuscitation. Investigators compared stored blood collected during life with central and peripheral samples obtained six days after death. No sting mark was identified at autopsy. The authors attributed death to venom anaphylaxis with mastocytosis contributing. Sampling sites, the postmortem interval and resuscitation complicated tryptase interpretation. The case supports integrating laboratory, clinical and scene evidence, without establishing a universal fatal concentration or proving preventability. [23]

Postmortem blood needs its own evidential framework. In Tse and colleagues' retrospective study, nine anaphylactic deaths were compared with forty-five controls using femoral blood. A proposed total-tryptase boundary of 53.8 micrograms per liter had estimated sensitivity of 89 percent and specificity of 93 percent in that dataset. Neither measure was perfect, and the small selected groups cannot establish a universal cause-of-death rule. Clinical reference limits from living patients cannot simply be transferred to autopsy material. Forensic interpretation must retain the sampling site, postmortem interval, analytical method, exposure evidence and competing explanations. An elevated tryptase or allergen-specific IgE may support an investigation; neither independently establishes the culprit, a person's intent or legal responsibility. [24]

# H025: Autoimmune antibody testing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: 
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 7)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *How can antibodies aimed at the body help diagnose illness without turning a positive laboratory result into a diagnosis by itself?*

## An autoantibody is a clue whose meaning depends on the illness

Autoantibodies recognize structures within the body rather than an invading organism. Some participate directly in disease, some help identify a disease pattern, and others occur without a corresponding illness. Antinuclear antibodies, or ANA, are a broad group commonly investigated when lupus or a related systemic disorder is suspected. The American College of Rheumatology emphasizes that a positive ANA result alone does not establish autoimmune disease or require immediate treatment. Age, medicines, infections and other conditions affect interpretation. This chapter follows selected systemic, blood-vessel, clotting and neurological antibody pathways; there is no single blood panel that excludes all autoimmunity. The clinician first identifies the pattern of symptoms and organ findings, then chooses tests that can change that assessment. [1]

## From a blood sample to a fluorescent pattern

Most systemic autoantibody tests use serum, the liquid remaining after blood clots. In an indirect immunofluorescence ANA assay, serum is incubated with prepared human epithelial type 2 cells, called HEp-2 cells. A fluorescent detection reagent reveals where immunoglobulin G, or IgG, antibodies have bound. Mayo's assay initially examines serum at a 1:80 dilution and reports a titer and pattern when positive. A titer describes how far a sample can be diluted while its reaction remains detectable; 1:320 means a greater dilution than 1:80, not four times as much organ damage. The cells provide many potential targets, and interpretation requires laboratory expertise. The result should include the method, because an ANA measured by a different technique is not necessarily an interchangeable measurement. [2]

Another ANA method is an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, abbreviated ELISA. Mayo's ANA2 assay uses material from HEp-2 cells supplemented with selected purified antigens, the structures to which antibodies can bind. Bound antibodies are detected through an enzyme reaction measured optically. This produces an instrument signal rather than a microscope pattern. Different antigen mixtures and reporting boundaries can make an ELISA result disagree with immunofluorescence without either sample belonging to a different patient. Identifying the actual method is therefore essential before interpreting an apparent conversion from positive to negative. A negative screen means the tested system did not detect its target response at its boundary; it does not prove that every potentially relevant autoantibody is absent. [3]

## Titer and pattern refine probability rather than naming the disease

Tan and colleagues' multicenter study illustrates why the positivity boundary matters. In the study's apparently healthy adults aged twenty to sixty, ANA was detected in 31.7 percent at a 1:40 dilution, compared with 5.0 percent at 1:160. Those percentages describe that population and testing approach, not the present-day prevalence of autoimmune disease. A more stringent boundary reduces positives among healthy people but can also miss some disease-associated responses. Higher titers deserve interpretation in context; low titers are neither universally meaningless nor sufficient evidence of illness. The useful question is how the result changes the likelihood suggested by the examination, rather than whether a highlighted portal value looks far from zero. [4]

The International Consensus on ANA Patterns connects staining patterns with appropriate follow-up questions. Homogeneous, speckled, centromere and nucleolar describe where fluorescence appears, not stages of one disease. Cytoplasmic staining, outside the nucleus, can also be relevant. Similar patterns may result from different antibodies, and a person's serum can contain several. A dense fine speckled pattern, for example, may suggest reactivity to the DFS70 target, but that identity needs specific confirmation and consideration of other antibodies. It is not an automatic exclusion of systemic autoimmune disease. The report is most informative when the pattern directs selected additional testing alongside the clinical picture. Memorizing a pattern-to-disease table cannot replace that process. [5]

Extractable nuclear antigen, or ENA, panels identify selected antibody targets more precisely, but panel contents vary. Sm, also called Smith, supports a lupus assessment; U1-ribonucleoprotein is relevant to mixed connective tissue disease; SSA/Ro can occur with Sjögren syndrome or lupus. These are associations, not one-antibody diagnoses. ARUP Consult describes selecting follow-up tests from symptoms, ANA findings and the suspected disease. A reflex test is one the laboratory adds according to a defined initial result or pattern. With strong clinical suspicion, selected specific testing can still be appropriate after a negative ANA. That exception requires a focused question, such as a compatible muscle or lung syndrome, rather than adding every antibody to a nonspecific symptom screen. [6]

## Lupus classification and clinical diagnosis are different tasks

The 2019 criteria from the European League Against Rheumatism and American College of Rheumatology require a positive ANA at least once as an entry condition for research classification of systemic lupus erythematosus. They then combine weighted clinical and immune findings, including selected antibodies and complement, proteins involved in immune responses. At least one clinical finding is required, and a finding should count only when lupus is the most likely explanation. These rules help assemble comparable research groups; the authors explicitly distinguish them from diagnostic or treatment decisions. A person can need specialist care without meeting a classification threshold. Conversely, counting laboratory points without excluding infection, medicine effects and other explanations can produce a persuasive-looking but incorrect label. [7]

The British Society for Rheumatology's published 2026 lupus guideline recommends combining a compatible clinical assessment with ANA, selected anti-dsDNA and ENA tests, and complement C3/C4. A negative ANA makes lupus less likely, but past positive results and uncommon pediatric presentations still require consideration. For established active lupus, monitoring combines clinical findings with blood pressure, urine and kidney assessment, blood counts, anti-dsDNA and complement, with other tests as indicated. This distinguishes diagnostic support from measuring ongoing organ involvement. New symptoms may represent active inflammation, accumulated damage, treatment toxicity, infection or thrombosis. An antibody trend alone cannot choose between those explanations. [26]

Antibodies to double-stranded DNA, abbreviated anti-dsDNA, illustrate method-dependent results. In a 2023 study of 207 people with established lupus, 586 paired results from a multiplex enzyme immunoassay and a Crithidia luciliae fluorescence assay included 209 discordant pairs. Crithidia is a microorganism whose DNA-rich structure serves as an assay target; its test preferentially detects more strongly binding antibodies. Different target presentation and antibody binding properties help explain disagreement. These were repeated measurements in a lupus cohort, not a study of 586 independently screened healthy people. Results belonged beside kidney findings, complement and clinical disease activity. A changed method or isolated rising number should not be mistaken automatically for new kidney inflammation or treatment failure. [8]

## Confirming a surprising specific antibody can prevent harm

Lam and colleagues evaluated fifty-two referred people with positive commercial Scl-70 results but insufficient clinical findings for systemic sclerosis. Forty-eight were negative by immunodiffusion, a comparison method in which antibodies and antigen react in a gel. Many had already undergone chest imaging or heart ultrasound. Among those followed, none of nineteen comparison-negative patients became classifiable as systemic sclerosis; three of four comparison-positive patients were classifiable. Follow-up was incomplete, with a median interval of six months in the negative group. This selected referral population does not establish the false-positive rate of every Scl-70 assay or a lifetime prognosis. It demonstrates why an unexpected antibody associated with serious disease deserves review of both the method and the clinical evidence before a durable label is applied. [9]

Recent treatment can introduce antibodies from outside the patient's own immune response. Intravenous immunoglobulin, or IVIG, is prepared from pooled human donations. A 2023 report described five hospitalized recipients who subsequently tested positive for ANA and SSA/Ro without meeting criteria for connective tissue disease. One patient's ANA and SSA became negative one month later. Pre-infusion antibody measurements were unavailable, so passive transfer was a plausible explanation rather than individually proven in every case. Only that patient had repeat antibody testing; three patients later died and another could not be located. The report did not establish that IVIG or those antibodies caused the deaths. Recording the infusion date can prevent a post-treatment antibody result from being read as proof of a newly developed systemic disease. [10]

## ANCA testing asks a distinct blood-vessel question

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, abbreviated ANCA, recognize components of neutrophils, a type of white blood cell. The two principal targets in the standard small-vessel-vasculitis pathway are proteinase 3, or PR3, and myeloperoxidase, or MPO. Vasculitis means inflammation that damages blood vessels. The revised international testing consensus supports high-quality PR3 and MPO immunoassays as primary screening methods for suspected granulomatosis with polyangiitis or microscopic polyangiitis. An initial fluorescence screen is no longer categorically required for those pathways. Cytoplasmic and perinuclear fluorescence patterns are not interchangeable with identification of PR3 and MPO. The exact target and method therefore matter more than a report merely stating ANCA positive, particularly when the clinical presentation or a second assay seems inconsistent. [11]

The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology's 2022 update, published in 2024, emphasizes assessing the suspected vasculitis syndrome and considering alternatives. Lung, kidney, ear-nose-throat, skin and nerve findings can require different investigations. Infection, drug exposure, malignancy and other inflammatory disorders can resemble primary vasculitis, while some clinically important vasculitis is ANCA-negative. Blood counts, urine microscopy, kidney function, imaging and a suitable biopsy can establish what tissue injury is occurring. A biopsy examines a small tissue sample and may distinguish active inflammation from residual scarring or another disease. A positive antibody is therefore part of a diagnostic argument, not a replacement for it. Urgency comes from threatened organs and deterioration, rather than a universal antibody number. [12]

## An infection and a medicine produced misleadingly familiar antibody patterns

A forty-seven-year-old man in Vercellone and colleagues' report had flank pain, dark urine, a splenic infarct and kidney injury. PR3 antibodies were elevated, although ANCA immunofluorescence was negative. Kidney biopsy showed immune-complex deposits, prompting investigation of a heart murmur. A more detailed cardiac ultrasound identified an infected aortic valve, and valve investigation established Bartonella henselae infection despite negative routine blood cultures. Valve replacement and antibiotics were followed by clinical and urinary improvement; creatinine fell to 1.4 milligrams per deciliter and inflammatory markers normalized within two months. PR3 antibodies nevertheless remained elevated. The case demonstrates both an infectious mimic and persistence of an antibody after improvement. Treating the antibody result as proof of primary autoimmune vasculitis would have missed an important competing cause. [13]

A 2026 report described a seventy-five-year-old woman taking hydralazine who developed kidney failure and bleeding into the lungs. MPO and anti-histone antibodies were positive, while ANA and several other antibodies were negative. Kidney biopsy demonstrated necrotizing inflammation with crescents and little immune deposition, called pauci-immune glomerulonephritis. Clinicians attributed the syndrome to hydralazine, stopped it and provided intensive treatment, including immune suppression and plasma exchange. The pulmonary disease resolved and ANCA was negative at three months, but she remained dependent on hemodialysis one year after admission. This is a reported medicine-associated autoimmune syndrome, not an incidence estimate or a treatment comparison. It shows why antibody disappearance and control of inflammation do not necessarily reverse damage already sustained by an organ. [14]

## A lung and kidney presentation can be an emergency

Coughing blood, increasing breathlessness, rapidly falling kidney function or markedly reduced urine output require urgent assessment. Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes, abbreviated KDIGO, recommends interpreting PR3/MPO results with the clinical presentation and considering a biopsy in suspected kidney vasculitis. Its 2024 guideline recognizes that waiting for biopsy performance or reporting should not delay treatment in a rapidly deteriorating patient with a compatible presentation and positive PR3 or MPO antibodies. Conversely, negative antibodies do not remove the need to investigate a convincing syndrome. Clinicians simultaneously consider secondary causes, including infection. This is a specialist balance between obtaining diagnostic evidence and preserving organ function, not a reason for readers to start or stop immune-suppressing medicines themselves. [15]

Anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies, shortened to anti-GBM, target a structural layer in kidney filters and can be associated with lung bleeding. This pathway is distinct from ANCA vasculitis, although both antibodies can occur together. KDIGO's anti-GBM chapter stresses prompt diagnosis in suspected rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, a pattern of severe kidney-filter inflammation. Blood antibody testing and kidney biopsy provide complementary evidence, and treatment may need to begin before every result returns when suspicion is high. Biopsy fluorescence can show a linear antibody-deposition pattern along the filter membrane. The laboratory must know that an urgent lung-kidney syndrome is being investigated. Routine scheduling of a broad outpatient autoimmune panel is not an adequate response to acute organ-threatening illness. [16]

Sporinova and colleagues reported a twenty-four-year-old man with coughing blood and kidney failure whose circulating anti-GBM test was negative. Kidney biopsy instead showed strong linear IgG staining with severe inflammation, supporting atypical anti-GBM disease. His lung symptoms initially improved with immune suppression, but substantial bleeding recurred a month later. Further immune-directed treatment did not resolve it; lung-fluid and blood investigations identified cytomegalovirus, abbreviated CMV, infection. Antiviral treatment was followed by resolution of bleeding and chest-imaging abnormalities, while kidney function had not recovered at reported follow-up and he remained dialysis-dependent. The report supports two separate cautions: a negative serum assay can miss important tissue-associated disease, and new deterioration during treatment can reflect infection rather than a need to intensify immune suppression indefinitely. [17]

## Antiphospholipid testing needs clinical events, persistence and the right assay

Antiphospholipid syndrome combines compatible clinical events with relevant antibodies; a single positive test is insufficient. The core laboratory groups are lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies and antibodies to beta-2-glycoprotein I. The misleading name lupus anticoagulant refers to prolongation of certain clotting tests in a tube; clinically, it can accompany a tendency to form clots and does not mean the person has lupus. The 2023 American College of Rheumatology and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology criteria were designed for research classification, using defined clinical and laboratory domains. Persistent positivity is assessed with results at least twelve weeks apart. An acute clot still needs immediate clinical care; the persistence interval is a requirement for interpretation, not an instruction to postpone assessment. [18]

The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis's 2025 guidance distinguishes clinical laboratory interpretation from research classification. Anticardiolipin and beta-2-glycoprotein I antibody assays measure binding, whereas lupus anticoagulant is investigated with functional coagulation tests. The numerical boundaries specified for ELISA-based research criteria cannot simply be copied onto every automated platform. Results depend on method, validated local cutoffs and the antibody class measured. The clinician should know whether positivity is isolated or involves more than one core group, and whether it persists. A laboratory result close to a method's boundary is not equivalent to a universally standardized amount of disease. Keeping the original report and assay information is more useful than recording only that an antiphospholipid panel was positive. [19]

Anticoagulant medicines can create an analytical mimic. Murer and colleagues reviewed fifty-nine people whose laboratory results met lupus-anticoagulant testing criteria; eighteen were taking rivaroxaban. In fifteen of those eighteen, confirmation-step behavior also resembled lupus anticoagulant. Rivaroxaban can prolong the screening reaction and show correction with additional phospholipid, imitating the antibody's laboratory pattern. This was a study of test interference, not proof that all eighteen patients lacked antiphospholipid antibodies or that the medicine caused autoimmune disease. The testing service needs the actual anticoagulant and collection context to judge validity or plan alternative testing. People should not interrupt prescribed anticoagulation to obtain a cleaner laboratory result without their treating team's direction, because that decision changes clotting risk. [20]

## Neurological antibody testing has important serum and spinal-fluid exceptions

New seizures, rapidly changing memory or behavior, or altered consciousness can indicate encephalitis, inflammation of the brain, and need urgent assessment. The October 2026 United Kingdom Joint Societies guideline for adults considers infectious and autoimmune causes in parallel. When autoimmune encephalitis is clinically suspected, it recommends early testing of both serum and cerebrospinal fluid, or CSF, the fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord. Clinicians select and safely obtain these specimens alongside other investigations; this is not a routine screen for every isolated psychiatric symptom. A negative antibody panel does not exclude every autoimmune encephalitis, while a positive result needs agreement with the syndrome and assay. Specialist review is particularly important when the clinical course and laboratory findings disagree. [21]

Gresa-Arribas and colleagues compared paired specimens from 250 people with anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis, commonly called anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis. Antibodies to this neuronal signaling receptor were detected in all 250 CSF samples but in only 214 serum samples using the study's combined methods. All one hundred paired control specimens were negative. These results establish a substantial specimen effect in that cohort, not perfect performance of every available commercial assay. Antibodies could also remain detectable after clinical recovery, so persistence alone did not establish continuing active brain inflammation. Other neuronal targets have different specimen-performance profiles. The important question is which antibody, in which specimen, by which method, in a person with which neurological syndrome. [22]

The opposite error can also harm patients. Flanagan and colleagues studied 107 adults whose previous autoimmune encephalitis diagnoses were replaced by alternative diagnoses at specialist centers. Seventy-seven did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria used in the study. Overinterpretation of positive serum antibodies contributed to fifty-three misdiagnoses, and seventeen of eighty-four people exposed to immunotherapy had documented treatment adverse reactions. Alternatives included functional neurological disorders, psychiatric illness, neurodegenerative disease and tumors. These were selected misdiagnosed referrals, not the misdiagnosis rate among all people investigated for encephalitis. The observed harms support careful clinical and specimen interpretation. They do not justify dismissing new neurological deterioration or assuming that someone with an initially negative antibody result cannot have a treatable inflammatory illness. [23]

## Choose the antibody pathway that matches the organ problem

Joint symptoms may require a different pathway from ANA, ANCA or neuronal testing. Rheumatoid factor recognizes other antibodies; anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide, or anti-CCP, tests for an antibody response associated with rheumatoid arthritis. ARUP Consult describes anti-CCP as more specific than rheumatoid factor, but neither result independently establishes the cause of swollen joints. Some people with rheumatoid arthritis have neither marker, called seronegative disease. The assessment still depends on evidence of inflammatory joint involvement, examination and, when needed, imaging and exclusion of alternatives such as infection or crystal arthritis. Repeatedly extending the antibody panel cannot substitute for establishing whether the joint itself is inflamed. A negative ANA is therefore not a general clearance certificate for inflammatory arthritis. [24]

## Repeat testing should answer a new clinical question

The Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health advises against repeating a positive ANA to monitor systemic autoimmune disease. Negative or borderline tests also should not be repeated for monitoring unless the clinical picture changes meaningfully. Its retesting intervals are safeguards, not instructions to reorder when an interval expires. Preserve specimen dates, assays, titers or units, patterns, medicines and infusions alongside the clinical question. Follow-up should identify what findings would alter the assessment and who will review them. The goal is a reliable account of the patient's course. [25]

# H026: Germline genetic sequencing

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Genetics
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *What can a genetic sequence explain, and why is finding a DNA change only the beginning of interpreting it?*

## Start with the question, rather than the size of the test

Germline genetic sequencing investigates DNA changes that may underlie an inherited condition or a condition arising early in development. DNA is the molecule carrying genetic instructions; sequencing determines the order of its chemical building blocks, conventionally written A, C, G and T. The laboratory compares a person's sequence with a reference and identifies differences, called variants. Most differences are ordinary human variation. The clinical task is to determine whether a particular difference has a credible relationship to the person's illness. Reading a variant accurately and explaining its medical meaning are separate achievements. [1]

A single-gene test examines one selected gene; a panel examines a defined group. Whole exome sequencing, abbreviated ES, focuses mainly on the protein-coding portions of genes. Genome sequencing, abbreviated GS, examines much more DNA, including regions outside exons. Wider sequencing can find an explanation that a narrow initial hypothesis missed, but it also generates more findings whose meaning is uncertain. A genome report is therefore not an exhaustive forecast of future health. The relevant comparison is between the clinical question and the regions and variant types that the particular laboratory actually analyzes. [2]

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, abbreviated ACMG, recommends considering ES or GS as a first- or second-tier investigation for children with congenital anomalies beginning before age one, or developmental delay or intellectual disability beginning before age eighteen. That recommendation concerns a defined clinical population; it is not a claim that every healthy person needs a genome. A detailed examination, previous results and family history help the laboratory recognize an explanation and help clinicians decide what a diagnosis would change. A useful result may guide surveillance, avoid an inappropriate investigation, clarify reproductive counseling or suggest treatment; a curative treatment is not guaranteed. [3]

## Consent includes choices about information

Consent should cover the purpose, likely benefits, limitations, possible uncertain results, effects on relatives and emotional implications. Families should understand who receives the report, how samples and data are retained, and whether research use is optional. A signature follows an explanation that the person can understand. For children, the responsible decision maker needs that explanation too. Consent to a diagnostic test should not be treated as unlimited permission for every future use of the sample. [26]

Secondary findings are deliberately sought results unrelated to the original diagnostic question. ACMG's policy supports an informed opportunity to decline their return. This discussion belongs before testing, including when the patient is a child, with attention to the child's interests. A risk discovered in the child can also matter to relatives, but each person's clinical evaluation and consent still matter. The team should explain which categories will be examined and how results will be discussed. Different laboratories and health systems may implement reporting choices differently; those choices should be documented rather than assumed. [20]

The current ACMG SF v3.3 list, published in 2025, specifies a minimum set of medically actionable gene-condition findings. It is not a comprehensive screen for every preventable disease or a substitute for testing prompted by symptoms. Reporting depends on the condition, inheritance pattern and qualifying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants; uncertain variants are not returned under this list. Actionable means that an intervention can potentially improve an outcome, not that illness is inevitable. A person with no reported secondary finding still needs ordinary medical care and assessment of a concerning family history. [21]

## Choose a specimen that can answer the question

A laboratory's specimen instructions are part of the test. Mayo's current hereditary-disorder genome assay accepts several postnatal specimen types, but warns that a previous stem-cell transplant from another person can interfere with testing. Saliva can yield less suitable DNA than blood, and insufficient quality can leave regions uninterpretable or require another sample. The assay is not its tumor-profiling or drug-response test, and it does not accept prenatal samples. Correctly labeled patient and family samples, clinical notes and previous genetic reports help prevent a technically successful analysis from answering the wrong question. [4]

Blood is not always a reliable stand-in for a person's constitutional DNA. In DeRoin and colleagues' study of 350 people, largely adults with hematologic disorders, skin fibroblasts were cultured to obtain another DNA source. Sixteen samples failed to grow, and the median culture time was 28 days. Some unexpected findings required assessment in another specimen. This is a practical clinical outcome: choosing a more informative tissue can add time and still needs quality review. Neither blood nor cultured skin should be assumed infallible, especially when the observed variant does not fit the clinical picture. [7]

Inheritance determines how a finding fits a family. In an autosomal dominant condition, one disease-associated copy may be sufficient; an autosomal recessive condition generally involves both copies of a gene. X-linked and mitochondrial inheritance follow different patterns. A newly arising, or de novo, variant can explain disease even when neither parent has the same finding in a tested sample. Conversely, carrying one recessive variant does not establish that it explains the person's symptoms. Testing biological relatives can help establish inheritance, but an absent family history alone does not exclude a genetic disorder. [27]

## From DNA fragments to a report

In a representative Illumina short-read workflow, extracted DNA is prepared as a library, a collection of fragments fitted with short adapter sequences. Indexes, or molecular barcodes, allow pooled samples to be sorted computationally. On the instrument, many copies of a fragment form a cluster that can produce a measurable signal. As DNA is copied, fluorescently labeled bases are incorporated in successive cycles; imaging records the signal and software assigns DNA letters. The temporary block and dye are removed before another cycle. Other sequencing technologies sense DNA differently, so this example explains one measurement principle rather than every clinical platform. [28]

Next-generation sequencing, abbreviated NGS, reads many DNA fragments and uses computer analysis to align them to a reference. Coverage has two dimensions: how much of the intended region is represented, and how many reads support each position. Near-identical DNA regions can make reads difficult to place correctly. A high average depth does not guarantee that every important exon was adequately examined. ACMG's technical standard calls for reporting methodology, coverage and limitations, including incompletely examined genes and variant classes. A negative report should be read alongside those limits. Deletions remove DNA and duplications add copies. Extra testing may be needed for these changes if the sequencing analysis does not comprehensively assess them. [5]

Quality is checked against more than the appearance of a plausible sequence. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's Genome in a Bottle program develops well-characterized reference genomes and benchmark regions for evaluating analysis pipelines. A laboratory can compare its calls with those reference results to identify errors and difficult regions. Such validation measures technical performance for the assessed material and variant classes; it cannot prove that a newly found variant causes a patient's illness. Reliable measurement and reliable interpretation require different evidence, and both can change when instruments, software or reference resources change. [6]

## The important blind spots are specific

A repeat expansion is an unusually long run of a repeated DNA motif. It presents different analytical problems from a single-letter substitution. ACMG's June 2026 statement recognizes that NGS can detect selected expansions when the method and software are appropriately validated. Short reads can struggle to span or size large repeats, while interruptions, mosaicism and DNA chemical marks called methylation may require further characterization. An apparent expansion can need confirmation by another method. It is therefore inaccurate to say that every genome test detects every repeat disorder, or that all modern genome tests necessarily miss them. [9]

Fragile X testing illustrates why the method name matters. The University of Washington's FMR1 assay uses polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, with capillary electrophoresis to examine the gene's CGG repeat region. Its guide explicitly excludes other pathogenic changes, such as single-letter variants, deletions and complex rearrangements. Conversely, a sequencing panel containing FMR1 should not automatically be treated as a validated expansion and methylation analysis. The report needs to specify which molecular question was answered; simply seeing the gene's name on an order is insufficient. [10]

Mitochondria, the cell's energy-producing structures, have their own small genome, alongside the much larger genome in the nucleus. Mitochondrial disease can involve either source. Heteroplasmy means that different mitochondrial DNA copies within a person are not all the same. The proportion can differ among tissues and change with age, so blood may not reveal an important finding present elsewhere. United Kingdom best-practice guidance describes blood, urine and selected tissue testing according to the suspected disorder. A negative blood result is not a universal exclusion, and additional sampling is a specialist decision rather than an automatic requirement for an invasive biopsy. [11]

Mosaicism means that cell populations within one person differ genetically after developing from the same fertilized egg. A variant confined to a small fraction of cells may be missed by standard-depth sequencing, particularly if the sampled tissue does not contain those cells. GeneReviews emphasizes matching both the specimen and the assay's sensitivity to the suspected mosaic disorder. A negative blood test does not exclude every tissue-limited variant. The percentage of reads carrying a variant is also not a direct measurement of the percentage of the entire body affected, or a simple prediction of severity. [8]

## Classification is an evidence judgment

The foundational ACMG and Association for Molecular Pathology framework uses five categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. Evidence can include population frequency, the gene's established disease relationship, segregation in relatives, functional studies and the match to the clinical presentation. A prediction that a protein might be damaged is not, by itself, proof of disease causation. A variant of uncertain significance, abbreviated VUS, should not determine clinical decisions as though a diagnosis were confirmed. Resolving uncertainty may require family studies, additional clinical investigations or new published evidence, while care continues according to the person's actual findings. [12]

ClinVar is a public archive of submitted classifications, not a machine that independently makes a diagnosis. Its review status distinguishes a single submission, agreement among submitters, an expert-panel review and a practice-guideline assessment. Conflicting classifications are explicitly displayed. The relevant condition and the evidence behind a classification matter alongside the star level. A patient should not have to decide which laboratory is right from a search result. The testing service can examine the disagreement, dates and supporting evidence, and explain whether the current report or follow-up plan needs revision. [13]

Even a convincingly pathogenic variant does not always predict exactly what will happen. Penetrance describes how often people carrying a particular variant show the associated condition. Variable expressivity describes differences in features or severity among those affected. These concepts explain why an apparently healthy relative may not settle whether a variant is relevant, and why two relatives with the same finding may need different clinical assessments. A predisposition to cancer is not a diagnosis of cancer. The practical question is which condition-specific surveillance or management follows from the finding, the person's age and their clinical history. [14]

Manrai and colleagues documented a consequential interpretation problem in cardiomyopathy testing. Seven patients of African or unspecified ancestry had received reports calling one of two variants pathogenic or presumed pathogenic; both variants were later considered benign. The original evidence had inadequately represented population diversity. The records did not establish whether affected families were recontacted. This was a failure of interpretation rather than proof that those patients had no heart disease. It shows why a rare-looking variant in an unrepresentative reference population can mislead, and why an amended classification must be connected to a review of the patient's and relatives' care. [15]

## Cases show benefit without promising a cure

Worthey and colleagues reported a boy whose severe bowel inflammation began at fifteen months. Exome analysis identified an XIAP variant; confirmation by Sanger sequencing, a second sequencing method in a clinical laboratory, and functional investigations supported XIAP deficiency. That diagnosis informed a stem-cell transplant from a donor. At more than 42 days after transplantation, the original report stated that he could eat and drink and had no recurrent gastrointestinal disease. This is a documented early outcome, not a lifelong cure claim. The reasoning joined the sequence, abnormal cellular function and clinical illness; it did not rely on selecting a striking variant from a long list. [16]

Project Baby Bear tested 184 critically ill infants across five California hospitals. Seventy-four received a genetic diagnosis explaining admission, and 58 actually had at least one change in care after review. Provisional results had a median turnaround of three days. The investigators separated diagnoses, uncertain variants and changes that were anticipated but never occurred. This was a quality-improvement program, not a randomized demonstration that sequencing saves every infant. Its cost estimates used modeled alternative care pathways, and most long-term outcome benefits were considered too speculative to quantify. Fast results are most valuable when they arrive before a specific care decision. [17]

## A nondiagnostic result can deserve another look

Wenger and colleagues reanalyzed 40 previously nondiagnostic exomes and established four additional diagnoses. Their original reports had been issued, on average, twenty months earlier. The newly recognized explanations involved de novo variants and gene-disease evidence that had been insufficient or difficult to find at the initial analysis. The endpoint was a diagnosis after reinterpretation, not a demonstrated improvement in symptoms. This small selected series does not set a universal ten-percent expectation. It illustrates that the same underlying sequence can acquire a different meaning as medical knowledge develops. [18]

Reevaluation of a particular variant and reanalysis of an entire dataset are related but different tasks. ACMG's reanalysis statement recommends clear laboratory policies, updated clinical information and attention to new evidence and methods. Sometimes the existing data can answer the question; sometimes another technology or new sequencing is needed because the original experiment did not adequately measure the relevant change. Families should know who can request review, how updated reports are communicated and how to maintain contact. Reanalysis should not be assumed to happen continuously or automatically, and an old negative report should not close a convincing clinical investigation forever. [19]

## Prenatal sequencing has a distinct setting

The September 2026 joint recommendations of the European Society of Human Genetics and International Society for Prenatal Diagnosis support a structured diagnostic pathway for selected fetal anomalies. They prefer trio testing, examining the fetus and both biological parents together, require attention to maternal contamination and stress expert counseling before and after testing. They do not recommend prenatal sequencing as routine screening when ultrasound shows no anomaly. Fetal appearances and disease knowledge are incomplete, and results may not arrive in time for a particular decision. The laboratory must state whether chromosome copy-number analysis is included or another test is needed. A postnatal review can become useful when the child's features are clearer. [22]

## A molecular autopsy asks a medical question

Forensic identity testing usually compares selected DNA markers, often short tandem repeats, to assess whether material could come from a person or relative. That is a different question from identifying a disease-associated variant. A germline medical sequence does not establish when or how biological material reached a location, and a forensic identity profile does not diagnose an inherited illness. The distinction remains necessary even when technologies overlap. The medical interpretation concerns health and inheritance; identity evidence requires its own validated comparison, statistical interpretation and case context. [25]

Bagnall and colleagues prospectively studied 490 sudden cardiac deaths among people aged one to thirty-five in Australia and New Zealand. In the unexplained subgroup that underwent genetic testing, clinically relevant cardiac-gene variants were identified in 31 of 113 cases. The investigation included autopsy, histology and toxicology before a death was considered unexplained. This provides a real molecular-autopsy outcome: additional possible inherited explanations after conventional investigation. It does not mean that 27 percent of every sudden death has a proven genetic cause, or that any rare cardiac-gene variant establishes the final mechanism of death. [23]

The Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society and Heart Rhythm Society consensus places these investigations within multidisciplinary care involving pathology, cardiology, genetics and counseling. Postmortem genetic findings need integration with the circumstances, autopsy and family clinical features; uncertain variants should not drive predictive testing throughout the family as though pathogenicity were established. When the underlying cardiac condition remains unidentified, it cautions against routine hypothesis-free ES or GS because of the risk of misinterpretation. Relatives may still need clinical assessment when no genetic explanation is found. Sudden collapse or concerning cardiac symptoms require clinical care without waiting for sequencing. A postmortem result can guide a family's investigation, but it does not independently establish blame, intent or preventability. [24]

# H027: Chromosome analysis

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Genetics
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *When chromosomes are counted, photographed or sampled with probes, which kinds of change does each method actually reveal?*

## One name covers several different measurements

Chromosomes are the packages that organize DNA, the genetic material, within cells. A karyotype examines their number, size and visible structure. Most nucleated human cells normally have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs, although biology and clinical testing include important exceptions. An extra chromosome is a trisomy; a missing one is a monosomy. Chromosome analysis can investigate developmental differences, a recognizable syndrome, infertility or pregnancy loss. The same broad laboratory discipline also studies acquired changes in cancer cells, but the specimen, culture and interpretation for that question differ. Testing for a chromosome condition present from birth and testing a tumor should not be treated as interchangeable. [1]

Structure matters as well as count. A deletion removes chromosome material, a duplication adds a copy, and a translocation moves material between chromosomes. A balanced translocation has no net gain or loss apparent at the assessed resolution; an unbalanced one does. A balanced carrier may be healthy yet have a reproductive risk because eggs or sperm can receive an unbalanced combination. Some rearrangements can also disturb genes. Neither the total number of chromosomes nor the word balanced, by itself, answers every question about an individual or future pregnancy. The precise rearrangement and family context determine counseling. [3]

## Karyotyping shows the arrangement in dividing cells

For a conventional blood karyotype, the laboratory grows selected white blood cells and examines chromosomes when they are condensed during division, at metaphase. Staining creates bands used to distinguish chromosomes and recognize larger changes. Mayo's congenital blood study generally analyzes twenty metaphase cells, with additional work according to the indication. It requires viable cells: unsuitable transport, temperature extremes or other specimen problems can prevent adequate culture. The request should identify the clinical concern and any known familial rearrangement. A failed culture means that an interpretable result was not obtained; it is not a normal chromosome result. [2]

Resolution describes the scale of change that a method can distinguish. NHS guidance gives a typical karyotype range of about five to ten megabases, meaning millions of DNA base pairs, compared with roughly fifty to two hundred kilobases, meaning thousands, for many arrays. These are practical ranges, not guaranteed cutoffs for every specimen or region. The physical arrangement may still be more informative on a karyotype, even when an array resolves smaller gains and losses. Higher resolution and broader kinds of detectable change are different advantages. [23]

Culture can change what is observed. An unusual cell may reflect a change arising during growth in the laboratory, called pseudomosaicism, rather than a cell population present in the patient. Conversely, one cell line may grow less well than another. European cytogenomic guidance therefore requires appropriate work-up of suspected mosaic findings and attention to tissue, independent cultures and the referral question. An isolated unexpected cell requires that assessment before a constitutional mosaic diagnosis. Cultured cell percentages do not necessarily represent the whole body. [17]

## FISH answers a question selected in advance

Fluorescence in situ hybridization, abbreviated FISH, uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes that bind to selected matching sequences. A microscope shows the probe signals within cells. Some probes address a particular chromosome region, some help count chromosomes, and others paint larger chromosome segments. FISH can examine cells between divisions as well as condensed chromosomes, depending on the test. This can make it useful for a focused, relatively rapid question or for evaluating a suspected mosaic finding. The signal identifies the region targeted by that probe; it is not a complete reading of every chromosome or gene. [4]

Mayo's 22q11.2 FISH assay illustrates that boundary. Its method uses a probe in the HIRA region and a control elsewhere on chromosome 22. A missing target signal supports deletion of the region tested. A normal pattern does not exclude every chromosome 22 change, abnormalities outside the probe's location, or a different genetic explanation for the symptoms. The request and report should name the target rather than simply say that FISH was normal. Broader copy-number testing may be appropriate when a small deletion or duplication is suspected but the correct region is not already established. [5]

## Microarray measures DNA dosage across many regions

Chromosomal microarray, abbreviated CMA, uses many probes distributed across the genome to assess gains and losses of DNA. It does not require a photograph of dividing chromosomes. Small deletions and duplications invisible on a routine karyotype may therefore become detectable. A single-nucleotide-polymorphism array, or SNP array, also examines patterns at variable DNA positions that can reveal long regions where the two copies are alike. A normal result on Mayo's constitutional array cannot rule out balanced rearrangements or all mosaicism. Its resolution depends on probe coverage and the assay's reporting rules, rather than one universal smallest detectable abnormality. [6]

The array's scanner measures fluorescence at probe locations, rather than spelling out the patient's complete DNA sequence. In a comparative array, differently labeled patient and reference DNA compete for binding; relative signals indicate a possible loss or gain. SNP arrays combine intensity with patterns of the alternative DNA letters at selected sites. A duplicated region can be detected without showing where the extra copy is located: beside the original, on another chromosome or elsewhere. Likewise, SNP patterns can suggest some forms of uniparental disomy but do not exclude every parent-of-origin abnormality. A normal dosage result is not a sequence analysis. [24]

The underlying signal needs quality checks. In comparative genomic hybridization, the patient's DNA is compared with reference DNA; SNP-based systems use additional patterns of alternative DNA letters and reference datasets. Software identifies changes across neighboring probes, and laboratory staff review whether the signal meets validated criteria. DNA quality, probe behavior and analysis settings affect what can be called reliably. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, abbreviated ACMG, reaffirmed its 2021 technical standard in 2025. It requires validation of the relevant abnormalities and mosaic levels. Modern SNP information can help detect some changes in ploidy, the number of whole chromosome sets, that a copy-number comparison alone may miss. A claimed capability must belong to the actual validated platform. [7]

## The best first test depends on the clinical setting

A major 2010 consensus evaluated the then-available evidence for children with unexplained developmental disabilities or congenital anomalies. It favored CMA over routine banded karyotyping for detecting relevant small gains and losses, while retaining karyotyping for recognizable chromosome syndromes, familial rearrangements and selected reproductive histories. This is historical evidence for choosing a method, not a promise of a fixed yield for every child today. A visibly normal karyotype cannot be read as proof that the smaller-scale genetic causes of developmental difficulties have been excluded. A targeted test can still be preferable when the clinical question is already specific. [9]

The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2025 report places genome sequencing, or exome sequencing with CMA, in its first-tier pathway for many children with global developmental delay or intellectual disability. This reflects complementary information rather than a contest in which one technology always wins. Sequencing can identify letter-level changes, while a validated chromosome dosage analysis addresses missing or extra regions. Whether a genome assay already provides the needed copy-number information should be clarified before ordering overlapping studies. Developmental regression or other important clinical findings still require prompt clinical assessment and appropriate investigations while genetic testing proceeds. [10]

## Prenatal screening and diagnostic testing are different

Cell-free DNA screening analyzes DNA fragments in maternal plasma; its pregnancy-related component comes predominantly from the placenta. The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine's 2025 guidance describes it as screening, including for common trisomies. A positive result needs counseling and appropriate diagnostic follow-up; it should not be equated with a complete fetal chromosome diagnosis. Chorionic villus sampling, or CVS, obtains placental tissue, whereas amniocentesis obtains fluid containing fetal cells. Placental and fetal findings can differ. The choice of follow-up depends on the suspected condition and clinical circumstances. Even a reassuring screen does not assess every chromosome change or replace evaluation of a fetal anomaly. [19]

Wapner and colleagues compared prenatal microarray with karyotyping in a prospective study. Of 4,406 participants with chorionic-villus or amniotic-fluid samples, 4,391 had material suitable for array analysis and 4,340 had successful array results. Among normal-karyotype samples with ultrasound anomalies, additional clinically relevant findings occurred in 45 of 755; the corresponding counts were 34 of 1,966 for maternal-age indications and 12 of 729 after positive Down-syndrome screening. These are subgroup diagnostic yields, not population rates or predictions of childhood severity. The study's primary comparison excluded 58 samples mosaic on karyotyping. Its array analysis did not detect balanced translocations or triploidy; its SNP information was masked, so that result should not be generalized to every modern SNP platform. [11]

## Mosaicism is also a sampling problem

A person can have two or more genetically distinct cell populations, called mosaicism. The number and type of cells examined determine what can be detected. The 2024 Turner-syndrome guideline recommends counting at least thirty metaphases for the initial chromosome analysis; this can detect approximately ten-percent mosaicism with 95-percent confidence under the relevant sampling assumptions. That is not a guarantee that a ten-percent cell line in another tissue will be found in blood. When the clinical features strongly suggest Turner syndrome despite a normal blood result, additional cells or another tissue may be appropriate. The specimen and uncertainty should remain visible in the interpretation. [16]

In a 2016 report, a girl with developmental delay had normal results in all fifty blood metaphases. Cheek-cell, or buccal, FISH found an additional chromosome 12 signal in 30 percent of at least 200 nuclei, supporting the authors' diagnosis of Pallister-Killian syndrome. Testing skin fibroblasts, connective-tissue cells, was not performed. By age four she had seizures resistant to treatment and loss of acquired abilities; growth-hormone treatment was not started. This individual case shows why a reassuring blood result, or a mosaic fraction in one sampled tissue, cannot establish a mild prognosis for the whole child. [15]

Sparks and colleagues interviewed 44 families affected by mosaic trisomy 16 or trisomy 16 confined to the placenta. One terminated pregnancy and one stillbirth were excluded from the 42-child obstetric and neonatal analyses: 30 of 42 births were preterm and 31 of 42 infants had birth weight below the tenth percentile. Yet the study also reported favorable later outcomes for many children, with most school-aged children in mainstream classes. Median age at interview was 5.8 years. This was a registry-recruited, parent-reported cross-sectional study, and original genetic records were obtained for only 45 percent of the cohort. It neither guarantees a favorable outcome nor supports assuming severe disability from a mosaic prenatal result alone. [14]

## Apparently balanced does not always mean biologically silent

Halgren and colleagues followed 41 liveborn people with prenatally detected de novo balanced rearrangements and no abnormal first-trimester screening or ultrasound indication. Registry follow-up averaged seventeen years. Adding clinical follow-up identified neurodevelopmental or neuropsychiatric disorders in 11 of 41. Arrays in 32 found no pathogenic imbalances, while sequencing identified gene disruption or other breakpoint complexity in some carriers. De novo means newly arising. The study concerns this selected category of rearrangements; it is not a 27-percent risk estimate for every inherited balanced translocation. Its long follow-up matters because some outcomes became apparent well after birth. A chromosome arrangement can be copy-number neutral while disrupting an important gene or its regulation. [13]

## Contamination can answer the wrong person’s question

Maternal cell contamination means that a prenatal specimen contains maternal cells or DNA as well as pregnancy-derived material. The Association for Molecular Pathology's laboratory guidance emphasizes checking sample identity and maternal contamination alongside prenatal analysis. Contamination need not be visibly bloody, and an apparently normal result may otherwise reflect the wrong mixture of cells. The maternal comparison specimen and the sensitivity of the contamination assay matter. Culture can reduce some contaminating blood cells but does not eliminate every source of contamination or growth bias. An inconclusive mixture may require another specimen or another analysis, rather than interpreting the result as a normal fetus. Collection is a clinical procedure with its own counseling and risks. [18]

## Some SNP patterns raise another inheritance question

A long region of homozygosity is a stretch where both chromosome copies carry the same pattern at examined positions. Many such regions across chromosomes can suggest shared parental ancestry; a pattern involving one chromosome can raise the possibility of uniparental disomy. ACMG's 2021 revision explains that these are inferences with confounding factors. The pattern does not independently prove a particular parental relationship, misconduct or a specific disease. Clinical context, the distribution of regions and appropriate follow-up determine what can be concluded. Such potentially sensitive findings should be anticipated in counseling, rather than presented as a definitive family or legal conclusion. [20]

Uniparental disomy means that both copies of a chromosome or segment came from one parent. It can matter because some genes are imprinted: their activity depends on whether the copy came from an egg or sperm. A person can therefore have the usual number of chromosome copies but an important parent-of-origin problem. Methylation testing assesses relevant chemical marks and may be needed in an appropriate diagnostic pathway. A chromosome count alone does not answer that question. Conversely, uniparental disomy does not always cause illness; the chromosome, genes and inheritance mechanism determine its significance. [21]

## Interpret the change separately from its implications

The ACMG and Clinical Genome Resource framework classifies constitutional copy-number variants as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign or benign. Classification considers gene content, dosage sensitivity, population and case evidence, inheritance and other relevant data. The framework separates the evidence-based classification from its meaning for this particular person. A large change is not automatically harmful, and a small change can be important. An inherited finding is not automatically harmless merely because a parent appears well; incomplete penetrance, meaning that some carriers remain unaffected, or differences in severity can complicate the comparison. An uncertain copy-number finding needs explanation and follow-up appropriate to the evidence, without being converted into a definitive diagnosis. [8]

## Postmortem testing contributes evidence, not a complete cause of death

Reddy and colleagues studied 532 stillbirths. Microarray produced results in 87.4 percent, compared with 70.5 percent for karyotyping, and detected abnormal chromosome counts or pathogenic copy-number findings in 8.3 versus 5.8 percent. Its ability to use nonviable tissue helped explain the difference. The authors nevertheless reported limitations, including missed low-level mosaicism, imperfect tissue comparisons and unavailable parental DNA. This is a documented postmortem diagnostic outcome, not proof that each detected variant independently caused death. Fetal examination, placental findings and other investigations remain part of determining what happened. A failed karyotype and a normal array also have different meanings and should remain separately recorded. [12]

## The report should leave a usable next step

Consent should explain the intended question, possible uncertain or unexpected findings, implications for relatives and how results will be communicated. Families need an opportunity to discuss the information before testing. A chromosome test may be technically straightforward while its result has complex emotional or reproductive implications. Those implications deserve counseling rather than an unexplained positive-or-negative label. [22]

A useful report identifies the specimen, method, result, limitations and appropriate follow-up. Chromosome coordinates need the reference-genome version. Next steps may include parental studies, another tissue, another method or clinical surveillance. A normal result means no reportable abnormality was found within the stated scope; it does not exclude every genetic explanation. [17]

# H028: Cervical cytology and human papillomavirus screening

- **Domain**: Human
- **Species Scope**: Human
- **Clinical Category**: Screening
- **Reference Level**: Deep Clinical Reference (Batch 8)
- **Source Verification Date**: 2026-11-08

> **Core Diagnostic Question**: *An HPV test looks for a viral warning signal; cervical cytology looks for altered cells. Prevention depends on understanding both results and completing the right follow-up.*

## Two tests with different jobs

The cervix is the lower part of the uterus, opening into the vagina. Cervical screening seeks changes before symptoms develop, especially precancer: abnormal cells that could eventually become invasive cancer. Cervical cytology, commonly called a Pap test, examines collected cells under a microscope. Human papillomavirus testing, abbreviated HPV testing, looks for infection with selected virus types associated with cervical cancer. Cotesting uses both approaches. A screening result estimates whether further investigation is needed; it does not by itself establish that cancer is present or absent everywhere in the reproductive tract. Benefit requires adequate sampling, reliable interpretation, completed follow-up and appropriate treatment. [1]

## Why persistence matters more than one positive result

HPV is a large family of viruses. Types described as high risk can contribute to cancer, while the types commonly causing genital warts are different. Most infections are controlled by the immune system without cancer developing. The important concern is infection with a cancer-associated type that persists over years and produces progressive changes in the cells it infects. A positive high-risk HPV result therefore means that a risk factor was detected, not that malignant cells were found. Conversely, a negative result means that the targeted viral material was not detected in that sample at that time. It greatly reduces risk within an appropriate screening programme, but cannot supply a lifetime guarantee. [2]

## What the laboratory does with cervical cells

With liquid-based cytology, a clinician-collected cervical sample goes into a preservative, and the laboratory prepares a thin layer of cells on glass for assessment. The observer examines cell shape, nuclear appearance and arrangement, looking for patterns of abnormal growth. Cells from the cervix are important: a vaginal sample validated for molecular HPV testing does not automatically provide an adequate cervical cytology preparation. Laboratories also check identification, the collection material, sample quality and the compatibility of the sample with the chosen test. England’s National Health Service laboratory guidance, updated in August 2026, uses HPV testing first and cytology to triage appropriate HPV-positive cervical samples. Other programmes can use a different sequence. [3]

## What a molecular HPV result measures

Molecular assays detect viral genetic material rather than antibodies in blood. For example, the United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, describes the cobas HPV assay as a laboratory test detecting DNA from fourteen high-risk HPV types. Its software can produce positive, negative or invalid results. The approved collection device, transport material and testing platform form a validated combination; exchanging a swab or fluid can change performance. In May 2024, the FDA expanded this assay’s indication to include specified self-collected vaginal specimens obtained in a healthcare setting when a cervical sample cannot be obtained. That particular authorization did not itself authorize every home collection kit or turn a vaginal sample into a Pap test. [4]

## Reading the language on the report

A Pap report reading negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy, abbreviated NILM, means that the examined cells show no such abnormality. It does not mean that an accompanying HPV test must also be negative. Atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance, or ASC-US, describes uncertain changes in the flat surface cells. Low-grade and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions, LSIL and HSIL, describe increasingly concerning cellular patterns, but are cytology categories rather than a cancer stage. Atypical glandular cells, AGC, concern the mucus-producing glandular cells and need their own assessment. The laboratory wording, HPV type if reported, age and previous results should be interpreted together; the word “abnormal” alone cannot determine the next step. [5]

## An unsatisfactory sample needs an answer

An unsatisfactory cytology result means there was insufficient usable material for a reliable cellular assessment. Too few cells, blood, inflammation, atrophy or preparation problems can contribute. This is a sampling limitation rather than a reassuring normal result. The American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, abbreviated ASCCP, updated its advice: with unsatisfactory cytology and absent, unknown or negative HPV results, repeat age-appropriate screening as soon as convenient and no later than four months. A negative HPV result obtained alongside the unsatisfactory cytology should not be treated as valid reassurance in that situation; both tests are repeated when applicable. Another unsatisfactory cytology result calls for colposcopy. Symptoms still require their own investigation while sampling is resolved. [6]

## Missing transformation-zone cells is a different issue

The transformation zone is the region where one cervical cell type meets and is replaced by another, and where many precancers arise. A report may state that endocervical or transformation-zone cells were absent even though the cytology is otherwise satisfactory. That is not the same finding as an unsatisfactory sample. Age, pregnancy and other biological factors influence which cells appear in a specimen. ASCCP notes that absence of these cells alone has not been shown to increase the subsequent risk of high-grade disease. Management of an otherwise negative cytology result depends on age and HPV findings. Automatically repeating every such specimen immediately can therefore add testing without following the actual risk-based recommendation. [7]

## Follow-up uses the whole history

ASCCP’s risk-based framework combines the current result with previous HPV tests, cytology, biopsy findings and treatment history. Two people with the same minor abnormality may appropriately receive different recommendations because one has a recent reassuring HPV test and the other has persistent infection or previous high-grade disease. Possible actions include scheduled repeat testing, colposcopy, or treatment after sufficiently concerning findings. A request to return for surveillance means that a defined interval is part of active care, not that the result was ignored. Conversely, a general screening interval should never replace the closer surveillance required after an abnormal result. Bring earlier reports when changing clinicians, because an undocumented history is different from a documented negative history. [8]

## Self-collection can change access, but the pathway matters

Home collection is now a real United States option within authorized pathways. The FDA granted the Teal Wand De Novo request on 9 May 2025 for home collection and transport of vaginal specimens for an approved HPV molecular assay. This is a specimen-collection device: the laboratory performs the HPV analysis. It does not inspect cervical cells at home, diagnose all causes of bleeding, or provide a general sexually transmitted infection screen. The approved device and laboratory pathway must be used as labelled, with an identified clinician responsible for ordering, results and follow-up. An internet product described simply as a “home Pap” should not be assumed equivalent to this authorized use. [9]

## A newer home pathway illustrates device-specific validation

The FDA cleared the Onclarity Self-Collection Kit on 2 April 2026. Its decision summary describes a prescription device for self-collection and transport of vaginal specimens from a home or private setting. Performance was validated with the specified BD Onclarity HPV assay, not with every molecular test. These authorizations are examples of available pathways, rather than an exhaustive catalogue of all current devices. Patients and clinicians need to check the particular assay, collection setting, age and other label restrictions when arranging screening. A usable home sample can reduce an access barrier, but an invalid sample still needs resolution and a positive result may require a subsequent clinician-collected cervical sample or examination. [10]

## The SELF-CERV trial: comparable detection is not perfect detection

In the 2025 SELF-CERV clinical validation study, 599 participants from sixteen United States sites contributed to the study analysis comparing a self-collected vaginal sample with clinician collection. Among 48 participants with tissue-confirmed cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade two or worse, CIN2+, self-collection detected HPV in 46; clinician collection also detected 46. Among 29 with grade three or worse, CIN3+, the corresponding detections were 28 and 29. Participants collected in a simulated home environment, and the study was not a trial of long-term cancer prevention. The manufacturer-supported study supports the particular validated collection method while preserving its limits: neither “equivalent” nor high sensitivity means every affected person is detected. [11]

## What happens after a self-collected positive result

The 2025 Enduring Consensus recommendations prefer clinician-collected cervical specimens but accept validated self-collected vaginal HPV samples for screening appropriate asymptomatic, average-risk people. After a negative self-collected HPV screen, they recommend repeat testing in three years. Follow-up of a positive result depends on genotype, meaning the viral type or group identified. HPV16 or HPV18 generally prompts colposcopy, while many other positive results need a clinician-collected cervical specimen for cytology or dual-stain triage before deciding on colposcopy. Some genotype-specific pathways differ. Evidence is more limited when using self-collection for surveillance after previous abnormalities, colposcopy or precancer treatment, so clinician collection is preferred in those settings. Collection is the beginning of the pathway, not its completion. [12]

## There is no single screening timetable for everyone

The American Cancer Society’s current average-risk guidance begins at age twenty-five and continues to at least sixty-five for people with a cervix. It prefers clinician-collected primary HPV testing every five years; an approved self-collected vaginal HPV test is repeated every three years. Cotesting every five years or Pap testing every three years remain alternatives when the preferred approach is unavailable. These intervals assume an appropriate negative result and no history requiring a different pathway. Stopping at sixty-five depends on documented previous normal testing and relevant history, not the birthday alone. Previous high-grade precancer, cancer, immune suppression, or uncertainty about earlier screening changes the discussion. After hysterectomy, whether the cervix remains and why surgery occurred also matter. [13]

## Why United States recommendations can look different

The Health Resources and Services Administration, HRSA, supports a separate Women’s Preventive Services Initiative framework. Its updated cervical guideline retains cytology every three years at ages twenty-one through twenty-nine and prefers primary high-risk HPV testing every five years at ages thirty through sixty-five, with specified alternatives and patient collection options. The updated guidance becomes effective for relevant insurance plan years beginning in 2027; that timing is distinct from when an FDA-authorized device exists. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists’ July 2026 statement addresses this updated framework with qualifications. Thus an age-twenty-one recommendation and the Cancer Society’s age-twenty-five recommendation can reflect different organizations. The clinician should identify which current guideline and personal-risk pathway are being followed. [14, 15]

## Programme rules also vary by country

England’s cervical screening programme offers screening to women and other people with a cervix aged twenty-five to sixty-four. Under the current programme, an HPV-negative routine screen generally leads to another invitation in five years. If high-risk HPV is detected in an appropriate clinician-collected sample, cytology helps determine referral or surveillance. Previous abnormal results can lead to earlier testing. These are programme-specific rules, not instructions to transplant an English interval into every healthcare system. Someone moving between countries should transfer the actual reports and next due date, including whether the preceding test was cytology, clinician-collected HPV or self-collected HPV. The programme invitation is useful, but it cannot replace assessment of new symptoms. [16]

## Higher-risk people need a tailored pathway

Human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, can impair control of HPV and changes cervical screening recommendations. The United States National Institutes of Health guidance, reviewed in March 2026, provides specific algorithms for people with HIV and recommends continuing screening through life, with decisions in older people considering life expectancy and cancer risk. It also notes that self-testing in this population is still being studied; general-population self-collection advice cannot simply be assumed to apply. Other forms of immune suppression likewise require an individual plan. This is why a laboratory result cannot be interpreted solely from an age chart. The relevant question is whether the person belongs to the population in which that test and follow-up interval were validated. [17]

## HPV FOCAL: finding precancer earlier changes later detection

The Canadian HPV FOCAL randomized trial assigned 19,009 women to primary HPV testing or cytology-based screening. Of these, 16,374 completed the study: 8,296 in the HPV arm and 8,078 in the cytology arm. At the forty-eight-month exit round, CIN3+ was detected at 2.3 per thousand in the HPV arm versus 5.5 per thousand in the cytology arm. HPV screening had detected more important lesions earlier, and both groups received HPV and cytology testing at the exit visit. The lower exit-round rate should not be relabelled as a difference in all lesions found cumulatively across the trial. These are precancer-detection outcomes within an organized follow-up system, rather than a guarantee that an individual negative screen prevents every cancer. [18]

## European trials followed the outcome that matters most

A pooled follow-up of four European randomized trials examined invasive cervical cancer rather than precancer alone. It included 176,464 women aged twenty to sixty-four, followed for a median of 6.5 years, with 107 invasive cancers identified. Across follow-up, the invasive-cancer rate in HPV-based screening was 0.60 times the rate with cytology-based screening, equivalent to a forty-percent relative reduction; the statistical uncertainty range was 0.40 to 0.89. The difference emerged more clearly after the first 2.5 years. This was evidence for a screening strategy coupled to investigation and management, not a comparison proving every HPV assay interchangeable. It also measured cancer incidence, not a separately demonstrated mortality reduction in this particular report. [19]

## Colposcopy and biopsy answer the next question

Colposcopy is a magnified examination of the cervix. A speculum holds the vaginal walls apart while the magnifying instrument, the colposcope, stays outside the body. It helps the clinician identify areas needing tissue sampling. A biopsy removes a small piece of tissue so a pathologist can assess cells in their structural setting and determine whether there is precancer, invasive cancer, or another explanation. This differs from examining dispersed cells in a Pap preparation. Some abnormalities extend into the cervical canal and require additional specialist assessment. A biopsy or excision can reveal disease missed by a limited surface sample. The planned procedure, pain-control options, possible bleeding, pregnancy considerations and expected result date should be explained before consent. [20]

## Dual stain is a triage tool, not a viral-load test

Some HPV-positive cervical samples can undergo p16/Ki67 dual staining. These stains look for two cellular proteins appearing together in the same cell: a pattern suggesting disrupted growth control. They do not measure how much virus is present or establish an infection date. The 2024 Enduring Consensus guidance allows the validated dual-stain assay to help triage selected HPV-positive results, including certain normal or low-grade cytology findings. Positive and negative dual-stain findings can therefore lead to different follow-up. However, HPV16, HPV18 and high-grade or concerning glandular cytology retain their specific management requirements. A negative dual stain should not be used to downgrade those findings, and results from an unvalidated substitute stain cannot automatically inherit the same recommendations. [21]

## Vaccination and screening protect at different points

HPV vaccination prevents new infections with the types covered by the vaccine; it does not remove an established HPV infection or treat a precancer. Protection is strongest when vaccination precedes exposure. An HPV-positive screen after vaccination is not, by itself, evidence that the vaccine was useless or improperly given. Vaccination history should be recorded so the clinician can review which preventive opportunities have already been completed. [22]

Vaccinated people still need cervical screening because vaccines do not cover every cancer-associated HPV type. The appropriate test and interval depend on age and health history. Vaccination and screening therefore complement each other rather than providing interchangeable forms of protection. [1]

## England’s vaccination outcome: strong protection, continuing follow-up

Falcaro and colleagues used English cancer-registration data with 13.7 million person-years of follow-up among women aged twenty to under thirty. Compared with a reference cohort not eligible for vaccination, the cohort offered vaccination at ages twelve to thirteen had an estimated eighty-seven-percent reduction in cervical-cancer rates and a ninety-seven-percent reduction in CIN3 rates. Across vaccine-eligible cohorts, the investigators estimated 448 fewer cervical cancers than expected by June 2019. This observational comparison concerns age at vaccine offer, not proof of each individual’s vaccination status or future immunity. It documents population benefit while leaving room for residual disease. Continuing the appropriate screening pathway remains part of prevention rather than evidence that vaccination failed. [23]

## One patient: persistent glandular concern despite limited biopsies

A 2017 report described a fifty-nine-year-old woman whose screening cytology showed atypical glandular cells favouring neoplasia. Repeated directed biopsies and a cervical cone specimen identified low-grade changes and an unusual glandular proliferation, rather than demonstrating the eventual malignancy. Because the glandular abnormality remained concerning, hysterectomy was performed; the larger specimen revealed gastric-type adenocarcinoma with lymphatic invasion. She subsequently received pelvic radiotherapy, and no clinical or imaging evidence of disease was reported through December 2016. This uncommon tumour type is HPV-independent, but the report does not establish a negative molecular HPV screen in this particular patient. Its lesson is the need to reconcile discordant cytology, tissue sampling and clinical findings, not that routine screening is generally unreliable. [24]

## When symptoms change the urgency

Bleeding after sex, between periods or after menopause, persistent unexplained watery or bloody discharge, and pelvic pain require clinical assessment even when screening is up to date. These symptoms have many non-cancer causes, but a negative screening test should not close the investigation. British Columbia’s current guidance explicitly separates symptomatic evaluation from routine screening: a vaginal HPV sample alone is inappropriate for symptomatic patients, and a suspicious cervical finding warrants urgent colposcopic referral without waiting for screening results. Urgency follows the symptoms and examination, rather than HPV positivity alone. Someone with new symptoms should arrange assessment instead of waiting for the next routine invitation or repeatedly ordering home samples in search of reassurance. [25]

## A positive HPV test cannot identify a culprit

The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention emphasizes that HPV is common, often shared between partners, and may become detectable many years after initial exposure through reactivation. A positive test therefore cannot reliably date acquisition, identify which partner transmitted it, or demonstrate infidelity. These limits also matter if a result is discussed in a legal or safeguarding context: a screening assay is not a reconstruction of events. Supportive, neutral explanation can reduce shame and make follow-up easier. Ask for the exact result, whether additional sampling is needed, the recommended timescale and who will track completion. A clear plan is more useful than treating a laboratory word as a verdict about cancer or personal conduct. [26]
